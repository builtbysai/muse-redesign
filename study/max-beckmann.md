# Max Beckmann and the German Expressionists' claim on Goya (1914-1950)

Deep study, 2026-10-05. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as a
next doorway by the 2026-10-05 Velazquez study ("the German
Expressionists' claim on Goya"), and carried in the Caprichos serial
study's doorways. The quality-first choice after the long print-lineage
run (Rembrandt, Segers, Goya, Redon, Daumier, the etching revival,
Whistler, the Black Paintings, Velazquez, the Caprichos as a sequence):
the lineage jumps a century forward, and the question is what the
twentieth century took from the old etchers. The answer turned out to be
specific and documented: not the plates, the series as a form. And the
compositional species is new to the lineage: the stuffed frame, where
the picture is not composed around a void but packed until the frame
itself is the pressure.

## Sources

Biography, technique, print facts, criticism, and the documented
Goya connections via a delegated research dossier (text-only pass;
this study's visual inspections are mine). Key facts, carried:

- Biography, end to end: born 12 February 1884 in Leipzig. Academic
  training in Weimar from 1900, Academie Colarossi Paris 1903, Berlin
  Secession from 1907 (board at 26, the youngest ever), Villa Romana
  Prize at 22. Early painting was late-Impressionist, theatrical,
  academic; in 1912 he publicly attacked the Blaue Reiter impulse toward
  abstraction. War service as a medical orderly in Belgium 1914-15 (the
  Ypres field hospitals), nervous breakdown, discharged end of 1915.
  The watershed: academically correct depiction replaced by distortion
  of figure and space, drastically foreshortened perspective. Frankfurt
  from 1915, painting resumed 1917. Master class at the Staedelschule
  from 1925. Dismissed as a "cultural Bolshevik" in 1933, more than 500
  works confiscated in 1937 (one account says 590), several shown in
  the Degenerate Art exhibition in Munich; left Germany for Amsterdam
  the day after Hitler's radio speech. Amsterdam 1937-47. St. Louis
  1947 (Washington University, filling the vacancy left by Philip
  Guston), Brooklyn Museum Art School 1949. Died 27 December 1950 in
  New York, heart attack on the way to see one of his paintings at the
  Met. Nine triptychs between Departure (begun 1932, completed 1933-35)
  and The Argonauts (1950, on the easel when he died).
- The documented Goya statement, the dossier's best find: in July 1919
  Beckmann wrote to his Munich publisher Reinhard Piper about a Hogarth
  engraving he owned, "To me this engraving seems beautiful. Things like
  this make me happy. Brueghel, Hogarth, and Goya, all three have that
  metaphysical objectivity which is also my goal." Quoted and
  interpreted by Wendy Weitman in "A Disturbing Reality: The Prints of
  Max Beckmann" (MoMA/Fort Worth, 1992). Weitman reads it as telling
  because it lands exactly as Beckmann conceived Hell (1919): he had
  "learned to realize the narrative potential of the portfolio format
  from some of the great printmakers of art history." The documented
  claim is about the PORTFOLIO AS A FORM: Goya as a model of
  printmaking as social and political commentary in a cumulative
  graphic cycle. No letter, diary, or interview was found in which
  Beckmann discusses studying specific Goya plates or distinguishes
  Caprichos from Desastres from Disparates. Treat any stronger claim
  as unverified.
- The broader Expressionist claim, in three forms: (a) the nightmarish
  imagery (Caprichos, Disparates as dream-logic precedent, ambient in
  German reception by the 1900s); (b) the print series as a cumulative
  form (Beckmann, Grosz, who called his own drawings "Capriccios" after
  the Caprichos); (c) the war etchings as model (Dix saw the 82 Desastres
  in Basel, studied etching with Wilhelm Herberholz to improve his
  technique, and built Der Krieg, 51 plates, 1924, as a conscious
  anti-war sequence; Kollwitz's Bauernkrieg belongs to this lineage by
  critical placement, not by any artist-stated invocation of Goya,
  which was not found). Grosz's chamber of horrors, per Sabine Rewald,
  "was inspired by the mutilated war victims, blind beggars and crippled
  amputees in Francisco Goya's Disasters of War," and Goya's blind
  donkey reappears in Grosz's The Eclipse of the Sun. Qualification,
  flagged by the dossier: Beckmann himself rejected the Expressionist
  label and attacked the Blaue Reiter in 1912, so grouping him with the
  Bruecke painters needs that caveat.
- Print technique: 373 prints total, all black and white, the major
  period 1914-1923. Began in lithography (tonal, Liebermann/Corinth
  manner), shifted to drypoint during the war for "the directness and
  angular strokes" and "the harsh, jarring effects he now preferred"
  (MoMA). Only 19 woodcuts exist. Printers: Paul Cassirer, J. B. Neumann
  (exclusive contract 1921), Reinhard Piper's Marees-Gesellschaft. He
  printed his own trial proofs on his own press.
- The cycles: Hell (Die Hoelle), 1919, ten image lithographs plus the
  front cover (self-portrait at a ticket window), edition 75, C.
  Naumann's Druckerei Frankfurt. An allegorical journey through postwar
  Berlin in the November Revolution: The Street and The Martyrdom
  depict the January 1919 murders of Liebknecht and Luxemburg, Hunger
  seats his own family around a meager table, The Family ends with his
  son Peter mistaking a grenade for a toy. Sold not one copy of the
  edition. Berliner Reise (Trip to Berlin), 1922, eleven lithographs
  including cover, edition 100, the "moral sequel" to Hell: less angry,
  less distorted, bourgeois and working-class Berlin alternating, each
  image in a rectangular border that compresses space into a
  voyeuristic window. Faces (Gesichter), drawn 1914-18, published
  1919: nineteen DRYPOINTS, not etchings (MoMA collection records and
  Hofmaier agree; the etchings label is a common error), Marees-
  Gesellschaft, edition 100 plus a 1966 posthumous edition. Subjects:
  lovers, family, theater, cafe, the asylum, the operating room.
  Annual Fair (Jahrmarkt), 1921 (published 1922): ten drypoints,
  edition 200, Beckmann as barker of "Circus Beckmann."
- Painting technique: the claustrophobic compressed space is the most
  documented formal trait. Fraenger on The Dream (1921): forms
  "crammed into a claustrophobically tight, angular space that seems
  barely able to contain them," and "the taming of nightmarish forces by
  means of a wide awake formal consciousness." On The Night, the critic
  quoted via Artforum: the composition is built from the figures
  themselves, "who, like the beams and struts of an old mine, support
  the rectangle of the canvas." On handling: small soft brushes and
  thin blended paint to 1925, then from 1926 big brushes, "pulling light
  colors over dark and dark over light" (Spieler), thin paint so the
  undercoats show, "a tough, carved look and an inner light." Note the
  dossier's honesty: the evidence supports layered darks worked
  against, not a literal dark-ground preparation as his standard
  technique; do not claim the dark ground as fact.
- Self-portraits: about 85 in total, "their number and intensity rivaled
  only by those of Rembrandt and Picasso" (Wikipedia). Self-Portrait in
  Tuxedo (1927): the National Gallery Berlin bought it in 1928;
  Harvard's gallery text notes the face half cast into deep shadow and
  a 1928 Berlin critic's "the effect is brutal."
- Conflicts carried: death 1950 not 1951 (one secondary source wrong);
  Faces are drypoints; Hell is ten image plates plus the cover;
  departure dates 1932-35; the Mayer citation for Expressionist Goya
  reception is snippet-sourced only (scholar unidentified); no
  documented Beckmann statement on a specific Goya series; Beckmann's
  father "a miller" per Weitman vs "middle-class family" per Wikipedia,
  unreconciled.

## The seven works

All visually inspected at source resolution via Wikimedia Commons
(public-domain files). The Night and Departure are install/gallery
photographs, not flat reproductions; stats are approximate for those.

1. The Night (Die Nacht), 1918-19, oil on canvas, 133 x 154 cm,
   Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen, Duesseldorf (10421x9000
   gallery photo). The frame is stuffed edge to edge: three intruders
   and a family interlocked in one knotted mass, bodies cropped by all
   four frame edges, no negative space anywhere. The composition reads
   as a knot around a diagonal table; the maze of outstretched limbs
   is the architecture. Heavy black contour lines around every form
   hold the masses apart. Color: grayed flesh and ochre, with rationed
   red punctuations (the lampshade, the cape at right) doing the work
   the bright values do in the etcher lineage. The bottom edge of the
   gallery photo shows the canvas edge; the signature is visible.
2. Departure (Abfahrt), 1932-35, triptych, oil on canvas, MoMA
   (3072x2304 install photo, three panels on a white wall). Each panel
   is its own stuffed room: the left panel a dark interior of bound
   and crowded figures, the center the boat with the king and queen,
   the right the staircase with the drummer. Each panel reads as a
   separate claustrophobic box joined by the triptych hinge; color is
   saturated but each panel holds one dominant temperature (left warm
   brown darks, center the blue sea, right ochre). The frames do the
   compositional work: three boxes, no shared space, the viewer
   assembles the narrative across the gaps.
3. Self-Portrait in Tuxedo, 1927, oil on canvas, Harvard Art Museums
   (412x600 file, the smallest of the seven). The calm form of the
   species: one vertical dark mass (the tuxedo, dark pixel fraction
   0.57, the highest measured) filling the frame, one pale vertical
   event (the shirtfront), the hand cut by the bottom frame edge. The
   face is half shadow; the mask effect the 1928 critic called brutal
   comes from the shadow division, not from distortion. One red-brown
   curtain strip at the left edge is the only side punctuation.
4. Der Nachhauseweg (The Way Home), Hell portfolio, 1919, transfer
   lithograph (1687x2383). Vertical format, everything jammed into a
   narrow upright: heads piled and cropped by all four edges, the lamp
   post slicing down the left third as a load-bearing vertical, the
   black coat mass bottom-right with a single white dot as the dark
   punctuation. Background figures dissolve into the street dark; the
   sheet stays light (mean 186.3), all darkness is drawn, never washed.
5. Irrenhaus (Madhouse), Faces portfolio, 1918, drypoint (3000x2568).
   Horizontal frieze of interlocked heads at wildly different scales,
   every figure touching or overlapping a neighbor, the woman at right
   reaching into the group, the bottom-left head cropped by the frame.
   No background, no floor, no horizon. The drypoint burr gives the
   heaviest blacks at the faces' cores; the reserve of the sheet is
   the light.
6. Das Theaterfoyer (The Theater Foyer), Berliner Reise, 1922,
   lithograph (3452x4250). The variant species: the crowd is held
   inside a large drawn oval, everyone facing a different direction,
   figures cropped at the container edge, and the sheet outside the
   oval left blank. The container line decides what belongs; the blank
   outside is load-bearing.
7. Kaethe Kollwitz, Losbruch (The Outbreak), Bauernkrieg cycle,
   1902-08, etching with aquatint (700x599). Carried as the counter-
   species: a crowd that surges as one diagonal wave, the leader with
   raised arms at left, the whole group moving in one direction, worked
   tonally across the whole sheet (mean 89.5, dark fraction 0.348) with
   a dark sky and a light ground. Against Beckmann's knotted stasis,
   Kollwitz's force vector is unanimous. Both crowded; opposite motion.

## Tonal arc

Measured from the reproductions, 3% margin crop, image-area stats:

- Self-Portrait in Tuxedo: mean 89.4, std 59.5, dark fraction 0.573
  (the darkest; the tuxedo as one dark mass).
- Departure (install photo, wall included): 104.9/63.2, 0.337.
- The Night (gallery photo): 123.7/66.3, 0.227.
- Losbruch (Kollwitz): 89.5/51.0, 0.348, the tonal etching.
- Der Nachhauseweg: 186.3/77.7, 0.123.
- Irrenhaus: 183.1/65.7, 0.086.
- Das Theaterfoyer: 184.6/63.3, 0.087.

The clean finding: Beckmann's prints are light sheets (means 183-186,
dark fractions under 0.13) working by line on reserve, the paper as
the light, exactly as the Goya/Rembrandt/Segers/Whistler studies found
the etcher lineage works. His paintings are mid-dark composed masses
(means 89-124). Kollwitz works the whole sheet tonally (89.5): the
opposite instrument, and it reads as weather, not as light. The value
key is free and chosen per composition, as the lineage has now found
in every study since Redon.

## Re-render families

Two PIL families, single pass, schematic. Scripts in the evidence
folder; thumbnails inspected.

Study A, the stuffed frame: A1 the packed knot with edge cropping
plus one dark punctuation mass plus two red accents is the species;
A2 the same count of figures spaced with margins reads as inventory;
A3 figures in an even 3x4 grid reads as catalog; A4 the packed knot
under a global vignette is the named filter failure. Findings: the
frame must be full or the picture dies; the figures must be cropped
by the edges, full figures inside the frame turn the pressure into a
lineup; the accent is rationed, one dark mass and one or two red
punctuations, never spread.

Study B, the reserved sheet: B1 clustered line groups on the blank
sheet with one heavy dark accent is the species; B2 the same lines
over a toned sheet dulls the reserve and reads as "finished"; B3 the
vignette is the named filter failure; B4 uniform all-over scribble
is wallpaper. Findings: the sheet is the light, the brightest value
must be untouched paper, darkness must be drawn incident not wash;
Kollwitz's tonal sheet works only because the tone is composed as
the sky mass, not as a wash.

## Quality notes

What makes Beckmann good, in compositional terms, and what would make
a derivative fail:

1. The stuffed frame is a composition, not a lack of one. The
   pressure comes from three decisions: figures cropped by the frame
   edges (never full figures floating inside), bodies interlocked so
   no negative space survives between them, and the knot organized
   around one diagonal (the table in The Night, the lamp post in Der
   Nachhauseweg). A derivative that packs figures but leaves them
   whole and separated is a crowd scene, not a knot.
2. The black contour is architecture. Every mass is bounded in heavy
   dark line; without it the interlocked bodies would merge into
   noise. The contour is what allows the packing: it separates what
   touching would fuse. A derivative with soft or broken contours
   dissolves the knot into mud.
3. The accent is rationed to the point of stinginess. In The Night
   the red is three or four punctuations in a grayed-ochre room; in
   the prints the single dark mass (the coat, the lamp, the hat)
   anchors a light sheet. More than one dark mass and the punctuation
   becomes pattern; more red and it becomes decoration.
4. The container variant (Theaterfoyer) shows the discipline from the
   other side: when the crowd is not edge-cropped, it gets a drawn
   boundary, and the blank sheet outside the boundary is part of the
   picture. The container line must be drawn, never implied.
5. The series-as-form is the documented Goya inheritance, and it is
   structural: Hell is paced as an allegorical journey (street,
   martyrdom, hunger, family), Berliner Reise as the moral sequel.
   The Caprichos serial study found the same thing in Goya: the
   composition lives in the pacing across plates. A derivative that
   treats the portfolio as a set of pictures misses the instrument.
6. Beckmann vs Kollwitz is the study's two-species finding: Beckmann's
   crowd is knotted and static, the force vectors tangled; Kollwitz's
   crowd surges in one unanimous diagonal. Both are composed crowds;
   the compositional decision is the force field, and the two choices
   are opposites. A derivative must choose: surge or knot, never both.
7. The failure mode the whole lineage repeats: Beckmann's darkness is
   never a filter. No vignette, no crushed blacks, no gradient
   atmosphere. The Night's darks are bounded masses and contours; the
   prints' darks are drawn incidents. Study A4 and B3 name the failure.

## Avoid list

- The vignette or crushed-black filter as his darkness (A4/B3 named).
- Atmospheric gradient backgrounds behind packed figures.
- Whole floating figures with margins as his crowd; the even grid
  lineup (A3); inventory spacing (A2).
- Soft or broken contours; painterly blur as his facture (his paint is
  thin and carved, not blended).
- More than one dark mass or more than two red punctuations.
- Horror signifiers (nooses, masks, monsters) without the value and
  contour discipline; the tortured-war-veteran story as branding.
- The triptych treated as three separate pictures; the narrative
  assembled across the hinges is the form.
- Claiming a documented Beckmann study of specific Goya plates; the
  documented claim is the portfolio as a narrative form, plus the July
  1919 Piper letter naming Goya among Brueghel and Hogarth.
- Calling Faces etchings; they are drypoints. Calling Hell ten works
  without noting the cover makes eleven.
