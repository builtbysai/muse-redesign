# Elena Asins: Study Notes

**Profile:** Elena Asins Rodriguez (Madrid, 1940, to Azpiroz, Navarra,
December 14, 2015). One of the first generation of artists using
computers in Spain, and one of the few representatives of the conceptual
line where the idea is the engine of the work. She worked in white paper
with black lines or black paper with white lines, through systematic
calculation, concrete poetry, drawings, sculpture, video and
installation. Gold Medal for Merit in Fine Arts 2006, National Prize for
Plastic Arts 2011, anthological show at the Reina Sofia that same year.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on six images, two local re-render
studies. Read in full: Joan Robledo-Palop, "La desaparicion de la
imagen: Conversacion con Elena Asins" (Forma vol. 04, Galeria Freijo
PDF, complete interview, the primary source for her own account);
Wikipedia biography; the Arquitectura Viva profile PDF; the Kutxa
Fundazioa "Estructura, espacio, tiempo" exhibition guide (2026);
the NoguerasBlanchard "Canons" 2023 exhibition brochure (Catalan and
Spanish versions, both read); the Reina Sofia "Fragments of Memory"
2011 dossier. Six images visually inspected at full resolution:
"Estructuras A" 1969 (arsmagazine.com, 1286x1946), center crop and
top-left crop of the same; "Sin titulo" 1978 (galeria Elvira Gonzalez,
1024x316), full strip plus mid crop; two Canons install views from the
NoguerasBlanchard 2023 show plus single-cell crops; one Canons 3x6
panel crop; one 7x7 Canon grid (Iberdrola collection). Two PIL
re-render studies built and visually inspected: the Estructuras fan
construction (two passes) and a Canon arc-glyph grid. NOT seen: the
Columbia-era continuous listing paper drawings, the Hamburg 1987-1990
computer-generated forms, the "Canon 22" 72-piece Zarautz work, any
plotter originals, her Hamburg-era video pieces.

## The instrument: no computer, then a computer, always a system

The honest chronology matters because the standard short biographies
fudge it. At the Centro de Calculo in Madrid (1969), Asins made NO
computer art. Her own account in the Robledo-Palop interview: she
attended the seminars for a year and a half, all of them, but "No me
hicieron ningun trabajo con ordenador, porque entonces el artista no
tenia acceso directo a la maquina." The work got done for Barbadillo
(mainly), Alexanco, and Sempere. Her only CCUM product was an article,
"Consideraciones generales sobre la obra de Mondrian" (Ordenadores en
el Arte, 1969, pp. 79-86). The seminars formed her thinking, she says,
but the machine time never came. She left for Germany; when she came
back, the center had closed.

Her actual computer work came later, in two waves. As a visiting
scholar at Columbia University (New York), the Department of Computer
Science gave her machine access and she produced drawings on
continuous listing paper with computer and printer, explicitly
conceptual in tendency. Then Hamburg, 1987-1990, where she
concentrated on computer-generated forms, later lab-amplified and
laminated. She called these "obra digital" and "obras de laboratorio":
not prints of drawings but works that only exist because of the
machine. Her line about this is the whole program: "Por todo esto
recurro al ordenador, porque me da una perfeccion que yo nunca voy a
alcanzar con las manos." And the complaint that completes it: "Lo que
me interesa es el hardware, programar, y lo que me fastidia ahora es
que todo esta tan tremendamente vulgarizado que ahora te dan todo
programado. Es decir, que quieres dibujar y no hay forma de
programar." She studied LISP and Pascal for the same reason: she
wanted the machine, not the app.

## The early vocabulary: fans on a spine

"Estructuras A" 1969 (signed "Elena Asins 69", white ink on black,
gold-ish in the photograph) predates any computer use and shows the
construction her later work never left. A central vertical spine of
about twenty nodes. A set of perimeter anchors: points along the top
edge, a few down each side, two apex points at the bottom corners.
Every anchor is joined by straight lines to every spine node. A
complete bipartite graph, drawn by hand.

The visual result: each anchor blooms into a fan; the fans overlap
along the spine into a woven moire; the crossing density builds a
diamond textile field in the middle; each anchor point reads as a
bright knot where its lines gather. The corners burst: the widest fans
throw from the corner anchors across the whole spine, and the empty
triangles between fans are as composed as the line fields.

Two local re-render passes confirmed the mechanism and taught two
things. Pass 1 (each spine node joined to every anchor, drawn node by
node) collapsed into a tube: too dense, the spine became a solid cord.
Pass 2 (each anchor joined to every spine node, drawn anchor by
anchor, bright dots at the anchors, generous spacing) reproduced the
species: distinct fans, open black between them, the woven middle.
Finding: the drawing ORDER is the piece. Anchor by anchor, the
composition grows as a sequence of fan gestures, and the compositional
act is choosing where the anchors sit. Placement alone decides the
moire. Her anchors are not perfectly mirrored; the corner bursts on
the left and right are different spans, and that irregularity is what
keeps the symmetry from going dead.

## The late vocabulary: one mark, a canon of mutations

The Canons series (1988-1996, digitally produced drawings, homage to
Bach) is her print voice in its purest form. The construction:
a single small mark, stated, then restated across a grid with
mutations, read left to right like a score. Obsessions named by the
exhibition text: order in the placement of notes in the bar, rhythms,
silences, repetition, mutation.

The marks inspected: one 3x6 panel of block-built glyphs, each cell
a small arrangement of filled rectangles and stepped wedges, like a
character from a digital readout, mutating cell to cell; one 3x6 panel
of thick parenthesis crescents rotated through angles and mirrors;
one 7x7 panel of solid black near-rectangles with a triangular bite
and a small spur, each cell slightly different. Every panel carries
generous paper margins and visible registration calm. The color is
paper white and one ink. Nothing else.

The re-render study (3x6 grid of a thick arc glyph, rotation 30
degrees per column, mirror every other row, on paper with faint cell
ticks) confirmed: the mutation parameter carries the whole piece, and
the grid needs the white space. The arcs in her work are shorter than
mine, parenthesis crescents with sharper tips, so they read as note
heads rather than donuts. Hers also have a hand feel at the edges
despite digital production: the Canons were produced digitally but
they are not vector-clean. That friction, digital logic with ink-like
edges, is the signature.

## The philosophy: structure is the color

"Trabajo en blanco con linea negra o en negro con linea blanca... lo
que me interesa es la esencialidad de la estructura, tener en la mano
la base de toda construccion posible. Naturalmente, esto no te lo dan
los colores, te lo da la estructura." Color for her is sensation;
structure is the base that maintains the organization of things. "En
los ochenta: uso todos los colores del mundo, todos los que estan en
el arco iris y se sintetizan en la luz." So the black and white is
not asceticism for its own sake; it is the claim that light contains
everything and the drawing is the structure under the light.

Two doctrines stand out for generative work. First, the serial ethics:
"I work in series, I never work a figure but an association of
figures." Canon 22 at Zarautz ran 72 pieces; her combinatorics ran to
120 variants, "almost infinite." And the hard line: "Si yo quito una
figura o la modifico, estoy mintiendo. Mi dignidad profesional logica
se perderia." If she removes a figure or changes it, she is lying.
The series is the work; selection after the fact is a falsification.
Second, the invisibility doctrine: she owns almost no work of her own,
because constant presence makes an image invisible ("la constancia
perpetua de una imagen produce su invisibilidad"). She is interested
in the process; once studied, the work is withdrawn, folded away like
the oriental images she admires.

Also of note: her connection to Max Bense is direct. She studied
semiotics with him at Stuttgart. So the Stuttgart information-aesthetics
line runs: Bense (theory) to Asins (practice), through the Cooperativa
and the CCUM seminar room, while Barbadillo was getting the plotter
time. The two Spanish computer-art paths start in the same room.

## What makes it sing

1. One mark stated and restated: the canon device gives a grid of
variants the reading order of a score. You read left to right and the
mutations become rhythm. A specimen plate does not have this; a canon
does.
2. Anchor placement as composition: in the fan works, everything
follows from where the anchors sit. The moire, the bright knots, the
corner bursts are all consequences. The decision list is short and
the consequences are long, which is the definition of a good system.
3. Digital logic, ink feel: the Canons are digitally produced and
hand-touched at the edges. The work holds both stations, machine
precision and material softness, without staging a war between them.
4. The no-removal rule: showing the full run, including the figures
she herself would delete, is a stronger gesture than curating the
beautiful ones. It is the opposite of the gallery edit and it reads
as dignity.

## Overdone, and the avoid list

- String art without the anchor logic: the fan look is easy to imitate
and means nothing without the bipartite discipline. If the anchors
are decoration, the piece is decoration.
- Canon grids as specimen plates: a grid of mutations that does not
read left to right as a score is just variants. The musical reading
order is the whole point; without it the grid is a catalog.
- The invisibility doctrine as theater: she withdrew the work after
studying it. A piece that claims process-only purity while being
sold as an object is posturing. The doctrine only works if it costs
something.
- "Pioneer of Spanish computer art" biographies that skip the fact
she got no machine time at CCUM. The seminars, not the computer,
formed her; Columbia and Hamburg are where the machine work
happened. Precision about this is respect.
