# Honore Daumier, the lithographer (1808-1879)

Deep study, 2026-10-05. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named
("Daumier the lithographer") among next doorways by the 2026-10-04
Odilon Redon study. The quality-first choice, and the other end of
the lithographic press from Redon's noirs: where Redon composed the
field around one suspended form, Daumier composed the crowd. The
surprise finding is that the caricature is not the achievement. The
arrangement is.

Key facts, carried:

- Honore-Victorin Daumier, 26 February 1808 Marseille, died February
  1879 Valmondois. Errand boy for a bailiff at twelve (the doorway
  into the legal world he later dissected), lessons from Alexandre
  Lenoir, the Academie Suisse, learned lithography from Charles
  Ramelet and apprenticed with Zephirin Belliard c. 1825. Drew
  directly on the limestone with greasy crayon for his whole career;
  no engraver's hand between him and the printed sheet.
- Gargantua (December 1831, Louis-Philippe as a bloated giant on a
  close-stool throne) bought him six months in Sainte-Pelagie (served
  August 1832 to January/February 1833, release date disputed) and a
  500-franc fine. The trial made him famous and left him poor.
- After the September Laws of 1835 killed direct political
  caricature, the social turn: Robert Macaire (Caricaturana, 100
  plates, August 1836 to November 1838; second series 1840-41),
  Les Saltimbanques (1839), L'Histoire ancienne (1841-43), Les Gens
  de justice (39 plates, 1845-1848). Le Charivari ran him at roughly
  eight lithographs a month for decades.
- Never used models, rarely made preliminary studies; the visual
  memory banked over decades of drawing the same Parisian types.
  The 36 clay busts of deputies (1832-35), modeled from the
  Chamber's viewing gallery, were three-dimensional studies for the
  Belly prints.
- Eyesight failing from cataracts c. 1865-66, retired from Le
  Charivari 1872, nearly blind by 1877. Twice refused the Legion of
  Honor. The Durand-Ruel painting retrospective 1878 was a critical
  success and a financial disaster.
- Catalogue raisonne: Loys Delteil, 11 volumes 1925-1930, about
  2,800 works, numbers to 3955 plus addenda. The 4,000 figure is
  the standard round count; treat every count as approximate.

## Six artworks, visually inspected at source resolution

1. **Rue Transnonain, le 15 avril 1834** (lithograph, plate 24 of
   L'Association Mensuelle, published August/September 1834). Met
   20.23, Rogers Fund 1920, DP835611. A room at night after the
   National Guard's massacre. One strong side light from upper
   right rakes across huge white bedclothes and the dead man's
   nightshirt. The body lies on a long diagonal across the
   plate, a crushed child under his torso, an old man's head
   entering from the right edge, a woman's body receding into
   the dark at left. What he withholds: no soldiers, no action,
   no perpetrators, no rendered gore. The dark doorway at left
   is the compositional void. Smarthistory's line is exact:
   no action or drama, only the stillness and silence of death.

2. **Le Ventre legislatif (The Legislative Belly), January 1834**
   (lithograph). Met 57.650.185, DP808878. A crowd of thirty-odd
   legislators in four receding tiers, seen from BELOW, the viewer
   seated beneath the ministers. The foreground is a huge empty
   curved desk surface (the Bancs des Ministres ledge), taking
   the bottom third. The deputies are a frieze of heads with
   varied spacing and attitudes: dozing, leering, whispering,
   blowing noses. The white waistcoats of the front row protrude
   as pale masses. The light is even and bright, nearly
   shadowless, the opposite of Transnonain's chiaroscuro. The
   satire is in pose and gesture, not in funny faces.

3. **Robert Macaire, avocat (Plate 44, Caricaturana, 1837)**.
   Met 53.693.35, DP878076. The con-man barrister in profile,
   center-left, right arm raised in a long theatrical diagonal.
   A tight cluster of spectator heads at left. Behind and above
   at right, the horizontal band of the judges, one awake, one
   asleep. The upper half of the sheet is blank paper doing the
   work of the room. The caption is the second half of the
   picture: the act "is obviously null, tainted with fraud and
   without legal character... Devil! Devil! It is true, I
   contradict myself... But this act is certainly legal, legal
   and perfectly valid... (He pleads five hours without stopping
   and loses his case)."

4. **Robert Macaire devant ses juges (Plate 30, Caricaturana,
   1836-38)**. Met 53.693.49, DP878062. The same stage grammar:
   Macaire at left, hat in hand, gesticulating; the judges in
   their horizontal band at right, one yawning; a guard in a
   tall shako at far left; the clerk's back at center-bottom;
   a huge empty middle. The punchline again in the caption:
   "After this brilliant improvisation Robert-Macaire is
   sentenced to the maximum penalty." Two plates, one stage.
   The series is the composition.

5. **Les Gens de justice, plate 37**. Wikimedia Commons
   (a Mourlot reprint, imprint "Imp. Mourlot Frs," tones
   degraded, flagged). Two figures only, nearly life-size in
   the frame: a judge in full black robe at left facing us with
   a sly smirk, and a second man with his back to us peering
   through the barred window of a prison door at right. The
   left third is blank paper. The robe is near-full black
   crayon; the joke is in the caption ("It appears decidedly
   that my fellow is a great scoundrel... so much the better...
   if I manage to get him acquitted, what honor for me!").
   An interaction diagram: two bodies, one door, one caption.

6. **The Third-Class Carriage, c. 1862-64** (oil on panel,
   noted as a painting). Met 29.100.129, DP-21027-001. The
   painted counter-species to the lithographs. A dark carriage
   interior; three foreground masses (nursing mother, old woman
   with a basket looking at us, sleeping boy); behind them a
   frieze of eight to ten heads filling the upper half, all
   facing left. Light enters from two windows at upper left.
   Warm browns, the basket and the baby's wrap the one warm
   accent, the old woman's dress a teal note. No caricature,
   only weariness. The sympathy is structural: the poor are
   packed together and given faces.

## Technique breakdown

- **The crowd as one body with many heads.** The Belly's 35
  deputies and the Carriage's 10 passengers share one device:
  a frieze of varied heads with composed spacing and one big
  counter-field (the empty desk, the dark carriage body). The
  School Arts close-reading names the Belly's devices: the
  curving bench backs, the slight diminishing scale, the
  standing foreground figure backing the front line so the eye
  has a point of entry. A monotonous row of fat men becomes a
  single organism. The politics ride the rhythm.
- **The diagonal hero against the blank room.** The Macaire
  plates and Transnonain's sprawled corpse share the stage:
  one figure on a strong diagonal, the room supplied by blank
  paper. The gesture is the picture.
- **The interaction diagram.** Gens de justice 37 reduces the
  courtroom to two bodies and a door. The caption does the
  rest. The series (100 Macaires, 39 Gens de justice) repeats
  the type and reinvents the gesture each time; the deadline
  rhythm became the style.
- **The withheld incident.** Transnonain draws the morning
  after, not the massacre. Philipon, who knew the difference,
  wrote it was "not a caricature, not an exaggeration, but a
  page of our modern history bespattered with blood." The
  restraint is the accusation.
- **Palette and handling.** Black crayon masses and the white
  of the paper; tusche wash for the atmospheric darks
  (Transnonain); blunt crayon for darks, sharp crayon for
  lights, needle scratches for accents on the Belly. Baudelaire's
  claim that his drawing is "naturally colorful" means the tonal
  masses do the work other artists assign to hue. A restricted
  palette is not a limitation to decorate around; it is the
  compositional engine.
- **Point of view as composition.** In the Belly we sit beneath
  the ministers. In the Carriage we sit among the poor. In the
  courtroom scenes the satire comes from inside knowledge (he
  worked in a bailiff's office at twelve); he knows their moves,
  he is not above them. The sympathy is structural, not
  sentimental.
- **The caption as second half.** The Macaire plates and
  justice 37 are incomplete without their captions, and the
  captions never explain the drawing; they complete the joke
  from the other side.

## Quality notes: what makes this work good

The caricature is decoration on structure. The 2026-10-05
re-render studies proved it schematically: a row of varied
heads over a blank void reads as a composed group (A1), while
big-nose grin faces spread evenly read as emoji (A4). The
exaggeration is, in Baudelaire's clause, "rooted in nature":
observation pushed until the inner life shows through the
outside. Vincent's verdict, quoted in the New Criterion: we
return "not primarily because of their satirical point but
because of their aesthetic excellence... not in their parodic
gaiety but rather in the high quality of their draughtsmanship."

What he withholds is as composed as what he draws. The
Transnonain silence, the Belly's empty desk, the Macaire
plates' blank upper halves, justice 37's blank left third:
Daumier always leaves room around the incident for the viewer
to stand in. A derivative fails the moment it fills that room.

What would make a derivative piece fail: copying the manner
(big noses, pear heads, funny bourgeois) without the
observation; exaggeration not rooted in a real gesture, which
reads as mere ugliness; filling the silence with setting;
explaining too much in the caption instead of completing the
joke from the other side; rendering the room instead of
leaving it blank; evening out the crowd's spacing into an
inventory lineup; centering the hero instead of putting him
on a diagonal.

## Avoid-list additions

- big noses, pear heads, and funny-bourgeois manner as
  Daumier-like; the manner is not the method
- the crowd as an evenly spaced inventory lineup
- filling the sheet edge to edge; heads as texture
- rendered rooms behind the figures; the room must be blank
  paper or it traps the figure
- the centered upright hero as emblem
- small figures in busy scenes; the gesture dissolves
- the incident instead of the aftermath (showing the massacre
  instead of the morning after)
- gore or horror as the subject
- captions that explain the drawing instead of completing it
- the late-even-light look as his species; the value key is
  free, but each picture commits to one key
