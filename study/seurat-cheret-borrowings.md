# Seurat and the Chéret borrowings: the system digests the poster (1888-1891)

Deep study, 2026-10-07. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as a
next doorway by the 2026-10-07 Jules Chéret study ("Seurat's documented
Cheret borrowings (the Herbert-identified posters: 'L'amant des
danseuses' 1888, 'Spectacle Promenade de l'Horloge' c.1880, 'L'Horloge:
Les freres Leopold' 1877)"), the quality-first choice: after the
Chéret study established the street poster's devices, the question
moves to what the most systematic composer of the 1880s did with them,
and the answer turns out to be a working method for borrowing that
generative artists can steal outright.

The study has two halves. A delegated research dossier (text-only, zero

## The finding, in brief

In June 1958, Robert L. Herbert published a short note, "Seurat and
Jules Chéret," in The Art Bulletin (vol. 40, no. 2, pp. 156-158),
identifying three Chéret posters Seurat borrowed from. All three
borrowings land in a single late painting: Le Cirque (The Circus),
1890-91, oil on canvas, Musée d'Orsay, Paris (bequest of John Quinn,
1924, inventory RF 2511). Seurat sent the unfinished canvas to the
seventh Salon des Indépendants, which opened March 20, 1891; he died
nine days later, on March 29, during the run. The finding was restated
with museum authority in the 1991 Grand Palais / Met catalogue
(Anne Distel's Le Cirque essay, note 18). That Seurat studied the
posters, not merely owned them, is documented twice: Distel and
Tinterow both record that "Seurat owned posters by Chéret," and
Emile Verhaeren wrote in 1891 that Seurat had studied Chéret's posters,
wanting to "demonstrate their expressive means and prise out their
aesthetic secrets." Jules Antoine (1890) and Verhaeren (1891) both
noted the Chéret influence in print during Seurat's lifetime.

## Poster 1: L'amant des danseuses, 1888

Chéret's poster advertises Félicien Champsaur's novel L'Amant des
danseuses, lettered on the sheet as a "roman moderniste." The V&A holds
an example (E.106-1921), 124 x 92 cm; the BnF holds one as well. The
sheet was reprinted in 1895 as plate 45 of Les Maîtres de l'Affiche at
the Imprimerie Chaix; the 1888 Chaix imprint on the original is
probable, not confirmed.

The social geometry of the poster is the finding: one still gentleman,
the "lover" of the title, standing as the still point at the center of
a whirl of dancing women, one twirling in a yellow dress in the
foreground, onlookers faintly sketched behind. The borrowing lands in
two places. First, the equestrienne of Le Cirque: Herbert connects
Chéret's poster to "the horseback rider," the bareback rider standing
on the galloping horse at the painting's center; the dancer, the
poster's figure of pure upward motion, became the circus rider.
Second, L'homme à femmes (1890): Seurat's ink cover drawing for Victor
Jozé's satirical novel, with Chéret's poster "among the prototypes"
(1991 catalogue). Seurat first painted a small oil panel of the same
composition (25 x 16 cm, Barnes Foundation, BF1149), but the publisher
preferred a drawing because the panel's nuanced color would not
reproduce well. Jozé's novel is a roman à clef whose painter character
"Georges Legrand," at work on a picture of the chahut, is Seurat
himself (1991 catalogue, citing Richard Thomson).

The compositional point is clean: Seurat split the poster into its two
poles and spent each in a different work. The "amant," the still male
center ringed by moving women, became the Jozé cover. The dancer, pure
upward line, became the Cirque equestrienne. Both works date to 1890.
The poster handed him a ready-made social geometry, one composed
figure ringed by moving ones, and that geometry is the geometry of
both late paintings.

My visual inspection, at 11861 x 15988 from the BnF scan: Chéret's
dancer up close is loose work. The face is a smudge, two dark slashes
for eyes, the flesh hatched with visible crayon grain, the yellow
dress drawn with quick unworried lines. Seurat's equestrienne keeps the
pose's skeleton (raised arm, arched back, the yellow costume) and
regularizes everything else: the arms become a symmetric V, the head a
profile, the smudge a mask. The borrowing is visible only at the level
of the skeleton; the flesh is all system.

## Poster 2: Spectacle Promenade de l'Horloge, about 1880

A poster for the Spectacle-Promenade de l'Horloge, a café-concert on
the Champs-Élysées. Herbert dates it about 1880; Bridgeman Images
dates its example about 1875; carry both. Ernest Maindron reproduced
the sheet in Les Affiches illustrées (1886). The borrowing lands on
the large acrobatic clown in the right foreground of Le Cirque, the
white-faced figure whose raised arms and wand anchor the painting's
right side, a mirror image of Chéret's clown (the WebMuseum's Seurat
page, following Herbert: "identical, only reversed"). Distel dwells on
this clown's hands as the picture's symbolic center: the left hand
holds a wand like an academic model from the École, the right is a
ruddy, homely, real hand. Seurat took the poster's gymnast and rebuilt
him as a hinge between academic art and lived reality.

Honest gap: no open high-res scan of this poster was located, and no
published prose description of its imagery was found. Its imagery is
known only through what the borrowing implies. Printer and imprint:
not found.

## Poster 3: L'Horloge: Les frères Léopold, 1877

A Chéret poster for an act called Les frères Léopold at the L'Horloge
venue (1991 catalogue; corroborated by a Poster Auctions International
sale record). Do not confuse it with the distinct sheet L'Horloge: Les
Girard, for a different act at the same venue. The borrowing lands on
"the clown in the first study," the earliest preparatory study for Le
Cirque (presumed to be cat. no. 225 in the 1991 catalogue, unverified).
The motif did not survive into the final canvas in the same form:
Seurat tried Chéret's clowns in the study, then replaced the figure
with the reversed Spectacle-Promenade clown. The poster was
scaffolding, discarded once the composition could stand on its own.

Honest gap: no open scan located, no published visual description.
The 1991 note speaks of "those" clowns in the poster, plural, so the
sheet shows more than one clown figure. Beyond that: gap.

## The signature move: reversal

The borrowings sit inside a larger documented pattern. Meyer Schapiro
showed that the overall design of Le Cirque comes from an anonymous
poster for the Nouveau Cirque (1888), with Seurat reversing horse and
bareback rider (1991 catalogue note 18 cites Schapiro 1958). The
finished painting is a composite of at least two posters: Chéret for
the figures, an anonymous hand for the scaffolding.

Reversal is doing compositional work here, not hiding the theft. A
motif flipped is a motif re-aimed along Seurat's own diagonals. The
WebMuseum's gloss is worth keeping: the anonymous poster's drawing is
heavy and clumsy, the horse sluggish, the rider ungraceful, "while in
Seurat everything gallops." He took the motif and re-energized it
inside his own system.

Distel's larger claim matters more than any single motif: "beyond a
borrowing of motifs, Seurat derived from Chéret a fresh sense of line
and color that accorded with his new formal concerns." Verhaeren saw
it in 1891: Chéret was Seurat's "rapid initiator" because the poster
artist was "equally preoccupied by the mysteries of curved and
straight lines." Seurat treated a Chéret lithograph the way he treated
a Chevreul diagram: as data about how lines and colors behave, to be
disassembled, reversed, and rebuilt inside his own system.

One correction the dossier caught: a dealer's page claims
Lautrec's Divan Japonais poster was "a preliminary study" for Le
Chahut. Chronologically impossible: Le Chahut is 1889-90, Seurat died
March 1891, and Lautrec's poster dates to 1892-93. False.

## The receiving end: what the borrowings landed inside

THE CONTÉ DRAWINGS: TONE WITHOUT LINE. From about 1881 Seurat drew
almost exclusively in conté crayon on Michallet paper, a laid Ingres
paper whose tufted grain is the drawing's middle value; he never had
to invent it. Darkness tracks pressure directly. There is no contour
line in the traditional sense; forms are extracted from tonal masses.
My full-res crop of the Aman-Jean (Met 61.101.16, c. 1882-83) shows
it: mass built on the tooth of the paper, the white doing the luminous
work. The drawings are where he settled the key of a picture
independently of color. Signac later said they were so studied in
contrast and gradation that one could paint from them without seeing
the model. Every Seurat composition is first a tonal map.

THE CROQUETONS: THE MIDDLE STEP. About seventy small oil studies on
wood panels, painted outdoors directly from the motif. The role sits
between drawing and final canvas: the drawings fixed the tonal design,
the croquetons fixed the observed facts. In the studio he synthesized
the two into a painted study on canvas before committing to the
full-scale composition. My inspection of View of the Seine (Met
59.16.5, 1882-83) shows the loose pre-system brushwork, short strokes,
the panel a small honest record. Nothing in a Seurat masterwork is
improvised at scale: tonality was solved on paper, observation on
panels, the large canvas only combined what was already decided.

LA GRANDE JATTE, 1884-86 (Art Institute of Chicago, 1926.224, 207.5 x
308.1 cm). The recession runs in clear bands: a shaded foreground
strip across the bottom that holds the figures as cut-out silhouettes
and anchors the whole plane so the eye measures distance from a firm
near field. Figures are cut by the frame at left and right, including
the man with the cigar at the far right edge: the photographic cut,
the only casual thing in a picture of absolute control, making the
scene a window on a continuing world. The figure placement follows
fixed stations around the Panathenaic frieze model (Seurat to Gustave
Kahn): modern people in their essential traits, moving like figures on
a frieze, organized by harmonies. No one talks, no one meets anyone's
eye. The stillness was manufactured: Herbert's finding is that Seurat
developed the composition "as though he were a theater director
moving figures about on a stage," and the studies show a livelier,
more bustling island that he worked hard to drain of bustle (the
sinuous skirt curves, the scalloped outlines, and the cigar were added
in the 1885-86 reworking). The painted dotted border, added 1888-89
per the Art Institute (the 1899 date on one non-scholarly page is an
error), does compositional work: it is a visual transition between
picture and frame, belonging to neither, it announces that the
picture's unit of construction is the dot, and it keeps the eye inside
the composition instead of leaking out at the edges.

PARADE DE CIRQUE, 1887-88 (Met 61.101.17, 99.7 x 149.9 cm). His first
nocturnal painting. Nine gas jets illuminate the platform; the five
yellow-white spheres through the ticket windows are interior lamp
globes. In 1990 the Met laboratory examined the picture under light
matched to gas-lamp color, and the transformation was, in Herbert's
word, extraordinary: the orange platform faces became flesh, the
ultramarine shadows went black, the whole painting glowed as if lit
from behind. The painting was composed for a light that no longer
exists in the gallery; judged under white light, the color logic
misfires. The composition is frontal and symmetrical, depth collapsed
into layered planes that are almost coplanar, the crowd at the bottom
reduced to silhouetted heads, a frieze of dark shapes behaving exactly
like a poster's bottom band of figures. Chéret's posters taught Paris
that a flat field, a strong vertical, and a repeated silhouette could
hold a wall; Seurat applies that lesson with a scientist's light. One
qualification carried: Tinterow himself writes that Parade has
"neither the grandeur of the former nor the poster-like memorability
of the latter" (the latter being Le Chahut and the Cirque). The poster
reading is strong, but the scholarly catalogue ranks it below the
later paintings.

LE CHAHUT, 1889-90 (Kröller-Müller KM 107.757, 170 x 141 cm). The
demonstration piece for Seurat's line theory. In his letter to Maurice
Beaubourg of August 28, 1890, he set out the system: gaiety of tone
from the dominance of light, of color from the dominance of warmth, of
line from lines above the horizontal. Le Chahut is built to exemplify
gaiety: the dancers' legs, the conductor's hand, the men's moustaches,
the upturned corners of mouths and eyes, the neck of the double bass,
even the background flowers, all ascending (the Kröller-Müller's own
reading). This is divisionism extended from color to line: the line no
longer draws the figure, it scores the emotion independently of
representation. The double-bass neck rises at the left like a visual
exclamation mark; the dancer's leg is not anatomy but an ascending
vector. My full-res crop shows the dots as separate marks and the bass
neck cutting its clean diagonal. The Chéret connection here is
compositional rather than figure-for-figure: Tinterow writes that
Chéret's Folies-Bergère poster's "decorative flatness and flamelike
motifs predict some of Seurat's rhythms," and the WebMuseum's Chahut
page says the posters' "compositional form is felt in Le Chahut."

DIVISIONISM AS A COMPOSITIONAL DISCIPLINE. Seurat planned light and
dark before color, and value outranked hue to the end (the Knobloch
letter, single source via the 1991 catalogue). Chevreul's law of
simultaneous contrast gave the working rule: a color gains intensity
beside its complement, so contrast does the modeling that chiaroscuro
used to do, with no tonal mud. Rood's Modern Chromatics gave the
optics: small dots blend in the viewer's eye. The Grande Jatte's grass
reads as green from a distance but is built from separate spots of
orange, blue, and green. The regular touch flattens hierarchy: a face,
a tree, a shadow all receive the same small mark, so nothing can be
more important by handling, and importance has to be carried by
placement, scale, and value. The refusal of anecdote is enforced by
the brushwork. One caution from Herbert, carried explicitly: the
scientific aspect of Seurat's method has been "both misunderstood and
overestimated." His references to scientific texts served more as
justification for what he had already done, to lend it authority,
"rather than as a program that he actually followed." Present
divisionism as a compositional discipline Seurat genuinely practiced,
value first, complement pairing, the regular touch. The science is
partly a rhetoric he wrapped around it. Both things are true.

## The re-render families

Family A, THE BORROWING TRANSFORMED: A1, the borrowed pose reversed
and regularized, rekeyed into a value-dot field with bands and a
dotted frame, is the species. A2, the verbatim asymmetric quote pasted
into the same field, is the failure: it keeps the poster's energy but
reads as collage, the asymmetry fighting the system's symmetry. A3,
the regularized pose rendered as modeled gradients, is the named
failure: the technique discipline is what makes the transformation
belong. A4, the figure floating on white with no frame and no bands,
is the unframed failure. The schematic finding is that the
transformation (reversal plus regularization plus rekeying) is the
authorship; the source gives the pose skeleton and the system gives
everything else.

Family B, THE SYSTEM COMPOSES: B1, recession bands with a cropped
frieze of figures, value dots, and a dotted frame, is the species.
B2, the same scene in vanishing-point perspective, is the illustration
failure: a view, not a frieze. B3, smooth gradients with no dot
discipline, is the named failure. B4, figures evenly spaced with none
cropped, is the inventory failure. The finding is that the crop is
load-bearing: figures cut by the frame edges make the frieze read as a
slice of a continuing world, and without the crop it reads as a
catalogue.

Honest schematic caveat: stick figures and dot fields, crude,
single-pass. They test compositional claims, not likeness.

## The quality thesis, carried

1. THE BORROWING IS A TRANSFORMATION, NOT A QUOTATION. Reversal plus
regularization plus rekeying into the value system. The source gives
the pose skeleton; the system gives everything else. A derivative
that copies the motif without the transformation has quoted; one that
transforms without a real source has nothing to digest.

2. SEPARATE THE PROBLEMS. Tonality on paper, observation on panels,
synthesis at scale. Never improvise at scale. For generative work this
is a pipeline discipline: solve the value structure first, in black
and white, before any color exists.

3. THE FRAME IS PART OF THE SYSTEM. The dotted border rims the method,
keeps the eye inside, and flattens the depth claim. A piece's edge
treatment is a compositional decision, not an afterthought.

4. THE RINGED COMPOSITION. One still figure encircled by moving ones.
The stillness gives the motion something to push against. A ring of
dancers with no center is bustle; add the still gentleman and the whole
field organizes.

5. THE LINE SCORES THE EMOTION. In Le Chahut the ascending vector IS
the content. The line theory is the one Seurat device with no
equivalent in the print lineage so far: line as a carrier of feeling,
independent of representation.

## Avoid-list additions

- The verbatim poster quote: the borrowed figure pasted in without
reversal, regularization, or rekeying (Family A, A2).
- Vanishing-point perspective as the default depth system (Family B, B2).
- Gradient or modeled rendering where the discipline calls for
value-first discrete marks (Families A and B, A3/B3).
- The even uncropped inventory: figures evenly spaced, none cut by the
frame (Family B, B4).
- The border as decoration rather than a continuation of the working
method.
- "Seurat the scientist" as program: the science is partly
retrospective rhetoric; the practiced discipline (value first,
complement pairing, regular touch) is the real thing.
- The dealer claim that Lautrec's Divan Japonais was a preliminary
study for Le Chahut: chronologically impossible.
- The 1899 border date for La Grande Jatte: eight years after his
death; the Art Institute's 1888-89 stands.
