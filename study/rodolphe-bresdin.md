# Rodolphe Bresdin, the clearing in the tangle (pen on stone, 1854-1883)

Deep study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as
the first next doorway by the 2026-10-06 Felicien Rops study. The
quality-first choice: Bresdin is the French symbolist printmaker at
the node the lineage studies kept brushing past. Kubin's 1898-99
Munich print-room encounter named Rops among the masters on the
wall; Rops's own practice led to the French etching revival around
him; and at the center of that revival's pen line stands Bresdin,
the self-taught pen-on-stone lithographer whose whole sheet is
worked and whose subject arrives as a clearing in the tangle. The
compositional species this study found is one the lineage had not
seen: the figure as a density event inside an all-over micro-mark
field.

## Sources

Biography, technique, publishing facts, works, the Redon link,
criticism, conflicts, and gaps via a delegated research dossier
(text-only pass; all nine visual inspections, the tonal measurement,
the column profiles, and both re-render families are mine). Key
facts, carried:

- Biography: born 12 August 1822 (some sources say 13 August),
  Montrelais or Le Fresne-sur-Loire, neighboring villages in the
  Loire country. Died 11 January 1885 in a garret in Sevres, buried
  in the common grave. (MoMA's 1961 "1825" birth date is an outlier
  error that persists in derivative bios.) The poverty is real in
  broad outline, street sweeper in 1878, common grave, but much of
  the famous detail is literary: Champfleury's 1847 nouvelle
  "Chien-Caillou" made him a character before he was a known
  artist, and the shoe-polish inking story survives in the 1931 Art
  Institute of Chicago catalogue only as hearsay ("it is said").
  Paris bohemia in the 1840s; after the 1848 counter-revolution he
  walked south to Toulouse, living in the open air and in
  workmen's huts. Bordeaux through most of the 1860s. Canada
  1873-1876 or 1877 on a banknote-design contest win (the issuer
  and year unverified); the return passage paid with money Victor
  Hugo helped furnish. Final years: destitution, separation from
  the family, death on a white wooden bed he had made himself.
- Technique, documented: his lithographs are "literally pen-drawings
  on stone" (AIC 1931), drawn in ink with the pen, not the
  lithographic crayon. Adrien Goetz (2022, from Maxime Preaud's BnF
  research) calls it pen lithography imitating the precision of
  copper engraving line. The French Wikipedia's "no paper, no
  pencil, stippling directly on the stone with pen alone" carries
  a citation flag and is plausible but not fully sourced. Firm:
  extremely fine line, obsessive dense crosshatching, self-taught
  ("no record of any early training in drawing," per the 1931
  catalogue), self-printed many impressions, plates heavily
  reworked across states (roulette work added all over, skies
  inserted, trees removed). Drawings: fine pen in India ink on
  thin transparent paper (papier pelure), often tiny, frequently
  transfer studies for the plates. One transfer process note:
  Lemercier printed transfer lithographs from eight of his etchings
  in 1873.
- Publishing: Auguste Delatre printed the seven 1861 Revue
  Fantaisiste etchings. Joseph-Rose Lemercier printed La Comedie de
  la mort (1854), Le Bon Samaritain (1861), and the 1873 transfer
  set (110 copies per image per a Lemercier invoice; Bresdin took
  about 100 of each to Canada and they were destroyed, leaving
  three to nine surviving copies per image). G. Chariol, Bordeaux,
  one 1868 frontispiece. Bresdin himself, Bordeaux 1866 ("fecit
  et imprime Bordeaux"), with a hand press he carried to Canada.
  No catalogue raisonne until Dirk van Gelder's (1976); Preaud's
  BnF catalogue followed in 2000.
- Key works: La Comedie de la mort 1854, lithograph printed by
  Lemercier, subject from Theophile Gautier's 1838 poem, the print
  Huysmans describes at length in A rebours (1884). Le Bon
  Samaritain 1861, lithograph printed by Lemercier, his greatest
  achievement per the AIC 1931 catalogue, designed 1861 and
  probably printed 1867 (Toledo). The Flight into Egypt series,
  his running leitmotif across decades, the work Redon considered
  his best. La Cite lointaine 1868 (the mysterious waterside city).
  The interiors: several versions, Moldavian and Flemish,
  1848-1860s. Late work: La Maison enchantee 1871, Mon reve 1883
  (published in L'Art Moderne).
- Redon, the documented one-way line: Redon met Bresdin in Bordeaux
  around 1863-64 and learned etching and lithography from him; his
  first etchings sit close to Bresdin's, same mountain landscapes
  and medieval horsemen, though he rejected the minute detail
  early. Then Redon became Bresdin's champion for life: the 1868
  and 1869 La Gironde articles, the memoir A Soi-Meme (1922), and
  decisively the 1908 Salon d'Automne retrospective, twenty-three
  years after the death, with Redon's catalogue text. Redon's
  summary, quoted by Goetz: Bresdin improvised "with joy," adored
  nature, insisted "My drawings are true, whatever they say."
- Reception: admired in his lifetime by Banville, Gautier, Thoré,
  Montesquiou, Courbet and Hugo (who tried to help); the Baudelaire
  admiration is repeated everywhere but no specific Baudelaire
  text on Bresdin was located. Breton hailed him as a precursor;
  Goetz's phrasing: he "opened the way to the Van Gogh legend,"
  the parallel structural (neglect, posthumous myth), not a real
  nickname, which no established source uses.
- Conflicts carried: birth 12 vs 13 August; birthplace Montrelais
  vs Le Fresne-sur-Loire (adjacent, the birth house at Fresne);
  Toulouse years 1848 onward vs 1852-1857; Canada 1873-1876 vs
  1873-1877; children four vs six plus a boy; the 1870-71 Toulouse
  siege has no historical basis; "Le Bonhomme Misere" is an
  Alphonse Legros etching in the sources found, not a Bresdin
  work; no printers named Bequet or Drouart connect to him.

## Works visually inspected (9, all at source resolution)

All nine are public scans, NGA unless noted, plate area measured
on a 90% center crop (mean, std dev, dark fraction below 80,
pale fraction above 200):

1. Le Bon Samaritain, lithograph, 1861 (NGA 30638, 3144x4000):
   117.1 / 54.9, dark 0.376, pale 0.121. The masterwork. A camel
   and a turbaned figure stand in a pale clearing at the center,
   framed left and right by walls of dark forest; above, the sky
   is a woven field of comma-curl clouds with flocks of birds.
   Huysmans's 1884 description, carried from Wikipedia, names the
   ingredients: palms, service trees, oaks "growing all together
   in defiance of seasons and climates," apes, owls, old stumps
   "shapeless as roots of coral," the clearing "dimly revealing
   far away, beyond a camel and the group of the Samaritan," a
   river, "a fairy-like city climbing to the horizon line," a sky
   "dotted with birds, woolly with rolling clouds." The
   detail crop of the upper-left foliage shows two mark families
   side by side: the sky built from thousands of small curls, the
   tree crowns from dense dot clusters with fine branch lines.
   The center crop shows the formula: dark forest wings, pale
   central pocket, the dead-branch tree at right drawn in fine
   line on the pale sky.
2. La Comedie de la mort, lithograph, 1854 (NGA 45667, 2848x4000):
   139.5 / 62.2, dark 0.281, pale 0.295. The skeleton pair with
   raised arms perched on the gnarled tree at right, the owl on
   the branch at left, the hermit's grotto below, the sky a
   woven curl field. The crop shows the species at its purest:
   the skeletons are outline, pale, reserved against the worked
   field, their bodies mostly white paper with light interior
   line, while the tree masses are heavy. The clouds are
   comma-curls accumulated into puffs, each mark the same small
   size, tone carried by accumulation alone.
3. La Maison enchantee (The Haunted House), lithograph, 1871
   (NGA 5266, 4000x2920): 102.9 / 43.5, dark 0.430, pale 0.008,
   the darkest of the exteriors. Almost no paper reserve; the
   whole sheet worked, the house a dark mass with small pale
   window and doorway pockets.
4. Interieur flamand (Flemish Interior), etching, 1873
   (NGA 38948, 2727x4000): 90.5 / 43.3, dark 0.627, pale 0.035.
   The crop shows the room's grammar: shelves with pots, hanging
   onions, a pitcher on a table, a seated figure, all built from
   fine crosshatch, with the palest object (the pitcher and the
   tabletop) as the light event inside the dark. Nothing in the
   room is left unworked.
5. Interior, Dancing Girls Entertaining Chieftains, c. 1870
   (NGA 56264, 4000x2505): 208.2 / 12.0, dark 0.000, pale 0.823.
   Far the palest sheet, almost certainly a washed-out scan of a
   light drawing rather than a tonal choice, carried as a scan
   caveat, not a finding.
6. Le Ruisseau sous bois / The Stream in the Gorge, transfer
   lithograph, 1880 (NGA 38947, 4000x3326): 127.3 / 60.6, dark
   0.315, pale 0.198. The center crop shows the landscape
   formula in late form: dark gorge walls left, a pale reserved
   city-and-river vista in the middle distance, the curl-cloud
   sky. The vista is the same pale pocket the Samaritan's
   clearing is.
7. Moldavian Interior, etching (Cleveland 1945.346, 2328x3400):
   56.3 / 28.0, dark 0.812, pale 0.000. The darkest sheet, a
   room worked almost to black, pale pockets nearly absent. A
   caveat: the Cleveland scan may print darker than the NGA
   sheets; the value is relative, not absolute.
8. Le Coq d'Inde et les paons (turkey and peacocks), 1868
   (1262x2000): 132.7 / 104.1, dark 0.398, pale 0.406, the
   widest spread of the set. The small-file caveat applies, but
   the grammar is unmistakable: the peacock trains are dark
   masses built from thousands of eye-spots, each eye a small
   white-rimmed oval on black, layered scale on scale; behind
   them the pale woven sky with V-formation bird flocks. Dark
   creature mass against the light stippled field, the exterior
   formula inverted from the clearing.
9. The Brook in the Woods, 1880 (NGA 43169, 4000x2898):
   89.8 / 37.7, dark 0.553, pale 0.000. Dark worked woods, the
   brook a pale reserved ribbon, the vista grammar again.

The tonal arc across the body: the value key is free per
composition, as the whole lineage found, but the species has a
rule the lineage had not seen. The pale fraction is never
mid-sized by accident: sheets are either reserve-carrying
exteriors (pale 0.12-0.41: samaritan, comedy, gorge, peacocks)
or worked-to-darkness interiors and night woods (pale 0.00-0.04:
haunted house, flemish interior, moldavian, brook). The sky is
never left to plain paper in the major lithographs; even the
sky is labor.

## The landscape formula, measured

Column-mean profiles (12 bands, plate area) for the three great
landscapes:

- Samaritan: 111.9, 107.3, 105.8, 108.3, 115.0, 126.1, 148.5,
  141.4, 123.2, 111.0, 108.4, 107.1. Dark forest wings on both
  sides, the pale pocket peaking at band 6 (148.5), the dark
  wings returning by band 9. The dip-rise-dip is the composition.
- Comedy of Death: 178.7, 130.9, 134.1, 133.2, 138.9, 138.3,
  138.5, 144.5, 139.6, 129.7, 136.9, 168.9. The dark figure-band
  in the middle, pale woven sky at both edges.
- Stream in the Gorge: 158.3, 114.2, 114.8, 118.4, 128.1, 125.4,
  138.8, 138.5, 128.8, 123.7, 126.0, 167.6. Dark left gorge wall,
  pale center vista, pale right sky.

Findings: the subject of a Bresdin landscape is a value pocket,
either a pale clearing between dark wings or a dark band inside
a pale field, and the pocket is never placed by symmetry alone.
The formula has two named modes: the clearing (samaritan,
gorge) and the mass (peacocks, comedy skeletons against sky).

## Two re-render families, built and inspected, single pass

Family A, the exterior grammar (880x680 schematics: stipple and
curl mark-field, one uniform tiny mark, density modulated by a
blob noise):
- A1 the species: the full field worked, modulated into
  cloud-puffs; a pale reserved clearing in the mid-field with an
  outline bird inside it; one dark accent mass (a stippled bird
  cluster) at upper left. The eye has a path: field, pocket,
  accent.
- A2 the conventional illustration: the same birds as dark
  silhouettes on white paper with a ground line, no field. Reads
  as icons, no weather, no world.
- A3 the uniform field: density flat, no clearing, no birds.
  Reads as a texture swatch, wallpaper.
- A4 the blank-sky inversion: dense dark birds and a dense tree
  blob at the bottom, sky left white. Reads as a sky that was
  forgotten; the density has no weather because the field does
  not surround it.
- Findings: the mark field must be modulated, never uniform;
  the subject must be a density event, both a clearing and an
  accent, not a silhouette placed on paper; the sky is not
  allowed to be empty. The crude schematic still carries the
  logic: A1 is the only frame that reads as a place.

Family B, the interior grammar (700x880 schematics: vertical
hatch plus stipple, one pale reserve pocket):
- B1 the species: the room hatched dark everywhere; the pitcher
  and table reserved pale as the one light event; a small pale
  window at upper right; shelf lines with pot ellipses. The
  pale pocket is the composition: the room reads as an inhabited
  night interior because the reserve is rationed.
- B2 the outline room: perspective box, pitcher in outline,
  nothing hatched. Reads as a diagram, no night.
- B3 the even-packed room: hatch everywhere at one value, the
  pitcher drawn in the same hatch as the walls. Unreadable noise;
  the objects drown.
- B4 the empty box: rectangle and a shelf line, no fill. Nothing.
- Findings: the interior reads through the light pocket; the
  hatch must modulate around it. Even-valued packing is noise,
  outline alone is a diagram. The species is B1: darkness with
  one permitted brightness.

## Quality notes: what makes this work good

The quality thesis: Bresdin's compositional decision is that the
subject is a clearing in the tangle, and the tangle is the
whole sheet. Every choice serves that decision.

1. The whole sheet is worked, edge to edge, with one uniform
   tiny mark. Tone is carried by accumulation, never by stroke
   length. This is why his sheets read as weather and not as
   drawing: the mark is too small to be a gesture and too
   numerous to be decoration. A derivative fails the moment its
   marks get big enough to read as strokes, because then the
   field becomes a style and the clearing becomes a shape.
2. The sky is labor, not rest. The curl-cloud fields are the
   single most distinctive Bresdin device: thousands of
   comma-curls, each the same small size, accumulated into
   puffs. No other printmaker of the revival works the sky this
   hard. A derivative that leaves the sky blank has not
   understood him at all; it has understood a forest vignette.
3. The subject arrives as a density event, in two modes. In the
   clearing mode the pale pocket sits between dark wings and the
   figure inside it is outline and reserve (the Samaritan's
   camel, the skeletons). In the mass mode the dark creature
   sits inside the pale woven field (the peacocks). The figure
   is never just dark-on-white; it is dark-against-woven or
   pale-against-worked. This is why his figures feel found in
   the texture rather than placed on the paper.
4. Equal devotion is the interior rule. In the Flemish and
   Moldavian rooms every object gets the same fine crosshatch:
   the onion braid, the shelf pots, the pitcher, the seated
   figure's sleeve. There is no hierarchy of finish. The eye
   finds the pitcher because it is the one permitted reserve,
   not because it is drawn better. A derivative that renders
   one hero object in detail and sketches the rest has made a
   still life, not a Bresdin room.
5. Restraint inside the obsession: the darks are rationed even
   in the darkest sheets. The Haunted House (dark fraction 0.43)
   still keeps its pale window and doorway pockets; the
   Comedy (0.28) keeps the skeleton bodies as paper. He never
   lets the tangle close over the clearing. The failure mode
   named by the B3 schematic: even-valued packing is noise, and
   noise is where obsession goes when it loses the pocket.

What would make a derivative piece fail: big gestural marks;
a blank or gradient sky; the subject as a silhouette on white;
a rendered hero object in a sketched room; uniform density;
the tangle with no clearing. Any one of these collapses the
species into either illustration or wallpaper.

## Avoid-list additions

- Pen-on-stone presented as crayon lithography (his line is pen,
  not crayon; the crayon grain is a different species).
- The blank or gradient sky sold as his atmosphere.
- Silhouette figures on white paper sold as his composition.
- The rendered hero object in a sketched room.
- Even-valued all-over density without a reserve pocket.
- Big gestural strokes as his "energy" (his marks are tiny).
- The poverty legend as branding: the shoe-polish story is
  hearsay, the eaten rabbit is fiction, and the work was made
  in the productive Toulouse and Bordeaux years.
- "Le Bonhomme Misere" as a Bresdin title (it is Legros).
- The MoMA 1961 "1825" birth date.
- The Baudelaire admiration presented as a documented text.
- "Van Gogh of lithography" as an established nickname.
- The 1870-71 Toulouse siege, which never happened.
