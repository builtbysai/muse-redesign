# Jules Cheret, the lettering that dances (1836-1932)

Deep study, 2026-10-07. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as the
next doorway by the 2026-10-07 Toulouse-Lautrec study ("Cheret" as the
predecessor), the quality-first choice: the Lautrec notes verified that
"invented poster lithography" belongs to Cheret 1866, not Lautrec, and
this session settles what that claim honestly means. After the Lautrec
study established the street poster as a composed object, the question
moves to the man who invented the street poster as a category: the
figure grammar, the dancing lettering, and the high-key palette the
whole 1890s generation worked against or inside of.

The study has two halves. A delegated research dossier (text-only, zero
em dashes, about 8,000 words across two files, built by two research
subagents and verified by the coordinator against Met, MoMA, V&A, BnF,
Art Institute of Chicago, Driehaus Museum, the 2010 Musee des Arts
Decoratifs retrospective catalogue, and peer-reviewed French scholarship)
did the biography, technique, publishing, criticism, conflicts, and gaps.
I visually inspected 10 works at source resolution, measured tonal
stats, made 9 detail crops, and built 2 PIL re-render families,
inspected single pass.

## The artist, briefly

JULES CHERET: born Paris 1836 (the day is contested, May 31 vs June 1),
died Nice 23 September 1932, aged 96. Son of a typesetter. At thirteen
apprenticed three years to a lithographer, lettering brochures, flyers,
and posters; the master's name is not in any source found. Drawing
classes at the Ecole Nationale de Dessin, study in the Louvre, Rubens
and Watteau named as his anchors. London 1859 to 1866: furniture
catalogue work, packaging and labels for the perfumer Eugene Rimmel,
absorption of British poster practice. First major poster commission
1858, Offenbach's Orphee aux Enfers, sold for 100 francs. Return to
Paris 1866: with Rimmel's backing he opened his own color lithographic
shop, the first print shop specialized in the illustrated poster
(journals.openedition.org/estampe/1326). In 1881 he sold the shop to
Chaix but kept artistic direction; the 1890s posters read "Imp. Chaix
(Ateliers Cheret), 20 Rue Bergere, Paris." Railway commissions followed
(Chemins de fer d'Orleans and others). Silver medal 1878, gold 1889 at
the Expositions Universelles; first solo poster exhibition at La
Bodiniere 1889, over 100 works; chevalier of the Legion d'honneur 1890
(officer 1900, commander 1912, grand officer 1926). From about 1895 he
withdrew toward decoration: the Villa La Sapiniere murals at Evian,
the Hotel de Ville salons, the Musee Grevin theatre curtain (dated 1894
in one source, 1900 in another). Blind in old age, retired to Nice,
stopped working 1925; a museum in his honor opened in Nice 1928, four
years before his death; posthumous retrospective at the Salon d'Automne
1933. The Goncourt Journal entry for 16 April 1890 records the banquet
toast: "le premier peintre du mur parisien," "l'inventeur de l'art dans
l'affiche."

## Technique, as the dossier established it

Color lithography, three transparent primary inks (red, yellow, blue)
overprinted to build the secondaries, completed by a separate black
pass. His own 1890 account (Gil Blas interview, via
journals.openedition.org/estampe/2544): three color impressions, then a
fourth that gives the poster all the black tones it needs. The
"three-stone" absolute is popularization; the museum-level evidence
supports three or four colors as typical (the Met catalogues his works
as "lithograph in three colors" and "lithograph in four colors").
Workflow, from his own mouth: draw the subject on paper, get the
client's approval, repaint it in pastel or (more often) gouache, then
redo the work on the stone, each color a new stone. He lettered his own
posters freehand on the stone and drastically cut the amount of text so
the image carries the message. The stone handling is painterly: brush
lines, crosshatch, stipple, watercolor-like washes, flat color areas.
MoMA credits him with crachis, spattering a wax resist on the stone for
shimmering tone with relatively few impressions; Lautrec's 1891 letter
claims his own spatter process as an invention, so the crachis credit is
shared or successive, not settled. His palette is largely limited to
the three primaries with the black outline reduced, blue sometimes
standing in for black. Poster sheets cluster around 120 by 88 cm.
Printer: his own shop to 1881, then Chaix.

The honest myth correction, carried as the session's central finding:
he did not invent lithography (Senefelder, 1790s) or chromolithography
(decades older). What he did in 1866 was open the first shop dedicated
to the poster, apply an economically viable simplification of
chromolithography at large format and high volume, and make the street
poster an art form: his own lettering integrated into the design, the
image carrying the message, the stone worked painterly. MoMA hedges
exactly right: "often regarded as the originator of the artistic
lithographic poster." The French scholarship's canonized phrase is
"inventor and absolute master of the modern poster," which is about the
poster as a category, not the chemistry.

## Works visually inspected (10, all at source resolution)

1. LA VIE PARISIENNE (1866), 6390x8406. The transitional work: a pale
olive ground carrying many small lithographic-crayon vignettes around
large display lettering ("LA VIE PARISIENNE" center, "J. OFFENBACH"
bottom), imprint "Imp. J. CHERET R. de la Tour des Dames 16." Nearly
monochrome. The 1866 poster that is not yet the poster grammar: it
reads as a chromolithographic music cover, not a street poster. Tonal:
mean 148.9, darkfrac 0.038, palefrac 0.119.

2. LA BICHE AU BOIS (sans lettre state), 7401x4985. Landscape format,
no lettering: a fairy in yellow on a tree branch upper left, a
striped-dress figure center right, a seated woman with a trident lower
right, forest left, palace colonnade behind. Flat color zones with
heavy black crayon drawing throughout, imprint "Imp. J. Cheret, 18 Rue
Brunel, Paris." Theatrical and illustrational, the English chromo
manner; the famous "first color poster" looks more like a print than a
poster. Tonal: mean 138.2, darkfrac 0.002. The file is dated 1876 in
one catalogue, 1866 in most general sources: conflict carried.

3. BAL AU MOULIN ROUGE, PLACE BLANCHE (1889), 3940x5848. A woman in a
yellow dress and yellow hat rides a black donkey with a yellow bridle,
whip raised, smiling; behind her a blue-gray wash crowd of dancers and
the red Moulin windmill; a pale laughing figure in reserve upper
right. The title "BAL au Moulin Rouge PLACE BLANCHE" tumbles across
the top in huge red letters with blue-green drop shadows, each letter
tilted its own way, overlapping the picture. Bottom band: "Tous les
Soirs & DIMANCHE JOUR GRANDE FETE LES MERCREDIS & SAMEDIS" in red.
The black donkey is one of his few committed blacks; the yellow
bridle is the attached accent. Tonal: mean 155.0, darkfrac 0.120,
palefrac 0.381.

4. SAXOLEINE, PETROLE DE SURETE (1891/92), 2195x3258. The cherette
species fully formed: a laughing woman in a yellow peignoir leans
from the upper left toward a lamp at right, hair flying, cropped by
the top and left edges, floating against a worked blue-gray ground.
"Saxoleine" huge at bottom in dark blue hand-drawn letters, the S
overlapping her dress, with the descriptive text below in the same
casual hand. The ground crop shows the blue as two washes (dark blue
grain, pale blue-gray) with white paper reserves left as patches:
the "flat" background is weather. Tonal: mean 114.1, darkfrac 0.261,
palefrac 0.064.

5. PAPIER A CIGARETTES JOB (1889), 11422x15539. A redhead in a
yellow-white striped dress, head tilted back, smoking; the huge red
"JOB" letters top left with her smoke curling up into them;
"PAPIER A CIGARETTES" small dark blue at top; "Hors Concours" and
"PARIS 1889" red at bottom; signature "Cheret 89" at right. The dark
blue worked ground fades to pale paper at the edges. The face crop:
flesh as a pale reserve with almost no line, the cheek a flat pink
zone, white reserve hatch marks on the cheek, the hair bright red
with black crayon squiggles, the eye glancing sideways. The lightest
sheet inspected. Tonal: mean 179.6, palefrac 0.512, darkfrac 0.000.

6. PASTILLES GERAUDEL (1892), 11265x15210. A woman in a voluminous
red coat and red bow hat dances through a snowstorm, twirling a
parasol, the pastille box in her left hand; the figure on a big
diagonal from upper right to lower left. The snow is white spatter:
short hand-drawn dashes over both the red coat and the blue ground,
confirmed in the detail crop. The lettering sits bottom right on the
pale ground beside her legs: "Si vous toussez prenez des PASTILLES
GERAUDEL," the brand in huge bold capitals. Imprint Chaix vertical at
the right edge, signature "J Cheret 92." Tonal: mean 150.7, darkfrac
0.138, palefrac 0.265.

7. VIN MARIANI (1894), 10733x14631. The key sheet. The title "VIN
MARIANI" arcs across the top like a rainbow in huge red hand-drawn
letters with dark navy drop shadows, each letter tilted along the
arc, the arc rhyming with the dancer's leap. A woman in a yellow
dress floats diagonally from lower left to upper right, pouring wine
from a bottle into a glass held by a gray silhouette figure at left
(a pale blue-gray double, the one ghost in the set). The dark blue
worked ground sits top left and fades to pale paper bottom right.
The lettering crop: every letter drawn, none typeset, the navy
shadow offset like a second stone slightly out of register. Tonal:
mean 131.8, darkfrac 0.107, palefrac 0.193.

8. PALAIS DE GLACE, CHAMPS-ELYSEES (1893), 10721x29570. The tall
vertical format. A laughing woman in a red dress with black fur trim
ice-skates with arms out; a small male skater behind her in
blue-gray. The red dress is the one big flat; the fur is black crayon
scribble. "PALAIS DE GLACE" arcs across the top in huge dark blue
letters, the P and G oversized, the arc echoing her raised arm;
"Champs-Elysees" in flowing script at the bottom. Pale paper ground
with light blue wash behind the figures. Tonal: mean 114.5, darkfrac
0.252, palefrac 0.061.

9. FOLIES-BERGERE, LA LOIE FULLER (1893), 12011x15660. The signature
work: the dancer as a swirling mass of drapery, arms outstretched,
floating against a flat dark blue-black ground, no horizon. Red
lettering in two bands: "FOLIES-BERGERE" top in the pale margin,
"La Loie Fuller" huge at bottom overlapping the dark field. The
dresser is layered orange, yellow, and peach with gray-green shadow
zones: the overprinted secondaries. The drapery crop is the
session's sharpest correction to the "flat pastel" story: up close
the orange carries fine horizontal white-reserve hatching (the silk
shimmer) and the shadows are diagonal hatch, not tone. The darkest
sheet inspected. Tonal: mean 114.2, darkfrac 0.286, palefrac 0.070.
Imprint: "N 125 Imp CHAIX (Ateliers Cheret) 20 rue Bergere, Paris."

10. ALCAZAR D'ETE, LIDIA (1895), 10829x15180. The intimate register:
a red-haired woman with a big pale yellow hat and pearl necklace,
white dress with roses, leaning back joyfully, her arm reaching to
the right edge. "ALCAZAR D'ETE" red capitals top on the pale band;
"LIDIA" huge red bottom left, tilted on the diagonal, overlapping her
skirt. The blue ground behind her head is heavily worked: hatch and
spatter grain in the detail crop, the roses red overprint on blue.
Tonal: mean 134.1, darkfrac 0.137, palefrac 0.060.

The Fete des Fleurs 1890 (Theatre de l'Opera) was located only at
533x760 and is not counted as inspected.

## Tonal arc

vie_parisienne 148.9, biche_au_bois 138.2, bal_moulin 155.0, saxoleine
114.1, job 179.6, pastilles 150.7, vin_mariani 131.8, palais_de_glace
114.5, loie_fuller 114.2, lidia 134.1. No arc: the value key is free
per composition, the same finding as every lineage study. But the
range is narrower and higher than Lautrec's (104.8 to 190.3): Cheret
never commits the sheet to darkness. The discipline is chroma, not
value: the palette stays in the high key and the black is rationed
(the darkest sheet, Loie Fuller at darkfrac 0.286, is still far from
Lautrec's Bruant cape at 0.437). The darkest color on most sheets is
the blue wash, not black.

## Re-render families (built and inspected, single pass, crude schematics)

Family A, the floating dancer grammar: A1 the tilted floating dancer
with the worked blue wash behind, the red skirt with hatch, and the
lettering arcing around the figure is the species. A2 the upright
centered figure with margins and the lettering in a box below is the
caption failure: it reads as an illustration with a label. A3 the same
grammar on a dark navy ground is the wrong-key failure: the red goes
muddy, the wash disappears into the ground, the cheer is gone, the
high key is load-bearing. A4 the gradient vignette ground is the
stage-prop failure: the poster flattens to a book illustration.
Schematic caveat: my arced lettering clipped letters at the canvas
edges and sits wider than Cheret's arcs; the comparative findings
hold, the letterforms do not.

Family B, the palette key: B1 three flat primaries plus paper plus
minimal black, the zones worked with hatch and grain up close, is the
species. B2 the same zones in muddy browns and olives is the mud
failure: it reads as a faded book plate, the cheer gone. B3 the pure
flat vector tints with no grain and hard edges is the logo failure:
it reads cold and corporate, the worked surface is what makes it feel
printed and human. B4 all colors used everywhere is the rationing
failure: it reads as a clown drawing. The palette is a discipline of
refusal.

## Quality thesis

1. THE LETTERING DANCES. Cheret's title is drawn to move like the
figure: the Vin Mariani rainbow arc following the leap, the Palais de
Glace top arc echoing the raised arm, the Bal Moulin Rouge title
tumbling across the sky with each letter tilted its own way. The
letters are hand-drawn with the same looseness as the figures, often
with a dark drop shadow like a second stone slightly out of register.
A derivative that sets its title straight has made a label. This is
the precise counterpoint to the Lautrec law from the 2026-10-07 study:
Lautrec's lettering lives INSIDE the picture; Cheret's lives in the
SKY and dances with the figure.

2. THE FIGURE FLOATS. No ground line, no horizon, no room. The dancer
is oversize, cropped by the frame edges, tilted on a diagonal, and
suspended in the wash. The frame is a window onto motion, not a stage.
The cherette is an energy vector, not a portrait: she is typically
the only thing on the sheet that is fully drawn, and she is drawn to
move, not to be looked at.

3. THE SMILE IS THE ENGINE. Every face laughs or smiles at the
viewer. The joy is the product being sold: the poster advertises the
feeling, and the figure stands in for the absent product (the Job
woman smoking, the Saxoleine woman lighting the lamp, the Pastilles
woman dancing in the snow). The semiotic economy is documented in
recent scholarship (revistas.um.es/analesff 2023: the poster no longer
invites reading but viewing). A derivative with a neutral figure has
removed the engine and kept the chassis.

4. THREE COLORS PLUS PAPER PLUS A LITTLE BLACK. Red, yellow, blue
transparent inks overprinted on paper, the paper doing the work of
white, black rationed to contours, hair masses, and lettering
shadows. The blue is the worked background wash; the red or the
yellow is the one big flat on the figure. The palette never goes
muddy because the colors never mix on the stone except by overprint:
the secondaries (the Loie Fuller orange, the gray-green shadows) are
optical, not mixed. A derivative that blends its palette on the
canvas has left the lithographic discipline.

5. FLAT AT DISTANCE, WORKED UP CLOSE. The zones read flat from the
street and show hatch, spatter, and wash grain at full resolution:
the Loie Fuller drapery carries fine white-reserve horizontal
hatching (the silk shimmer), the Pastilles snow is hand-drawn white
dashes over red and blue, the blue grounds are washed with paper
showing through. The "flat pastel" reputation is a distance
phenomenon. A derivative that renders true flat vector tints gets
the postcard, not the poster. This is the same distance law the
Lautrec study found, in a different hand: Lautrec spatters, Cheret
hatches.

6. THE WASH IS THE WEATHER. The background is not a color but a
condition: irregular blue-gray wash patches laid around the figure
like a halo, with paper showing through. The figure is always set
against the wash, never against a flat field. Loie Fuller is the
exception that proves the rule: the one flat dark ground in the set,
and it reads as the night the dance happens in, not as a background.

7. THE INVENTION IS THE SHOP, NOT THE STONE. The honest version of
the 1866 claim: he did not invent lithography or chromolithography,
and "three stones" is popularization of his own account (three
transparent impressions plus a black pass). What was genuinely new
was the first print shop dedicated to the poster, the economically
viable simplification of the color process at large format, and the
poster as an art form: his own lettering drawn on the stone, the
text cut to a minimum, the image carrying the message. The two 1866
works inspected here are still transitional chromo illustration; the
mature grammar arrives in the 1870s and 1880s. Pinning "the first
color poster" to one sheet is unverifiable, and the Biche au bois
date itself is disputed.

## Avoid-list additions

The centered static figure with margins; the straight band title as
his signature lettering (his bands exist, the arc is the signature);
gradient or vignette grounds as his atmosphere; true flat vector
color as his surface; muddy mixed colors; the unsmiling figure; the
dark key as his register; "invented lithography" or "invented the
three-stone process" as settled fact; the 1866 Biche au bois date as
settled; the 1900 Legion d'honneur date (1890 is verified, with the
Goncourt Journal as primary source); the Daragnes 1925 catalogue
raisonne (no trace found; the working catalogue is Broido 1992); the
Cockburn monograph (no trace found); the Goncourt 1895 article (dealer
lore, unverified; the real Goncourt text is the 1890 Journal toast);
crachis credited to Lautrec alone (MoMA credits Cheret); "father of
the poster" as a verified contemporary phrase (period sources say
"maitre de l'affiche" and "pere de l'affiche artistique/moderne" in
citations not directly read); the BnF washed scans as his palette
(this session used the full-resolution Gallica-derived scans);
Maindron's book credited to Cheret as author (it is Maindron's, with
Cheret's chromolithographs, printed on his presses).

## Conflicts carried

Birth day May 31 vs June 1, 1836. La Biche au bois 1866 (most general
sources) vs 1876 (French Wikiversity study page, Alamy record) vs 1867
for a Bernhardt-related version (visual-arts-cork). Musee Grevin
theatre curtain 1894 (Galerie Ary Jan) vs 1900 (fr.wikipedia). Crachis:
MoMA credits Cheret with the wax-resist spatter; Lautrec's 1891
letter claims his own spatter process. Les Maitres de l'Affiche:
Cheret plate count 67 (Appelbaum, secondhand via auction listings,
unverified against the series); series plates 1-240 with 202 and 212
missing in volume 5. Maindron 1886: publisher's title page says 20
chromolithographs by Cheret; two dealers' collations say by Cheret,
Choubrac, and Gambier. Poster oeuvre: "more than 1,000" repeated
everywhere, no exact count; Broido's catalogue total not established
this session. Exposition silver medal "1879" in one dealer source,
almost certainly 1878. Senefelder's invention 1796 vs 1798.

## Honest gaps

No original handled; all web reproductions with unverified color
balance. The two 1866 works are the transitional chromo manner, so
the "first color poster" claim could not be visually tested on a
mature 1866 sheet. The Fete des Fleurs 1890 secured only at 533px.
The BnF catalogue record for La Biche au bois was unreachable (domain
blocked for the research tool), which is the single best source for
settling its date. Whether Cheret used transfer paper, and whether
assistants ever handled lettering on high-volume later work, are
unverified. Exact stone counts per poster are unverified (museums give
color counts, not stone counts). The Goncourt 1895 article, the exact
contemporary wording of "father of the poster," and the first
appearance of the "invented the three-stone process" label are
primary-source tasks for a future session. The 1884 first group poster
exhibition claim is single-source (Driehaus). Schematics single-pass
and crude; the arced lettering in A1/B1 clipped at the canvas edges.
