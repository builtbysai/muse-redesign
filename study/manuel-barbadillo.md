# Manuel Barbadillo: Study Notes

**Profile:** Manuel Barbadillo (Cazalla de la Sierra, Seville, 1929, to
Torremolinos, Malaga, 2003), Spanish painter, a leading figure of Arte
Normativo and a pioneer of computerized art in Spain. Self taught. He
studied law, then gave himself to painting. Morocco 1955 to 1957, where
Islamic art pushed him from figuration to abstraction. New York 1959 to
1962, where abstract expressionism gave way to monochrome and structure.
From 1964 his work became modular: geometric compositions built from one
repeated module, almost entirely black and white, brown only rarely. In
March 1968 a letter arrived from Mario Fernandez Barbera, an IBM
technician, saying the computer could ease the experiments he was
already doing by hand. That took him to the Centro de Calculo de la
Universidad de Madrid (CCUM), with its IBM 7090 and IBM 1401, and to
the seminar on the automatic generation of visual forms. DAM describes
his career in four stages: 1964 to 1968, the single U module and
macro-modules; 1968 to 1979, a four module system built with computers;
1979 to 1984, the personal computer years, modules broken into two
basic shapes, color admitted, then regrouped into five modules; 1984 to
2003, the computer as an auxiliary instrument of creation rather than a
generator. Member of the Computer Arts Society in London and of the
artistic council of the Gesellschaft fur Computer Grafik und Computer
Kunst in Munich. Work in the Reina Sofia and the CAAC, Seville.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on four images, one local re-render sheet.
Read in full: Michael Thompson, "Computer Art: A Visual Model for the
Modular Pictures of Manuel Barbadillo" (Leonardo, Vol. 5 No. 3, Summer
1972, pp. 219 to 226); F. Briones, "Pintura modular" (BCCU, the full
account of the 1968 to 1969 CCUM work, including the module algebra and
the three zone terminal); the DAM biography (the four stages and the
Wiener connection); Barbadillo's own 1969 text "El ordenador.
Experiencias de un pintor con una herramienta nueva" via quotation; the
Galeria Rafael Ortiz exhibition pages. Four artworks visually
inspected: Disalda 1969 (finished black and white modular composition,
edition 2/20, signed), Acuario (working drawing on graph paper, a module
lab sheet annotated ACUARIO, MODULO, INTERIOR, EXTERIOR, dated Ene-71),
Relieve Uno 1984 (wooden relief of quarter circle groove tiles in a
tray), and the 1969 to 1970 mural on the Apartamentos Milan in
Fuengirola. One PIL re-render sheet built and visually inspected: the
module family, three 4x4 compositions with broken edge counts, and the
COMPO algebra tiled. NOT seen: Thompson's Fig. 1 modules except through
garbled OCR (my module geometry is reconstructed from the Disalda and
Acuario inspections), his PAGE 12 article and the Ordenadores en el
arte texts firsthand, Wiener's book as he read it, any original plotter
output, the relief or the mural in person.

## The module: one shape, sixteen postures

From 1964 to 1968 Barbadillo based many pictures on a single black and
white square module. His own description, in his own words: compositions
in which the total form is generated by the integration of smaller
forms, identical to each other and ideally inscribed in a grid, which
can have four postures, each produced by a 90 degree turn of the last.
And the position of each one determined in certain measure, not totally,
those surrounding it, since its design allows connection of one to
another in several ways.

Thompson counts sixteen different structural elements from the module:
four rotations, mirror image, and black white interchange. Any of them
can go in any cell of a 4 by 4 grid. The pre computer module is the
hook: "como de gancho encerrado en un cuadrado," like a hook enclosed
in a square, which he said came from two basic forms, a square and a
quarter of a circle. The Acuario sheet shows the lab work: rows of the
module in different orientations, hatched interior against exterior
studies, a small grid diagram, a developed squared spiral. Disalda shows
the finished state: the grid has vanished into flowing black and white
ribbons, and you can still pick out the cups and hooks interlocking.

## What makes it work: coalescence

Thompson's central observation, and the one that survives every
re-reading: areas of the same color in adjacent structural elements
coalesce and lead the eye freely about the picture. The module grid is
the scaffold. The fused color regions are the building. When it works,
you cannot see the cells at all, only the meander. When it fails, you
see tiles.

Barbadillo noted the characteristics himself, and Thompson records
them: the number of pictures producible from a few elements is enormous,
but the number that is pleasing is very small; a 2 by 2 group can be
used as one element; the proper joining of edges is critical and often
extremely difficult (he wrote an edge matching program for it); the
angle at which a form meets the adjoining element gives rhythm, and
poor matching may be used deliberately to get the effect; pleasing
relationships come from repetition, point reflection, mirror image,
rotation, and color reversal. Fusing of forms into extended black and
white areas sometimes gives the feeling of a white form on a black
background, or the reverse.

## The selector: symmetry as liveliness

On top of coalescence, Barbadillo used strong symmetry, which Thompson
says gives liveliness to the work. Thompson's paper is really an attempt
to program a selector: a subjective visual model of how the eye moves
over these pictures, built from two kinds of movement. Tracking: the
eye follows continuous same color paths, modeled as nodes joined by
arcs, with arc values for smooth continuation (2) and for sharp bends
or color crossings (1). Skipping: the attention jumps between lobes
that are repetitions, mirror images, or point reflections of each
other, scored by relationship type and by enhancing features (same
color, emphasized by strong tracking, isolated by the picture edge). He
computed both scores by hand for eight 2 by 2 test pictures. The scores
are subjective and he says so; the point is that the definitions are
temporarily closed so a program can use them.

Barbadillo, writing to Thompson in 1971: "I very strongly believe
rhythm to be the main, the meaningful element of a painting, whether it
is an abstract or figurative one." And the closing request of the paper,
again Barbadillo's words: could the program modify the structure of
the initial picture in several steps, according to an ascending scoring
table, so that it would show progressive phases in the interconnection
of networks? He calls this the process nature of the art research. The
piece is the search, not the result. That sentence is forty years early.

## The algebra: Briones and the three zone terminal

At the CCUM, F. Briones wrote up the system as a formal algebra. The
working vocabulary is micromodules: N (black), B (white), A, M, and c
(the quarter circle cell everything recent can be generated from). The
functions: COMPL (black white interchange), GIR01, GIR02, GIR03 (90, 180,
270 degree turns), ESPEC (mirror), UNION, INTER, EXCLU (boolean
combinations), and COMPO(k, x1, x2, ...), which lays k by k modules in
rows and reduces the composed result back to micromodule size so it can
be used as a unit again. His own modules get written as formulas:
B1 = COMPO(2,N,B,N,B), and so on. There is even an edge description
language for continuity: module borders written as sequences like
/N45/B/B45/N/, with rules for how a small letter's indifference to
continuity propagates into the capital beside it.

The working method has three phases: choose the working module or
modules; compose the macro-module; compose the picture. And Briones
describes the instrument they were building, a light pen terminal with
three zones. Zone A, the archive: twelve cells holding generated
modules. Zone T, the work zone: two modules and the transformation
keys. Zone C, the composition zone: a k by k grid, k two, three, or
four. Point with the light pen to move, rotate, combine, compose. When
the artist signals SELECCIONAR, the chosen working modules are drawn
on a permanent support by printer or plotter, and their characteristics
are punched onto cards for later use. The program was being written by
Enrique de la Hoz. The punch cards are the save file.

## The re-render: what the mechanism actually does

I implemented the hook as a maze tile: a thick U band whose arms meet
one cell edge at the 0.30 and 0.70 points. Eight structural elements
(four rotations times complement; the symmetric U has no distinct
mirror, where Thompson's more asymmetric module variant gives his
sixteen). Three 4 by 4 compositions, same module:

1. Random placement: 11 broken edges. Bands dead end mid cell, the
grid reads as tiles, the composition is a specimen plate. The admitted
failure state.
2. Edge continuous placement, found by backtracking: 0 broken edges.
The bands connect across every shared edge into long meanders, and
large same color regions fuse exactly as Thompson describes. This is
the selector doing its work, and it confirms Barbadillo's own note that
each module's position determines its neighbors in certain measure.
3. A 2 by 2 macro-module reflected by point reflection, after
Thompson's reading of Barbadillo's 1966 Modular painting I: 4 broken
seams. Naive symmetry breaks continuity. This confirms Thompson's
warning that proper edge joining is critical and often extremely
difficult, and it is the honest reason his edge matching program
existed.

The COMPO panel (COMPO(2,x,GIR01(x),GIR03(x),GIR02(x)) tiled two by two)
renders as a textile field, pretty and dead: formal construction with
no continuity guarantee reads as pattern, not composition. The lesson
is structural. The module is trivial. The selector, the continuity
repair, and the progressive scoring are the entire art.

## What makes it sing, and what is overdone

What sings: the disappearance of the grid. One dumb module, placed
under a strict continuity rule and a symmetry scheme, producing a
composition the module never contained. The eye led by fused color, not
by drawn lines. Rhythm as the stated subject.

Avoid list additions: Truchet tile fields as pure pattern (the quarter
annulus variant is taken several times over in the gallery and the
seed file; anything new here must be about coalescence, repair, or
process, not about the tiles). Enumerating the sixteen postures as a
specimen plate (that is documentation of the system, not a piece).
Symmetry for its own sake (the pinwheel COMPO tiling shows how fast
formal symmetry goes decorative). White noise as profundity stays on
the list from the Moles session; Barbadillo is the constructive
counterexample, the man who replaced noise with a selector.

## Honest gaps

Thompson's Fig. 1 module geometry comes from my reconstruction off the
Disalda and Acuario inspections, not from his printed figures, which
the OCR garbled. My U is symmetric and yields eight structural
elements; his sixteen belong to his more asymmetric module variant.
The backtracking selector in the re-render is mine; Barbadillo selected
by eye, and Thompson's tracking and skipping scores are not
implemented here. His PAGE 12 article, the Ordenadores en el arte
texts, and Wiener's Cybernetics and Society are known through
quotation, not firsthand. The Briones edge language section on
indifference propagation is summarized, not fully worked through. No
original plotter drawing handled; the relief and the mural seen only in
web photographs.
