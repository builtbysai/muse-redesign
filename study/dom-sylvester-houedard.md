# Dom Sylvester Houedard: Study Notes

**Profile:** Dom Sylvester Houedard (Pierre-Thomas-Paul Joseph, Guernsey,
February 16, 1924, to January 15, 1992), known everywhere as dsh, all lower
case. The doorway named first by the 2026-10-03 Robert Rehfeldt study, the
other end of the typewriter line: where Ruth Wolf-Rehfeldt ran the strictest
grid machine and her husband ran the postal system, dsh ran the page as a
visual field. Benedictine monk at Prinknash Abbey in Gloucestershire,
ordained 1959, after army intelligence work 1944-47 and history at Jesus
College, Oxford (the en-academic biography entry says Cambridge, every other
source says Oxford). Literary editor of the Jerusalem Bible 1961-66. He
introduced concrete poetry to Britain in 1961, published the first UK article
on it, "Concrete Poetry and Ian Hamilton Finlay," in Typographica no. 8,
December 1963. His friend Edwin Morgan coined the term for his work:
typestracts, typewriter plus abstract. All made on an Olivetti Lettera 22,
the portable he had already started experimenting with during his army
years. First visual poems appeared in Ian Hamilton Finlay's Poor. Old.
Tired. Horse. in 1963. Co-founded the concrete poetry collective Gloup with
John Furnival and Ken Cox in 1964, co-founded Openings Press with Furnival
the same year. Exhibited at Better Books and Signals 1965, the ICA's
"Between Poetry and Painting" 1965, Arnolfini 1966, Lisson 1967 and 1968
(first solo during Lisson's inaugural year), the V&A retrospective 1971,
Stedelijk Amsterdam 1972. The 1969 "12 dancepoems of the cosmic typewriter"
were made with David Medalla's Exploding Galaxy and performed as dance.
Fame made him a problem for the monastery in the mid-1970s, and for the last
ten years of his life he turned back to theology as an infirmarian and
stopped making art. His archive came to the John Rylands Library in the
early 1990s; the Sackner Archive in Miami holds the largest collection of
the work. Address book said to run to 3,000 names; his letters were
collectable typewriter-art objects in their own right.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on three artworks at source resolution, two
local re-render studies built and inspected, pass 2 of Study B after a
layout overprint in pass 1. Read end to end: the Richard Saltoun biography
and Lisson biography (Lettera 22, blue black and red ribbons, the Morgan
coinage); the DesignObserver "Cosmic Typewriter" piece (the Typographica
essay in condensed note style, the frailty line, the Simpson "Notes from the
Cosmic Typewriter" 2012 book and South London Gallery day, the John Rylands
archive, Meister Eckhart to Zen as the reading list behind the work);
the Fast Company piece (the estate history, the monastery pressure quote);
the Rylands "Digital Concrete" reconstruction of Frog Pond Plop (the 1965
haiku as a folding paper fortune teller: flat it is jumbled letters, folded
it reveals frog, then pond, then plop, in sequence, the transience made
mechanical; the blog's honest account of failing to model it in Blender);
the Iowa Sackner finding aid entries (the Frog Pond working ideas, the
Opening Number No. 6 realization); the Peter Harrington listing for the 1965
Openings Press edition (500 copies, letter sheet folded into the fortune
teller plus instructions); the Princeton concrete poetry archive guide
(the dsh alphabet sent to Edward Wright, the 4-fold Maoscreen, the frog pond
plop memorial card). Visually inspected: "cored emptrix" (Artsy, framed
sheet: isometric blue slash-hatch block with a cored diamond void, tiny
lowercase title top-left, vertical "dsh 270563" signature at right, dated
27 May 1963); the 1964 red-and-black collage sheet signed "dsh 041264"
(designobserver image: overstrike red 5-glyph, "chopin" word-ribbons
curving, "henri" line-polygon, red "h" tower, the ECKERT AILLON diagonal
band, dense overstrike "e" halftone block at lower right); "that and that"
(dreamideamachine archive, signed "dsh 090771": red wireframe cube over a
blue field of one repeated "o" forming a sheared triangular shadow plane,
lowercase blue title top-left); one Tate artist-page image (too small to
read detail, a thin black box holding scattered glyphs, rain-like). NOT
seen: any original in person, the Frog Pond Plop folded object, the moire
poems, the mirror reverse poems, the 12 dancepoems performed, "sonic
water," "69," "splendid weeping" as objects (known through captions only),
the Simpson monograph, Ceolfrith 15.

## The core finding: the machine keeps its staircase

Every artist in this study sequence owns an instrument, and dsh's is the
most literal: the Olivetti Lettera 22 is the whole workshop, and the cell
grid of its typebasket is the composition's law. His diagonal forms
staircase. They cannot help it. The finding of both re-render studies is
that he never fought this, and his good work comes from the refusal to
fight it. When he wanted a clean edge he did not smooth the typewriter's
line; he changed instruments entirely, drawing the wireframe in red with a
felt pen over the typed field. Two voices, each honest in its own register,
never one voice faking the other. That is the compositional decision the
whole practice turns on: the machine is allowed to be a machine, and
cleanliness is bought with a second instrument, not with correction.

**Study A, the cored-cube species: the staircase is the texture.**
I built his "cored emptrix" species as a cell-grid drawing: blue slash
hatching for the isometric faces, a cored diamond void cut from the left
face, tiny lowercase title, vertical dsh-date signature, wide margin.
Control one: staircase edges left alone. Control two: the same hatching
with a smoothed anti-aliased diagonal drawn over it. The smoothed version
reads as a vector graphic that happens to contain slashes; the staircase
version reads as a typewriter that happens to contain a cube. The surprise
finding: the smoothed control accidentally reproduced his own move. The red
straight edge I drew to "fix" the staircase is exactly his red wireframe
solution, an overlay in a second voice. He never anti-aliased. He overlaid.
So the species rule is: the machine keeps its staircase, and any clean line
arrives as a guest, signed by a different instrument.

**Study B, the letterfield-shadow species: one glyph is a shadow, two are
a pattern.** I built his "that and that" species: a red wireframe cube over
a blue letterfield forming a sheared triangular shadow plane. Three
controls: the field in one glyph ("o"), the field in two ("o", "x"), the
title in uppercase. The one-glyph field reads as a single cast shadow; the
eye stops reading characters and starts reading tone and edge, which is
what lets the red cube float. The two-glyph field reads as camouflage:
the eye keeps reading characters, the shadow reading dies, the field
becomes decoration. The uppercase title shouts at the cube's volume and
breaks the sheet's quiet. Pass 1 overprinted the third panel (a layout bug
that ironically produced dsh's own overtyping); pass 2 is clean and the
finding rests on the clean pass.

## Quality notes: what makes it good

The margin is the composition. On every strong sheet most of the A4 is
blank, and the motif sits in it the way a haiku sits in a season: one
event, placed. The blank is not left over; it is the field the event
happens in. A derivative piece fails here first, by filling the sheet.

The title refuses hierarchy. Every title is lowercase, small, and parked
away from the motif, "cored emptrix," "that and that," "frog pond plop."
They read as labels on a specimen jar, deliberately quieter than the work.
A derivative fails by letting the title perform, or by titling in a way
that explains the piece.

The signature is typographic, not calligraphic. "dsh" plus a six-digit
date, typed vertically at the sheet's edge: 270563, 041264, 090771. It is a
date discipline as strict as Kawara's, but it lives in the margin as part
of the grid, not as the subject. The signature never competes; it
timestamps. A derivative fails by signing like a painter.

Two colors carry the syntax. Blue or black for the machine field, red for
the overlay, the comment, the wireframe. The red is always the second
voice, never the ground. In "that and that" the red cube is the object and
the blue field is its shadow; reverse the assignment and the piece dies.

The word is allowed to be a material. In the 1964 collage sheet "chopin"
and "henri" are not names being invoked; they are ribbons, bent into
curves and polygons, repeated until the word becomes a line. The semantic
content is thinned to near zero and the visual content carries everything.
A derivative fails by picking words that insist on being read.

Restraint of vocabulary is the whole technique. The re-renders proved it
twice: one glyph is a shadow, two are a pattern; one motif is a poem, two
are a collage. His sparest sheets are his best because the instrument's
limitation is treated as the poem's form. Frog Pond Plop is three words
and a fold. The 1964 collage is the busiest sheet I saw and the weakest
in the set, interesting for its energy, not its judgment.

**Avoid-list additions:** typewriter texture as set dressing (the grid
without the grid's law); ASCII cubes without the staircase discipline;
anti-aliased typewriter pastiche; two-glyph fields presented as shadows;
uppercase titles on quiet sheets; signatures that perform; words chosen
for meaning in a piece that needs material; full sheets (the margin is
load-bearing); the monk story as branding over the machine discipline.

**Honest gaps:** no original handled, so ribbon color readings follow
reproductions; "sonic water," "69," "splendid weeping," the moire poems
and the mirror reverse poems not seen as objects; the Simpson monograph
and Ceolfrith 15 not read; Frog Pond Plop known through the Rylands
reconstruction and the Harrington listing, never folded; the dance poems
never seen performed; letterforms measured from DejaVu Sans Mono, not a
Lettera 22; Study A pass 1 had no void bug but the void placement is my
reconstruction, not a measured tracing; the 1964 collage sheet's title is
unknown, described by its signature date only.

Next doorways: concrete poetry proper (Eugen Gomringer, the Noigandres
group), the mail art network as a node (Ray Johnson, Eastern Bloc
circuits), Paulo Bruscky as his own study, Edwin Morgan (who named the
typestract), Ian Hamilton Finlay (the other British pole of 1963).
