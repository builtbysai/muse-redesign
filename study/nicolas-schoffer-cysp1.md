# Nicolas Schoffer, CYSP 1 and the Cybernetic Towers - Deep Study Notes

**Date:** 2026-09-30 (study pass)
**Subject:** Nicolas Schoffer (born Kalocsa, Hungary, 6 September
1912, died Paris, 8 January 1992), CYSP 1 (1956, with Philips
engineer Jacques Bureau), the spatiodynamic tower at Saint-Cloud
(built 1954, shown 1955), the Cybernetic Tower of Liege (1961),
and the unbuilt Tour Lumiere Cybernetique for Paris La Defense
(designed 1963). Schoffer read Wiener, abandoned painting for
sculpture, and built the first works that sense a room or a city
and answer it. His own summary of the aim: not to create, but
to create creation.

**Honest gaps:** CYSP 1's exact response rules are still not
documented in any source read here. The only sourced colour
rule is the critic Guy Habasque's line, quoted in the thesis,
that "a red light calms it and causes its movements to slow
down." The often repeated counterpart, that blue excites it,
was searched for twice this session and not found in a primary
or technical source, so it is not claimed here. How loud a
sound had to be, which of the two travel speeds a given input

open. Schoffer's own books (La ville cybernetique 1969, La
Tour Lumiere Cybernetique 1973) were read only through the
thesis. The Liege tower's mirror count disagrees between
sources (66 in Monoskop, 64 mirror plates on 33 turning axes
in the Greisch restoration sheet), and CYSP 1's dimensions
disagree between the Hyperallergic caption (250 x 180 x 170
cm) and a catalogue record (250 x 130 x 170 cm). Both
discrepancies are left as found.

## The mechanism, as far as the sources take it

CYSP 1 stands 2.6 m tall on a base mounted on four rollers.
The base holds the mechanism and what Philips called the
electronic brain, built by Jacques Bureau. This was the second
brain Bureau had made for Schoffer, after the Saint-Cloud
tower. Above the base, a black steel grid carries 16 pivoting
polychromed plates, each turned by a small motor under its own
axis; Monoskop counts 19 micromotors, two photoelectric cells
(or phototubes), two microphones, a traction motor and a
direction motor. The sculpture had total autonomy of movement:
travel in all directions at two speeds, plus axial rotation of
the whole frame and eccentric rotation that sets the plates
swinging. The cells and microphone caught variations in fields
of colour, light intensity and sound intensity, and all of
these changes occasioned reactions.

It was first presented in 1956 at the Sarah Bernhardt Theatre
in Paris during the Night of Poetry. The same year, Maurice
Bejart's dancers performed with it on the roof terrace of Le
Corbusier's Cite Radieuse in Marseille, to concrete music by
Pierre Henry. In Jasia Reichardt's 1968 Cybernetic Serendipity
at the ICA it was programmed to move in a restricted space in
response to hand claps.

The Saint-Cloud tower came first and was bigger: a 50 m
cybernetically programmed spatiodynamic sculpture with sound,
built for the 1955 building trade show at the Parc Saint-Cloud.
Its brain held a rheostat, a photoelectric cell system and a
microphone, and it was programmed to respond to humidity and
temperature as well as light, colour and sound. The brain chose
among 12 tape sequences by Pierre Henry, composed partly from
recordings of the tower's own plates being struck and set
vibrating. Philips promoted it as a unique robot conductor.
The tower was temporary and came down within a year.

Liege, 1961, is the Saint-Cloud idea made permanent: a 52 m
tubular lattice in the Parc de la Boverie on the Meuse, beside
the Palais des Congres. The city brochure describes arms of
different lengths carrying motorised aluminium vanes, driven
by an electronic brain reacting to temperature, wind and city
noise, with three kinds of action: movement of the reflective
vanes, sound (reworked natural noise and electronic sound,
chosen at random), and light (daylight reflected by day,
coloured artificial light by night). Monoskop counts 66
revolving mirrors and 120 electronically controlled coloured
projectors; the Greisch restoration sheet counts 64 mirror
plates of different shapes on 33 motor driven axes with
variable speeds. The tower also gained a fourth response
channel Saint-Cloud did not have: its parts move. It was
classified as a monument in 1997. After restorations it still
moves and flashes colours, but it no longer emits audio and no
longer responds to environmental data; since 2016 it answers
Twitter instead (tweet a colour word to @CyberTower and it
flashes that colour and tweets back a song lyric containing
it). The conservator Manon D'haenens argues the best way to
respect Schoffer's intent is to change and substitute, an open
artwork. Schoffer himself said the tower would be permanently
modifiable for a century, and that what matters is the idea
and its sociocultural impact.

The Tour Lumiere Cybernetique for La Defense (designed 1963,
never built) is the same system at civic scale. Sources give
307 m, 347 m and 360 m for its height at different planning
stages. Wikipedia's technical list: 14 concave mirrors, 363
mirrors, 144 rotatable motorised axes, 2085 electronic
flashes, 2250 partly coloured headlights, and thermometers,
hygrometers and anemometers feeding it; 15 light cannons at
the top would extend its optical presence by 2 km. Seven
visitor platforms were to hold restaurants, a concert hall
with an organ, a post office and shops. De Gaulle, Pompidou
and Malraux backed it; Pompidou's death in April 1974 and the
oil crises killed it. Schoffer gave it about 15 years and
considered it his magnum opus.

## The perturbation rule

The part of Schoffer that matters most for this project is not
the hardware, it is his definition of cybernetics, quoted in
the thesis from Le nouvel esprit artistique (1970): cybernetics
keeps phenomena in balance through organised control of
information, including perturbations, and the result should be
"a fluid permanence, in a flexible equilibrium", where any
tendency to periodicity or stagnation triggers an intervention
of disturbances to preserve openness. In Perturbation et
chronocratie (1978) he sharpened it: the disturbance is an
energetic stimulus secreted by any evolutionary process whose
program reaches a threshold of redundancy, saturation,
periodicity or stagnation; without disturbance the process
must regress or disappear; "only the disturbance emerges as
permanence." The Liege brochure shows this shipped in
hardware: an "indifference engine" (moteur d'indifference)
intervenes at random to break any monotony in the tower's
reactions. A Schoffer system does not only answer the room.
It watches itself for sameness, and kicks itself when it finds
any.

## Photographs inspected (visually studied, 2026-09-30)

1. CYSP 1 (Hyperallergic image 1-cysp_isized.jpg, 1460 x 2100).
The black grid is the composition: a strict rectangular cage,
and inside it the plates hang like flags. Rectangles split
white and blue, white and red, black and yellow; two discs,
one large black and white target, one small white disc with a
red dot. Nothing is centred; the eye keeps crossing the cage.
The base is a plain black cylinder, deliberately dumb, so the
colour lives up top where the motion is.
2. Spatiodynamique 2, the spatial clock with contrasting
movements, 1949 to 1950 (4-spatiodynamique_2sized.jpg, 1460 x
2189). Before the electronics: a tall thin mast, a painted
clock disc at the top left ringed with blue and yellow ticks,
and a small cage of red and black slats tilting off to the
right. The whole thing is a sentence about time told with two
unbalanced weights. Worth noting how much of CYSP 1 is
already here: the mast, the grid, the primary colours, the
refusal of a pedestal.
3. Tour Lumiere Cybernetique, the La Defense drawing, c. 1970
(11-dessin_tlcsized.jpg, 1460 x 1902). Monochrome gouache,
night. The tower is a lattice of light cannons standing over
the parvis, beams crossing the black sky in long fans. The
city around it is grey slabs and tiny figures. The drawing
sells weather, not architecture: the tower reads as a
lighthouse that reports the air.
4. Effet prismatique (14-effet_prismatiquesized.jpg, 1460 x
974). A mirrored kaleidoscope room: coloured light fragments,
red and cyan and warm white, broken across faceted mirrors
into a field with no floor. This is the luminodynamic line of
the work, light as the material itself, and it is the most
immediately beautiful image of the six.
5. Maquette du Centre de reflexion prismatique, 1975
(15-centre_reflexion_prismatiquesized.jpg, 1460 x 1996). A
model interior with tiny figures under walls of red, blue and
black targets and diamonds, lit until the paint glows. The
maquette makes the urban ambition legible: a room whose walls
are instruments.
6. KYLDEX I, 1973 (9-kyldex_isized.jpg, 1460 x 946). Black and
white photograph, dancers as silhouettes in front of a glowing
sculpture with light panels. Five autonomous sculptures danced
with the Hamburg ballet, music by Pierre Henry. Even in a
still, the hierarchy is clear: the machine glows, the humans
are shadows, and the photograph knows which one it is about.

## Palette and composition, translated

The CYSP 1 palette is Mondrian disciplined: red, yellow, blue,
black and white, and, per the thesis via Eleonore de
Lavandeyra Schoffer, a standing distaste for green. The plates
are flat colour, split in halves or bands, never gradient.
Composition is a cage plus flags: a rigid grid that never
moves as a shape, carrying coloured rectangles that never stop
recomposing inside it. The still photograph already contains
the piece's whole argument, which is rare for kinetic work:
fixed armature, restless colour, no front, no pedestal, no
privileged view. At tower scale the palette becomes light
itself, coloured projectors on aluminium, and the composition
becomes the sky.

## Local re-render (Python model, 2026-09-30)

A CYSP 1 model was built in Python (matplotlib, 16 plates on a
rotating black frame, 60 seconds per scenario at 10 fps): two
photocell channels (light level, redness) and a microphone
level drive plate pivot speeds through per plate temperaments;
the base picks still, slow or fast gear from sound and light;
claps turn the heading; an indifference engine copied from the
Liege brochure fires a random kick after 8 seconds of uniform
input. Colour balance enters only through the sourced rule:
red dominant light calms (a 0.72 damping factor), and the
calming releases as redness falls. Four scenarios:

- Quiet room: mean plate speed 0.301, base still 100 percent
  of the time, 7 perturbations in 50 measured seconds. The
  plates never fully stop: the indifference engine keeps
  restarting them. A Schoffer piece in an empty room still
  performs for itself.
- Hand claps every 6 seconds: mean plate speed 0.399, 20 gear
  switches as each clap bumps the drive over the slow gear
  threshold, 7 perturbations.
- Red wash: mean plate speed 0.178, the slowest of the four,
  base still throughout. Habasque's line survives translation
  into code: red calms, measurably.
- Concert (loud, varying): mean plate speed 0.869, fast gear
  34.5 percent of the time, 15 gear switches, zero
  perturbations, because the input never stays uniform for 8
  seconds. The environment does the disturbing by itself.

Frames at 8, 30 and 58 seconds per scenario were saved and the
concert and red wash mid frames inspected: pivoting red, blue,
yellow, black and white bars on the dark rotating frame read
clearly as the CYSP 1 family, and the red wash frame sits
visibly closer to rest. The model is a study instrument, not
an artwork claim.

## What makes it sing

- The grammar is two speeds of everything: plates pivot, the
  frame rotates axially, the base travels at two gears. No
  single rhythm can take over, so the eye never settles.
- The perturbation rule is the soul of it. Most reactive work
  is a mirror with a delay. Schoffer's systems audit
  themselves for monotony and intervene. That is the
  difference between a response and a temperament.
- The palette does the legibility work. Flat primaries on
  black steel can be read across a theatre, and a colour
  field can be a sensor input and a picture at the same time.
- Scale ambition without style change: the same brain runs a
  2.6 m sculpture, a 52 m tower and a 300 m civic instrument.
  The idea survives every scale because the idea is the loop,
  not the object.

## Avoid list

- Do not build a reactive piece whose whole behaviour is a
  lookup table from input level to output level. That is the
  "black box" the thesis criticises in the early critics, and
  it reads as a toy within seconds.
- Do not let a self running system settle into a period. If
  the motion can be predicted after ten seconds, it needs its
  own disturbance, not a longer loop.
- Do not claim colour response rules the sources do not
  support. Red calms is documented; the rest of the CYSP 1
  wiring is still an honest gap, and dressing a guess as
  history would break the study rule.
- Do not copy the Mondrian plate look. The transferable part
  is the loop (sense, respond, self perturb), never the flags.
- City scale light spectacle without the feedback is just
  illumination. The towers earn their size by listening.
