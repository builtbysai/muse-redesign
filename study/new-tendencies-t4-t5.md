# New Tendencies: Tendencies 4 (1968/69) and Tendencies 5 (1973): Study Notes

**Profile:** Nove tendencije / New Tendencies, Zagreb, 1961 to 1978.
Five exhibitions organized with the Galerija suvremene umjetnosti
(Gallery of Contemporary Art, today the Museum of Contemporary Art
Zagreb): t1 1961 (concrete and constructive art, ZERO, GRAV, Gruppo
N), t2 1963, t3 1965 (cybernetics and art, Moles already in the
catalog), t4 1968/69 "Computers and Visual Research" (Kompjutori i
vizuelna istraživanja), t5 1973. Key organizers: art historians
Matko Meštrović, Radoslav Putar, Božo Bek, Boris Kelemen; artists
Ivan Picelj and Almir Mavignier, whose chance meeting in Zagreb in
autumn 1960 launched the network. The journal **bit international**
(1968 to 1972, nine numbers) made Zagreb a point of initiation for
information aesthetics and media theory. This study is the capstone
node of the information-aesthetics arc: Bense, Moles, Franke, Nees,
Nake, Kawano, Barbadillo, and Sýkora were all studied before it, and
their next-doorway lists all named Tendencies 4/5 as the political
end of the line. The movement lost its thrust in 1973, ran a t6
symposium "Art and Society" in October 1978, and ended there.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on images for the Bonačić material,
medium on the rest. Read in full: Darko Fritz, "The Work of Vladimir
Bonačić" (Monoskop PDF, the complete illustrated essay); Eric
Engle's "Computer Art in the (Former) Soviet Bloc" (arxiv PDF, read
via text extraction, used for the Bonačić installation facts and the
mama.mi2.hr image leads, its Maoist polemic not adopted); the
New East Archive "Behind the Yugoslav art movement" feature in full
(Fritz interview, the movement's aims and end); Picelj's "Concrete
Utopia" essay, sections I through III (lines 0 to 129 of 387, the
EXAT 51 and programmed-art background); the bibliofil.hr t4 poster
listing (full participant roster, poster description, exhibition
dates). A research subagent read the Monoskop New Tendencies page,
the DADA movement and exhibition entries (t4 1968/69, t5 1973),
Kluetsch's "London/Zagreb" computer-art history PDF, and the
digitalna-umjetnost-u-hrvatskoj.eu Bonačić author page, and returned
verbatim facts with attributions; those facts are marked [subagent]
below. Six images visually inspected at full resolution: four from
the Fritz monograph (oscilloscope loop, twelve-panel dot-pattern
contact sheet, Nama facade night photo with the dot-matrix band,
Ilica street night photo with the vertical light columns), the t4
install view of Benthall before Milojević's plotter sheets, and the
t5 install view of the Zottl/Rase computer graphics wall. Three
local PIL re-renders built and visually inspected: four degree-10
maximal-period rasters, the successive-state strip for x^10+x^3+1,
and a 160-state window of the DIN.PR18 degree-18 sequence.

## The shape of t4: two stages, 189 works

Tendencies 4 ran in two stages. First, an informational exhibition
of computer graphics and programmed works at the Centar za kulturu
i informacije, 2 to 8 August 1968, around the international
colloquium "Computers and Visual Research" of 3 to 4 August 1968.
Then the full program, May to August 1969: "tendencije 4"
(retrospective of NT1 to NT3 plus Latest Examples of Visual
Research, Muzej za umjetnost i obrt), "Computers and Visual
Research" (Galerija suvremene umjetnosti), "typoezija" (Galerija
Studentskog Centra), and an exhibition of publications and books.
An informational seminar followed 10 to 12 January 1969 and an
international symposium 5 to 6 May 1969 [subagent: Monoskop,
Kluetsch]. The computer section showed 189 works.

The roster, from the t4 poster listing [bibliofil.hr]: Kurd
Alsleben and Cord Passow, Ars intermedia (Otto Beckmann, Alfred
Grassl), Vladimir Bonačić, California Computer Products (Kerry
Strand, Larry Jenkins, Doyle Cavin, Dee Hudson, Jane Moon), Compos
68 (Jan Baptist Bedaux, Jeroen Clausman, Arthur Veen), Waldemar
Cordeiro, Charles Csuri, Darel D. Eschbach, William Allan Fetter,
Alan Mark France, David R. Garrison, Jens Harke, Leon D. Harmon
and Kenneth C. Knowlton, Hiroshi Kawano, Auro Lecci, Robert
Mallary, Gustav Metzger (with D. E. Evans, Beverly Rowe, R. J.
Stibbs), Leslie Mezei, Petar Milojević, Frieder Nake, Georg Nees,
A. Michael Noll, Michael Palyka, Ivan Picelj, the Rechenzentrum
of the Boris Kidrič Institute at Vinča, Manfred Robert Schroeder,
Lloyd Quinton Sumner, Alan Sutcliffe, Zdeněk Sýkora, Evan Harris
Walker, Edward Zajec.

Cross-checked against the earlier studies: Nees and Nake present
(both also spoke at the August 1968 colloquium). Kawano present,
showing his 1964 OKITAC 5090A rectangle designs, the family from
the Kawano study, line-printer output hand-colored by assistants
[subagent: DADA]. Sýkora present. Franke present in the movement
but not in the computer show: he opened the accompanying
exhibition at the August 1968 colloquium. Molnár absent from both
t4 and t5 lists. Morellet at t4 only through the retrospective.
Barbadillo and Manfred Mohr absent from t4, both present in the
t5 computer section.

## The information-aesthetics arc inside t4

**bit international 1 (1968), "The Theory of Informations and the
New Aesthetics,"** carried a text by Max Bense alongside Meštrović,
three texts by Moles, and Putar [subagent: Monoskop]. Bense's
contribution to t4 was textual; no source confirms he attended or
lectured in Zagreb, and he is not among the colloquium papers.

**Moles** gave the introductory speech at the 3 to 4 August 1968
colloquium: "...But the machines already approached the man,
faster than the man approached the machines..." [subagent:
bit international 2 via the arxiv/Fritz paper]. After him, Marc
Adrian read "notizen zu t-4," asking whether New Tendencies after
six years "could be considered to be dead." Moles was already in
the t3 (1965) catalog.

**Franke** opened the accompanying exhibition at the colloquium,
published in bit 3 as "Einführung zur Ausstellung
'Computergrafik'." His opening argument [subagent: Kluetsch]: the
computer is capable of three kinds of information processing,
creation of order, transformation of order, and destruction of
order; because evaluating computer art is hard, he proposed a
cybernetic art theory grounded in a scientific theory of
perception. In bit 7 (1971) his "Social aspects of computer art"
imagined households connected by television screens to central
computer units, "as it is nowadays the case with the phones,"
making screen-presented computer graphics possible, "that
possibility seems today utopian."

Leslie Mezei, who did not attend in person, divided computer art
into seven categories for the colloquium: mathematical curves
(Milojević, Sumner, Citron, Whitney, Mason, Mitchell), random
abstract design (Nees, Nake), representational art (Csuri, Mezei),
designs with letters (Mezei), scanned photographs (Knowlton,
Harmon), computer generated movies (Knowlton, Noll), computer
graphics (Fetter), plus a computer-controlled animation stand and
camera (Liang, McLaren) [search-result excerpt of Kluetsch]. The
colloquium committee (Eco, Gerstner, Krampen, Horvat-Pintarić,
Kelemen) closed with the line that would haunt the field: "There
should be no computer art."

## Vladimir Bonačić: the Galois fields

The deepest vein of the session. Bonačić (1938 to 1999), a
research scientist at the Ruđer Bošković Institute, treated art as
an extension of his technological experiments. The organizers
found him at the institute during t4 preparations; he used visual
research in his scientific work on the roots of polynomial
equations, and the finite fields of Évariste Galois became his
instrument [Fritz].

**IRB series (1968 colloquy):** eight computer-generated pictures,
photo reproductions of oscilloscope screenshots, made on a
self-constructed light-pen system hooked to a PDP-8, programmed in
Assembler. "IRB" is the institute; numbers distinguish the works.
One, IRB 9-9, outlines a female figure and was never publicly
exhibited. His Mondrian reference in the texts was a critique of
A. Michael Noll's computer-generated Mondrian-like drawing. Noll
exhibited at t4; Bonačić's position was that the computer "must
not remain merely a tool for the simulation of what exists in a
new form. It should not be used for painting in the way that Piet
Mondrian did."

**PLN series (1969):** visualizations of Galois fields. The exact
language of the work IR. PLNS. 0044. 7714. 7554. 7744: "IR" means
irreducible, "PLN" polynomial, "S" symmetry, and the numbers are
linked to the polynomial properties. Bonačić described it as a
"successive depiction of generating a maximal period in four
irreducible polynomials of the tenth degree, x^10 + x^3 + 1
(0044), 7714, 7554, 7744." The visualized combinations, thousands
or millions depending on the degree, were shown in rasters of
16x16, 32x32, or 64x64 elements; some works were collaged by hand
from multiple screenshots for higher "resolution." PLN 5 through
9 are single photographs with a long-exposure look, achieved
through hardware and software parameters with photographer
Marija Braut. Of RS. PLNS. 0374. 1024. 0064, a two-dimensional
tenth-degree polynomial, he noted "a rare symmetrical structure
in a polynomial of tenth degree (it cannot be expressed by means
of the existing mathematical apparatus) 0374, 1024, 0064.
Symmetry cannot be disrupted by a change of starting number."

**The t4 object (1969), with Ivan Picelj:** front panel a grid of
round aluminum tubes, each holding a small bulb, each tube cut at
an angle. The upper part shows variations of the characters "t4"
moving left to right; the rest of the panel lights in asymmetric
patterns; four knobs on the back allow manipulation.

**DIN.PR18 (1969):** 36 meters on the Nama department store
facade, Eugen Kvaternik Square. Eighteen elements, each a 3x5
grid light matrix, performing 262,143 patterns, the maximal
period of the irreducible 18th-degree polynomial
x^18 + x^5 + x^2 + x + 1, clocked at 200 milliseconds, adjustable
"to the border of the perception of the observer and frequency
clock." The square was dark; the installation doubled as street
lighting. Replaced in 1971 by DIN.PR16, a triple frieze with a
spatial extension around the building. DIN.PR10 (1969 to 1971)
went on the Ilica Street Nama: 100 elements of 88x48x25 cm. A
further object stood on the Kreditna Banka facade on Ban Jelačić
Square. None of the outdoor works survive.

**Random 63 (1969):** 76x76x7 cm electronic object on a PDP-8.
Sixty-three independent true random generators, each driving one
lamp; the geometric bulb placement computed with the
pseudorandomness of Galois fields. His only true-randomness work,
built as a critique: "I am especially sceptical of the attempts
to produce computer art through play with randomness and the
deliberate introduction of errors in programs prepared for
nonartistic purposes" (1974). The Dynamic Objects otherwise all
use pseudorandom Galois-field algebra, programmed on an SDS-930
in Real-Time FORTRAN with Assembler for bit manipulation
(co-programmer Miro A. Cimerman), viewer-adjustable rhythm, and
in the GF.E 32-S object (1969/70, a 32x32 grid of square
aluminum tubes, 1024 bulb-pixels) 32 indicator lights and 32 keys
so that "with the binary notation, any pattern from the sequence
can be read or set." At a 2-second rhythm the same pattern
repeats in about 274 years [subagent].

**The Pollock passage** (his longer statement on randomness):
"the random generator creates the accidental and unique
presentation, which has neither value nor importance for human
beings... However, a computer used in such a way lags far behind
the human being. Even if the expressive potentialities of the
computer were equal to those of a human being, the essence of
Pollock's world and creation would not be surpassed, regardless
of the complexity of future computers or peripheral units. That,
of course, does not mean that a man (or a monkey or other animal)
aided by a computer could not create an aesthetically relevant
object if they act consciously or unconsciously obeying the law
of accident."

## The graphics: Picelj's poster and bit international

Picelj designed the t4 visuals, posters, and catalogue layout,
and the bit international pages. The t4 poster (49.5x97 cm
screenprint, in the MoMA and Tate collections): black ground, a
raster of circles and rings printed in silver-gray, the
exhibition title in Croatian and English below, then four blocks
of informational text separated by vertical lines, all white
[bibliofil.hr description]. The dotted design reads as perforated
computer-programming paper, the punch card as ornament. The
poster itself was not visually inspected this session (see gaps).

## Tendencies 5 (1973): the political end

t5 ran 1 June to 1 July 1973 at the Tehnički muzej (Technical
Museum) Zagreb, in three sections: Constructive Visual Research,
Computer Visual Research, and Conceptual Art [subagent: DADA].
The computer section included Alexanco, Barbadillo, Beckmann,
Bonačić, Cordeiro, Franke, Garrison, Hertlein, Huitric, Lecci,
Mohr, Nees, Rase, Lillian Schwartz, the Seguís, Whitney, Zajec,
Zöttl, and others. The conceptual section: Baldessari, Buren,
Dimitrijević, Gilbert and George, Huebler, Kounellis, Latham,
Sol LeWitt, Paolini, Penone, Trbuljak, the Hungarian Anonymous
Collective, and more. LeWitt's Wall Painting was executed by the
exhibition workforce from a one-sentence textual instruction,
described as a programme, "the algorithm of descriptive
geometry, expressed by text"; On Kawara's "I am still Alive"
arrived as five identical telegrams sent in advance to Putar
[subagent: oris.hr].

The accompanying AICA symposium, 2 June 1973 at the Hotel
Esplanade, was titled "The Rational and Irrational in Visual
Research Today, Match of Ideas." Participants included Argan,
Cordeiro, Franke, Moles, Nake, Milojević, Putar. Putar's t5
catalog framing asked whether the constructive fields were
"exhausted or whether there were sufficient remaining tasks to be
accomplished; and also whether there was a real need for the
solution of these tasks or whether commercial demand was giving
the illusion that efforts of this kind were needed." The
symposium was "a unique place where participants from all three
artist groups and accompanying theoreticians were engaged in an
open dialogue, that was however characterized by mutual
misunderstanding" [subagent: oris.hr].

Nake's break gives the political end its dates. On 16 April 1970
(PAGE 8) he announced "I stop exhibiting for the present": "It
looks as if the capitalist art market is trying to get hold of
computer production." In October 1971 (PAGE 18) he published
"There Should Be No Computer-Art": computer artifacts had not
changed the structure of aesthetic behavior and formed no
separate category of art; artists must not "become silent
servants of the ruling classes by reconciling high technology
with the masses of the poor and suppressed" [subagent]. He then
sat on the t5 symposium.

Fritz's verdict on the stall: "The art world was changed in a
manner they had not foreseen. New types of art were coming into
the world: body art, performance art, land art, conceptual art.
Much of this seemed to be in conflict with what New Tendencies
had proclaimed: a rational approach to art, the idea of art as
research, and the idea of art as collective work. Mystification
had become respectable again." And on t4's place in the arc:
"The fourth Tendencies exhibition (1968/69) was marked by a
further penetration of the idea of a theory of information and
exact aesthetics. The artistic use of computers was a 'last try'
of the Tendencies movement to synchronize its goals as the
'scientification of art' and 'bettering the society' and
historical movement of 1968" [subagent: Fritz via Kluetsch].

## The re-renders: what the polynomials look like

Three PIL sheets, all white dots on black, the oscilloscope
aesthetic of the PLN works.

**Panel A, four polynomials:** four primitive degree-10
polynomials, each maximal output stream (1,023 bits) laid
row-major into a 32x32 raster, one cell left empty. The finding
is Bonačić's own, confirmed on the pixels: the four panels look
genuinely different. One reads blotchy and clustered, one even
and fine, one carries horizontal banding, one dense. "The
different visual appearance of the patterns resulting from the
polynomials that had not been noted before by mathematicians"
holds. The polynomial is the composition.

**Panel B, successive depiction:** all 1,023 states of x^10+x^3+1
as rows of 10 dots. The shift register shows itself as diagonal
drift, the feedback bit entering at the left of each row. The
generation is visible as texture.

**Panel C, DIN.PR18 window:** 160 successive 18-bit states of
x^18+x^5+x^2+x+1 as rows of 18 dots. Reads as woven textile,
diagonal bands from the register shift. Both of Bonačić's own
polynomials verified maximal in code: periods exactly 1,023 and
262,143. At the installation's 200 ms clock the full DIN.PR18
cycle runs 14.6 hours, a number worth printing on the wall label.

Two implementation notes, kept honest. The first LFSR build used
the wrong shift direction and produced period 21 for x^10+x^3+1;
fixed to the standard right-shift Fibonacci form, both
polynomials went maximal. The strip tops show the register
filling from the seed, a transient, not the cycle; the cycle is
what repeats.

## What makes it sing

The t4 computer room's throughline, read off the works: the
machine's output presented as a document of its own process.
Bonačić titles his works with the exact algebra (IR. PLNS. 0044.
7714. 7554. 7744). Milojević pins plotter sheets like lab notes
next to the formulas. Picelj sets the punch card as ornament.
The honesty is structural: nothing is dressed up as more than
the computation, and the computation turns out to be enough.
Bonačić's deeper move is the one to steal: he uses
pseudorandomness with complete deliberateness, a maximal-period
sequence is the opposite of "play with randomness," and his one
true-randomness piece exists to make that point. Determinism,
fully committed, reads as more designed than randomness ever
does.

## Avoid-list additions

Punch-card dot ornament as a period signifier without a
generative rule behind it; true-random scatter presented as
profundity (Bonačić's critique stands: the accidental has
neither value nor importance until someone commits to it);
Mondrian-like computer pastiches, the exact thing he called out
Noll for; shift-register strips shown without acknowledging the
seed transient at the top; calling any maximal sequence
"random," it is the most unrandom thing in the room.
