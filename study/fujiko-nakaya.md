## The technique, stated plainly

Fog, she once noted, is a cloud that has descended to earth. Her father Ukichiro Nakaya, a Hokkaido University physicist, made the first artificial snowflake and founded Iwanami Productions to film science; she grew up inside his weather laboratory. She studied painting at Northwestern (BA 1957), painted clouds after returning to Japan, and hit the limit of the medium: she wanted a more direct, experience-oriented form. Early experiments made dry-ice clouds on a heated plate, chasing temperature difference as a form engine. She joined E.A.T. in 1966. When E.A.T. took the Pepsi Pavilion at Expo '70, the idea of shrouding the dome in fog was on the table early, and journalist Nilo Lindgren reported that no one knew whether it was actually possible to make a fog, but the idea persisted and survived. Nakaya became the person in charge of executing it.

She spent six months gathering statistical weather data on Senri, the Expo site: wind patterns, the open ground broken only by a small hill to the southwest, no windbreaks. She consulted Japanese physicists and meteorologists, including Magono Choji and Higashi Akira at Hokkaido University, both students of her father who had worked with him on wartime fog-dispersal research in the 1940s. Her path crossed her father's at exactly the point where she needed it.

The apparatus that resulted was invented by American cloud physicist Thomas Mee, introduced to Nakaya by Rauschenberg for the pavilion job. In her Getty Recording Artists interview she says she asked whether he could make a pure-water fog, and he came back with a working system within one month; early prototypes were energy hungry, but within weeks she was walking through engineered fog in his Los Angeles backyard on a 105-degree day. The Smithsonian's technical description adds the nozzle internals: pressurized water forced through a tiny orifice (six thousandths of an inch at the tip, on the Fog Bridge spec) strikes an impingement pin, shattering the jet into 15-to-20-micron droplets; the Gazette's Fog x FLO reporting gives 17 microns and filtered water above 1,000 psi. Both Mee (the hardware) and Nakaya (the airflow) patented the system after Expo, per Wikipedia, and the National Gallery of Australia reports a separate 1989 Nakaya patent for making cloud sculptures from water fog. There is one more naming detail worth keeping: the numbers in her titles (Fog Sculpture #47773, Fog Bridge #72494) are the international codes of the nearest weather stations, whose data informs each work's planning (Haus der Kunst). The title is a pointer to the dataset.

Nakaya stated her three requirements for the fog in a 1972 essay, and they read like a spec sheet for the entire 55-year practice. One, visibility: it must scatter enough light to hide what is behind it while making visible the otherwise invisible dynamics of the atmosphere. Two, tangibility: it must feel soft and cool on the skin. Three, vulnerability: it must be subject to atmospheric conditions; it must disappear, not persist. Her one request to Mee, quoted by Furuhata: dense, bubbling-out fog, as close a simulation as possible to natural fog in its physical nature, to cover the entire pavilion, with perhaps dropsize control added to make rain once in a while. The rain request is the tell: she wanted not fog but weather, an adjustable instrument of atmosphere. Her signature formulation of what the medium does has not changed in fifty years: "Fog makes visible things become invisible and invisible things, like wind, become visible."

## The instruments, site by site

Five decades of commissions, and each one is a new instrument tuning, not a repeat. The 1982/83 Canberra work, "Foggy Wake in a Desert: An Ecosphere" (#94925), was built with Kyoto University scientist Yasushi Mitsuta to test what a one-square-kilometer fog would do in desert conditions: 900 nozzles ran daily from 12:30 to 2 pm, climate and ecological changes were recorded for ten years, the site was landscaped to her specifications, and the piece was designed as a self-sustaining ecosphere, thickening foliage and cultivating species. It is still running forty years on. Fog Bridge #72494 at the San Francisco Exploratorium (2013) puts 800 nozzles on a 150-foot pedestrian bridge, ten minutes every half hour, lit at night, and its programmed valves respond to real-time wind data: an east wind triggers fog only on the east side. "Veil" at the Philip Johnson Glass House (2014) wrapped the house in 600 nozzles for 10 to 15 minutes each hour, and the nozzle placement was based on a year of on-site weather-station data sent to Nakaya in Japan. "London Fog" at Tate Modern (2017) added a Ryuichi Sakamoto and Shiro Takatani light-and-soundscape with dancer Min Tanaka performing inside the fog. And in 2002, as a consultant to Diller Scofidio + Renfro on the Blur Building for Swiss Expo 2002, she told the architects their original fog idea was unachievable, and Wikipedia cites the architects: it was her idea about irregular nozzle concentrations that saved the day. Concentration, not just placement. Nozzle density as a second compositional axis.

The compositional grammar is minimal and complete. What the artist controls: the exact position and number of nozzles (260 to 900 in the Boston pieces, hidden in trees, in the ground, behind rocks, up high), the pressure, the duration. Everything else is delegated. "The atmosphere is my mold and the wind is my chisel to sculpt in real time." She studies wind rose diagrams and site notes the way a painter studies light. For Fog x FLO she read Olmsted's own landscape notes for the Emerald Necklace. "Nature does 90 percent of the work and I just help it perform at its best." The 8-minute activation every half hour is the form: a scripted performance the climate improvises. At the Neue Nationalgalerie the fog emerges from selected sides of the garden at intervals, blends with the trees, and dissolves from the center upward, nearly tangible one moment, a translucent veil the next.

She treats fog as a medium for the transmission of light and shadow, "much like video" (her phrase, via the Guggenheim text). The lineage is real: the 1980 Bill Viola collaboration projected laser beams through the fog as an ambient screen; at YCAM's CLOUD FOREST in 2010 she and Shiro Takatani built a special mirror device to redirect actual sunbeams into the fog, with sound design woven through it. Fog is a volumetric display that nature re-renders for free.

## What the photos teach

The 1970 dome photo is the thesis frame. The faceted white dome (a slightly distorted 120-foot-diameter geodesic structure by Tadashi Doi of Takenaka Komuten) surfaces from the fog like an iceberg, the Breer Floats sit on the plaza like grazing animals, one visitor walks through for scale. The fog is densest at the crown where the roofline nozzles vent, cascading down the facets and thinning at the edges. A wind-tracking system distributed the fog over the building's surface (Smithsonian), and it worked almost too well: on the first day the cloud brought out the fire department, who thought the pavilion was burning (Getty). It does not decorate the architecture; it digests it. The most technological building on the Expo grounds read as weather.

The two Bourse de Commerce photos (2026, Cloud #07156) teach the interior grammar. The fog is emitted from floor grates and sits low, a thick opaque blanket rolling across the rotunda floor under Ando's concrete drum and the baroque commerce fresco overhead. In the doorway shot the fog pours through the arch toward the historic wooden doors, and above the door hangs a barometer inscribed PLUIE, VARIABLE, BEAU TEMPS. Rain, variable, fair weather: a 19th-century weather instrument watching over a 21st-century weather machine. The fog makes the building's lowest stratum a place you disappear into while the frescoes carry on above.

The Berlin garden aerial (2026 press photo) is the best evidence of the compositional grammar at work. From above, the fog is not a cloud; it is a river, a distinct white channel running through the walled garden between the trees, with visitors walking in and out of it. You can read the nozzle line in the fog line. The hidden infrastructure is visible too: a small black pump container parked behind the wall, the shipping-container heart the Gazette described in Boston. The black box belongs in the booth, never on a pedestal.

The advection re-render built this session (3 frames, plan view) confirms the grammar is generatively complete. Nozzle placement plus a valve schedule plus a curl-noise wind field plus dissipation produces everything: at t18 the river forms along the nozzle line and the wind already shears it into billows; at t42, just after the valve closes, the fog is at its fullest, the river plus the grove cluster dense and pluming downwind; by t104, sixteen minutes after shutoff, the fog has abandoned the emitter line entirely and exists only as a thin wind-drawn arc the wind drew across the garden. Decomposition is the composition. The strongest frame is the last one, and in it the nozzles are irrelevant: the valve script sets the initial condition, the wind authors the ending.

## The second career (video)

The fog would be a full life's work for anyone; Nakaya also ran a parallel career in Japanese video art that rivals it. Her first video work, "Friends of Minamata Victims: Video Diary" (1972), documented a pollution disaster. With Katsuhiro Yamaguchi she organized Japan's first video-art exhibition at the Sony Building in Ginza in 1972; the collective Video Hiroba formed around that show. She published the first Japanese translation of Michael Shamberg's "Guerilla Television" in 1974, assisted MoMA's 1979 "Video from Tokyo to Fukui and Kyoto," and in 1980 opened Video Gallery SCAN in Harajuku, Japan's first video-art gallery (Bill Viola named it), which ran the Japan International Video Television Festival in 1987, 1989, and 1993. The through-line is not a coincidence: fog treated as a transmission medium for light and shadow, "much like video." She spent the 1980s building galleries for a screen medium and the rest of her life building the medium that has no screen.

## Sound design

The fog pieces are scored environments, not silent ones. David Tudor did the interactive sound for the 1970 pavilion and the YCAM press release explicitly invokes his "Rainforest" as the model for CLOUD FOREST's fog-and-sound interface. The Boston Fog x FLO run wrapped the sculptures in a season of events: brass and percussion playing water-fluidity compositions inside the fog, a story time for small children, an improvised live soundtrack, Macbeth performed in the mist, a lyric soprano singing into it. The pattern is consistent across five decades: the fog is the stage, and the weather it summons includes whatever humans do inside it. Visitors' footsteps, their voices, their bodies displacing the air, are the chance operations.

## What to steal and what to avoid

Steal: the three requirements as a spec for any environmental piece. Visibility (hide the object, show the system), tangibility (the body must be able to enter the work), vulnerability (it must disappear). Any procedural work that satisfies all three will feel like weather; most generative art satisfies none of them. Steal: the delegated-authorship split, stated as a percentage. Name what the system controls and what the environment controls, and let the environment have the larger share. The 90/10 split is the aesthetic. Steal: the valve schedule as form. A work that only exists in 8-minute windows, separated by long silences, makes presence the medium. The countdown between activations is part of the piece. Steal: nozzle density as a second axis. The Blur Building save says compositional density gradients matter as much as placement: sparse where the wind does the work, dense where the object needs weight. Steal: fog as a light instrument. Any volumetric medium, real or simulated, becomes a screen the moment light crosses it; lasers through fog is the cheap ancestor of every volumetric display. Steal: the title as a data pointer. Naming a work after the weather station whose records composed it, instead of after the artist's feelings, is a cleaner kind of authorship.

Avoid: fog as decoration. The concert-smoke trap, where atmosphere is applied to flatter a weak source, is the exact inverse of her practice. Her fog reveals; decoration conceals. Avoid: the permanent-sculpture instinct. "Both a phenomenon and an artifact," she says of F.O.G., and the phenomenon is the point; a fog sculpture you can buy as an object is a contradiction she has spent 55 years refusing. Avoid: controlling the wind. The one failure mode visible in the documentation is any installation where the nozzles fight the site instead of reading it. The six months of weather data came first; the nozzles came second.
