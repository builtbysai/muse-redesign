# Franz von Lenbach, the brown glaze and the serial sitter (1836-1904)

Deep study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as
the next doorway by BOTH the 2026-10-06 Weber Portfolio study and the
2026-10-06 Franz von Stuck study, which left "Franz von Lenbach as the
senior Malerfuerst" as an open doorway. The quality-first choice:
after the Stuck study established the oversize figure straining
against the frame on a committed flat ground, Lenbach is the senior
master of the other grammar, the one the lineage has been circling
since Velazquez: the head emerging from a dark worked ground, painted
eighty to a hundred times. He is also the honest limit case for the
quality doctrine. His brown glaze is a real compositional discipline,
and it is also a filter that nearly killed him. Both things are true,
and the study has to carry both.

## Sources

Biography, technique, the portrait business, key works with
holdings, criticism, the Weber/Kubin/Stuck lineage, conflicts, and
gaps via a delegated research dossier (text-only pass, zero em
dashes; all eight visual inspections and both re-render families are
mine). Key facts, carried:

- Biography: born 13 December 1836, Schrobenhausen, a master
  mason's son. Munich Academy from January 1854, Hermann
  Anschuetz's technical class 1856, Karl Theodor von Piloty 1857.
  Rome August-November 1858 on a Glaspalast travel scholarship.
  Professor at the new Weimar art school June 1860 to April 1862,
  about a year and a half, left on his own account saying he had
  more to learn than to teach. Copyist years 1863-1868 on Adolf
  Friedrich von Schack's guaranteed salary, copying Titian,
  Rubens, Velazquez, and Murillo in Italy and Spain; his last
  landscape, the Alhambra, 1868. Munich breakthrough at the 1869
  Glaspalast international exhibition, gold medal against Courbet,
  Corot, Millet, and Daubigny. Villa Lenbach built 1887-1890 with
  Gabriel von Seidl in Tuscan palazzo style, steam heating, a
  power generator, studio daylight. Ennobled 1882. Stroke 12
  October 1902. Died 6 May 1904 in the villa. Widow Lolo sold the
  villa to Munich in 1924; it opened as the Staedtische Galerie im
  Lenbachhaus on 1 May 1929.
- Technique: dark warm grounds and glaze layering through the
  mid-1890s, German sources naming "braeunliche Galerietoene",
  brownish gallery tones, as the characteristic. Around 1897 a
  documented turn: brighter colors, thinner paint, alla prima
  instead of glaze, faster and lighter brushwork, the late
  self-portraits painted this way. Photography the central
  technical fact of the late practice: by the 1890s projecting
  diapositives onto canvas and tracing them was his standard
  method, working with the Munich photographer Carl Hahn, the
  portrait painted without the model present and often composited
  from several photographs; the Leo XIII of 1885 was the famous
  first case, the Pope having no time to sit. Studio assistants,
  and in 1893-95 a forgery scandal: an employee stole discarded
  sketches, dealers had poor students color them in, some forged
  the signature. The exact ground recipe and the "Rembrandt
  varnish" as a named medium are unverified here; the brown glaze
  is documented as an observed style, not a proprietary medium.
- The portrait business: met Bismarck 1874, regular family guest
  from 1879. The Bismarck count conflicts across sources: about
  80 (Britannica and several), about 80 paintings through 1897
  plus masses of sketches (Thieme-Becker), about 100 (German
  History in Documents). The 1879 portrait bought 1880 by the
  Berlin National Gallery, destroyed in World War II. From 1890,
  after the dismissal that outraged Lenbach, increasingly in
  uniform: the cuirassier portraits. Reproductions of the
  Bismarcks hung in bourgeois living rooms across Germany. By
  1900 the best-paid portrait painter in the German and
  Austrian-Hungarian empires. Per-portrait fees unverified.
- Criticism: the "Muenchner Malerfuerst" title is his
  chronologically, ennobled 1882; the Lenbachhaus itself says
  Stuck emulated Lenbach's painter-prince model "under more modern
  auspices." The "brown sauce" jibe cannot be pinned to
  Meier-Graefe by name in this session; verified attackers are
  Adolf Bayersdorfer 1873 ("academicism and theater, archaisms
  and phrases," "arrogant showy chic"), Anselm Feuerbach 1873
  (the pictures look like "old restored pictures, far too
  deliberate"), and Karl Stauffer-Bern 1887 (enormously gifted,
  become thoroughly trivial; the later works lean toward virtuoso
  display, "since he only has to do with emperors, kings and
  popes, he lacks the time for serious work"). The 1911 Britannica
  verdict is admiration: "painted many of the most remarkable
  personages of his time." Reputation ebbed after the 1905-06
  memorial shows, the Nazis revived him for the 1936 centenary,
  scholarly rehabilitation only from the late 1960s; no wholesale
  modern reassessment of the paintings' quality is documented.
- Lineage: Hans von Weber 1872-1924 is definitively the 1903
  Kubin portfolio publisher; A. Paul Weber, born 1893, was ten
  and cannot be the publisher. The Lenbachhaus catalogs it as
  the "Hans-von-Weber-Mappe," so the dual-attribution puzzle
  from the Weber study resolves cleanly, no puzzle remains.
  No documented Lenbach-Kubin contact found; the Lenbachhaus
  now holds both worlds, Lenbach's villa and the Kubin holdings
  from the Blue Rider collection. Stuck was never Lenbach's
  student; Lenbach painted Stuck's portrait in 1892 and Mary
  Lindpaintner as Salome in 1894 (Neue Pinakothek); Stuck
  co-founded the Munich Secession in 1892 in direct response to
  the exhibition policies of the Kuenstlergenossenschaft that
  Lenbach presided over. Succession and rivalry between
  generations, not a master line.

## Visual inspections (8 works at source resolution)

1. Bismarck 1884, Alte Nationalgalerie (2899x4100): the civilian
   Bismarck in a black frock coat, three-quarter turn, the head
   high at upper right, the white shirt and stock at the neck the
   one crisp pale incident. The ground is near-black to every
   edge. Signature "F Lenbach" bottom right. In the face crop the
   face is warm ochre and pink with visible brushwork, the eye
   crisp, and the cheek simply ends where the black begins; the
   ear half-lost in the ground. In the ground crop the ground is
   essentially flat black with faint speckle, no modulation worth
   naming. This is the species at its strictest.
2. Bismarck 1890, in Halberstadt cuirassier uniform, Lenbachhaus
   (2000x2467): signed and dated top left, "F. Lenbach
   Friedrichsruh 1890." Same face, now in the white uniform with
   the pale orange sash and spiked helmet. The light moved: the
   uniform carries it, the ground stays dark. The face stays the
   constant. The costume does the meaning.
3. Bismarck 1890, Walters (1200x1668): oil on panel, the same
   cuirassier sitting as no. 2, warmer gray ground, the uniform
   the great pale mass, the orange sash and the medals the small
   hot accents, signature bottom left. Pairing 2 and 3: the serial
   species in miniature, one sitting, two canvases, the ground
   value the only variable.
4. Self-portrait 1903 (2037x2589): the bearded old painter with
   glasses, holding the palette, seated inside a painted oval
   within the rectangle. The face and the white beard are the
   pale mass, everything else near-black. The oval is a format
   decision, not a spotlight: the canvas shape itself declares
   the container. Late work, and it follows the 1897
   alla-prima report: the handling is sketchier than the 1884
   Bismarck.
5. Contadinella bavarese 1860 (1962x2346, museum photo cropped of
   its frame): the early control. A Bavarian peasant girl in a
   RED bodice and white blouse, seated, signature "Lenbach 1860"
   bottom right, against a dark ground. The palette is still
   full: red is allowed. The mature Lenbach killed the red and
   forced every sitter into his brown. This picture is the
   before, and it proves the brown was a decision, not a
   limitation.
6. Richard Wagner 1895 (3485x4752): bust, white collar, signed
   "F. Lenbach" top right. The face the pale mass, the body
   dissolving into near-black, the white collar the one crisp
   accent. The formula at its most reduced: head plus collar.
7. Kaiser Wilhelm I 1888 (1439x1925): seated, dark uniform, red
   collar, medals, white mustache, signature bottom right. The
   red collar is the one hot accent, attached to the figure,
   exactly the Stuck-study rule. The background carries a red
   drape at right, worked, not flat: the ground is allowed to be
   modulated when it stays in the hue family.
8. Papst Leo XIII, Lenbachhaus L 262 (1607x2000): the photo-template
   portrait. White zucchetto, white collar, white cassock: the
   pale mass is the costume itself, the red mozzetta the hot
   accent, the ground a worked brown with a red drape at right.
   The face in the detail crop is built from loose sketchy
   strokes, almost caricature-warm. The critics count it among
   his finest, and it is the one painted without the model
   present. The template plus the glaze, nothing more.
9. Portrait of a Young Woman, Indianapolis (4703x5891), signed
   "F Lenbach 1893": the reverse species. The ground is PALE
   ochre, worked in horizontal strokes, and the dark-haired head
   emerges from the light instead of the dark. Same discipline,
   inverted key. The value key is free per composition, as the
   Redon, Velazquez, and Goya studies found; what is NOT free is
   the rationing: the light is always rationed, the ground
   always committed.

## Re-render families (PIL, single pass, visually inspected)

Family A, the portrait grammar. A1: dark ground committed to
every edge, head high, white collar the crisp incident, body
dissolving into the ground, is the species. A2: the centered
headshot with margins and rendered shoulders is the emblem
failure. A3: the vignette spotlight ground is the named-filter
failure, the ground explaining the light instead of committing
to the dark. A4: the full-palette modeled figure on a mid-tone
ground, the 1860 control, is the before-Lenbach failure.
Findings: the head must sit high, never centered; the ground
must commit to the dark edge to edge, a spotlight ground turns
the portrait into a stage prop; the white incident must be
crisp and small, one collar or one stock, never a costume.

Family B, the serial species. B1: eight heads, one sitter, one
scale, one placement, varying only in turn and ground value,
is the species, the 80 Bismarcks as a format. B2: eight
different sitters is the inventory failure. B3: one head on
eight different grounds is the variety-pack failure, the series
is about the sitter, not the ground. B4: random scales and
placements is the no-format failure. Findings: the format
constant is what makes repetition into a study rather than a
catalog; the repetition IS the portrait.

## Quality thesis

The brown glaze is a value discipline, not a mood. One head
placed high, the face the only fully worked thing, one crisp
white incident, the body dissolved into a ground that commits
to its value edge to edge. The palette narrowed to one hue
family, and the narrowing is the point: the early contadinella
proves he could paint red, and he chose not to. Every sitter,
Bismarck, Wagner, Wilhelm, the Pope, gets painted in the
painter's color, not their own. The Velazquez study found that
the hue family must be the figure's own; Lenbach breaks that
rule on purpose, and it works because the house color is
applied with total consistency. The house style IS the
composition.

The serial sitter is the second finding. Eighty to a hundred
Bismarcks is not a habit, it is the portrait. The 1884 civilian
and the 1890 cuirassier teach by adjacent contrast: the face
constant, the costume doing the meaning, the ground value the
only other variable. A generative series that repeats one
procedural face with a constant format and varies only costume,
turn, and ground value is working in his medium.

The honest limit, carried because the quality doctrine demands
it: the formula is also a filter. Stauffer-Bern's 1887 verdict
stands on the evidence of the late canvases: virtuoso display,
too much salon manner, too little self-criticism. The 1897 turn
to brighter alla prima is the counter-move, and it reads as a
man trying to get out from under his own glaze. A derivative
piece that only applies the brown filter has learned the
manner and missed the discipline; a derivative that copies the
rationing, the high head, the one white incident, and the
serial repetition has learned the discipline and can drop the
brown entirely.

## Avoid list

- the vignette spotlight ground as his darkness
- the centered headshot with even margins
- the full-palette modeled figure presented as mature Lenbach
- the "brown sauce" jibe attributed to Meier-Graefe
- the Rembrandt-varnish medium stated as a documented recipe
- the Bismarck count stated without the 80 vs 100 conflict
- the 1903 Weber portfolio misattributed to A. Paul Weber
- Stuck named as Lenbach's student
- Lenbach named as a Munich Academy professor
- the brown glaze used as a filter without the rationing
  discipline (the head high, the one white incident, the
  committed ground)

## Honest gaps

No original handled. Color balance unverified on all eight
scans; the 1884 ANG Bismarck reads darker than the original
likely is. Only a fraction of the Bismarck series inspected,
and the full 80-to-100 set was never seen together. The ground
recipe and the photogravure publishers unverified, per the
dossier. Liszt, Wagner, and Moltke public holdings unverified.
Dossier gaps carried. Schematics single-pass and crude.
