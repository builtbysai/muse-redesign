# Felicien Rops, the small board delegated and the large board reworked (Les Diaboliques, 1883-1892)

Deep study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as
the next doorway by the 2026-10-06 Felicien Rops study. The
quality-first choice: the small/large Diaboliques pair is the only
study in the whole lineage where the SAME artist remade the SAME
compositions twice, once as a delegated commission and once as his
own project, and his own verdict on the difference survives: "les
petites planches ne disent rien," the small boards say nothing. What
his hand changed is directly visible on the sheets.

## The two series, what they are

The Diaboliques are not one edition but two series of nine plates
each, constantly confused in the literature, which is why the dating
keeps wobbling between 1882, 1884, and 1886. Alric Delaporte's 2014
master's thesis (Paris 1, drawn from the Rops museum archives in
Namur) is the one document that disentangles them, and this study
follows it. Correction to the 2026-10-06 Rops study: it was Rops
himself, not Lemerre, who delegated the small boards to the engraver
Leon Evely, and even the large boards were engraved by Evely first,
with Rops reworking them personally afterward. The delegation story
is messier and more interesting than "Lemerre delegated the work."

The plates: frontispice Le Sphinx, six story plates (Le Rideau
Cramoisi, Le Plus bel Amour de Don Juan, Le dessous de cartes d'une
partie de whist, A un diner d'athees, Le Bonheur dans le Crime, La
Vengeance d'une femme), and the two-version postface La femme et la
folie dominant le monde I and II. The Barbey d'Aurevilly portrait
was engraved by Paul Rajon, not Rops, because Rops's caricatural
portrait of Barbey could not match the classic facture of Lemerre's
author frontispieces.

Small series (commissioned): drawings ordered 20 November 1883 per
Rops's letter to Peladan, delivered in dribs through September 1885
(the ninth plate, the second postface culispice, added 4 September
1885 as a make-good for the lateness), engraved by Evely, published
February 1886 by Lemerre. Format about 12 x 9 cm, "Lemerre format"
paper. The margin carries the full apparatus: title, "Felicien Rops
del. & sc.," "A. Lemerre Ed.," "Imp. A. Salmon." Delaporte shows the
"del. & sc." is a fiction: Rouir's technical examination found no
trace of Rops's retouching on the first states, and the Rops-Evely
letters show Rops sending Evely the drawings to engrave from June
1884, then complaining in September 1885 about the deplorable state
of the plates, all while reassuring Lemerre "not to be frightened by
certain brutalities." Evely was led to believe his plates were
reproductions, not the final state.

Large series (his own): announced by Rops in April 1886, two months
after the small series appeared, with the verdict that the small
boards say nothing. Exhibited once, at the Salon des XX in February
1889. The engraving was Evely's again, per Rouir's hypothesis built
on the September-October 1888 copper-theft letters ("there is only
you who engraved the Diaboliques in large format"), but the
retouching was Rops's own, pursued with Armand Rassenfosse through
the early 1890s, developing the "undetectable" soft-ground varnish
the two men were inventing together. Rops's April 1891 letter: "Il y
a deux editions de Diaboliques une, la petite, chez Lemerre. Puis la
grande en train de se faire. ... La Vengeance de femme n'est pas
encore faite ni le Vol & la Prostitution dominant le monde." The
large series was still unfinished in 1891, and Rouir holds that the
large La Vengeance was never completed. Ramiro's 1887 catalogue
lists only the small series plus one large Sphinx; the 1893
supplement lists six large plates. Lemerre had no part in the large
series and may not have known it existed; his 1894 debt claim of
3100 francs against Rops may be the shadow of the fraudulent large
edition sale.

Also in the shadows: Rops sold proofs of the small series at Drouot
in May 1885 before publication, without Lemerre's knowledge, and
used a boy engraver, Louis Legrand, to "mass" (model) the drawings,
a collaboration he then furiously disavowed. The whole production is
a paper trail of delegation, deception, and debt.

## Technique

Small boards: heliogravure, pure or slightly retouched, reproducing
Rops's drawings. Line-and-crosshatch register on white paper, the
titles drawn in the sky of the plate (lettered in the image itself,
like the Sataniques) with the publication apparatus in the margin.

Large boards: heliogravure transfer engraved by Evely, then reworked
by Rops with soft-ground (vernis mou), drypoint, and aquatint. The
Rassenfosse collaboration produced a soft-ground varnish retouch
"undetectable" by the standard examination, which is exactly why
Rouir's authenticity criteria misfire on them. The visual register
is the reversal of the small boards: full-bleed tonal ground, the
figure reserved pale, line dissolved into grain. The title stays
inside the plate, lettered faintly in the field; the signature is
Rops's own, written in the plate. No Lemerre, no Salmon, no margin
apparatus: the plate is a self-contained object.

## What was inspected

Nine small boards, the complete 1886 series, from the Mu.Zee uploads
on Wikimedia Commons (~1400x2000 each): Le Sphinx (frontispice), the
six story plates, and both postface versions. Seven large-board
impressions for comparison: six Rijksmuseum sheets (PK.MP.11371 Le
rideau cramoisi, PK.MP.11343 Le bonheur dans le crime, PK.MP.11347 A
un diner d'athees, PK.MP.11363 Le dessous de cartes d'une partie de
whist, PK.MP.11346 La vengeance d'une femme, PK.MP.11349 La femme et
la folie dominant le monde) and the KBR Don Juan large board
(23.5 x 16.2 cm, etching, drypoint, aquatint), plus LACMA
impressions of A un diner d'athees and Le Sphinx for cross-check.

Plate-area tonal measurements (crops of the image area, not the
sheet): the numbers confirm what the eye sees, with a caveat.
Small boards run pale: Rideau 123.2, Bonheur 132.2, Diner 104.8,
Whist 148.9, Postface II 142.5, Vengeance 123.2, standard deviation
36-49, dark fraction 0.06-0.35. Large boards run lower and wider in
feel but the means overlap (Rideau 124.4, Bonheur 128.3, Diner
130.1, Whist 120.8, Postface 172.2, Vengeance 110.9) because the
difference is textural, not metrical: the small boards are line on
white (high local contrast, white reserves), the large boards are
grain on a continuous ground (the whole field carries tone). Mean
gray flattens the distinction; the discriminator is whether white
exists as paper or as the palest part of the field.

## What makes the large boards good, pair by pair

Le Rideau Cramoisi: the largest change. Small board: vertical, a
curtain fills the left half, a pale woman lies diagonally across a
dark right field, small reaching hands at the bottom, everything
legible. Large board: HORIZONTAL, the woman now floats upright in a
dark mist, the curtain dissolving into tonal haze, the composition
rebuilt around her as a spectral presence rather than a scene. Rops
did not just re-register the drawing; he recomposed it for the
tonal medium.

Le Bonheur dans le Crime: the most faithful carry-over. Small:
lovers bound to a pillar with a Medusa head, a ghost woman at the
bottom, radiating line sky, "LE BONHEUR DANS LE CRIME" lettered
across the top. Large: the same composition, but the radiating lines
become a real tonal night, the pillar and bodies modeled in light
on dark, signed "Felicien Rops" in the plate. The finding: the
composition survives translation, but only because the light logic
is rewritten, light as reserve in a dark field instead of line on
white.

A un diner d'athees: same scene in both (dead woman sprawled on a
table, candle, the coffin lid diagonal, boots below), but the small
board is a hard linear rendering and the large board is a nocturne:
soft-ground grain, the title lettered in the dark field above the
scene, the candle the one punctual light. This is the pair where
"say nothing" reads most clearly: the small board is a crime-scene
illustration; the large board is the night after.

Le dessous de cartes d'une partie de whist: tall black-clad woman,
hands clasped at her mouth, skull and cards at her feet, on a
full soft-ground dark field. The small board's version is linear and
explanatory; the large board's is the species the Sataniques study
identified, the committed tonal ground with one pale figure, the
title faint in the field.

Le plus bel amour de Don Juan (KBR large board): full-bleed grain,
the pale girl center-left, Don Juan's ghost looming in the dark
behind her, the title barely legible in the upper field, Rops's
signature in the plate. No hard contour anywhere; the figures
dissolve into the ground they came from.

La femme et la folie dominant le monde / La Vengeance d'une femme:
the large Vengeance turns out to EXIST as an impression, and this
session can say which file it is. The Commons upload "De wraak van
een vrouw, objectno PK.MP.11346.jpg" is a linear hatched proof, all
diagonal lines, no tonal ground, an early state of the engraving.
The other Commons upload, "Felicien Rops - La vengeance d'une
femme," is the finished large board: full soft-ground tonal ground,
the woman in the top hat with the candle, and Rops's own signature
in red pencil in the margin below the plate. That red-pencil
signature is the mark of his own issue, the same gesture as the
Sataniques signatures. The cataloguers' dispute (Rouir's "never
finished" vs the nine-plate completeness) is about the edition as a
publication; the plate itself exists in the tonal register, signed
by the man. The postface pair shows the same register logic: tonal
field, pale woman, faint in-plate title.

The quality thesis: the large boards work because they are the
diametrical opposite of the commission conditions. The small boards
were fast, linear, legible, margin-apparatus illustrations for
somebody else's book, engraved by somebody else's hand. The large
boards were slow (1886 to at least 1892), tonal, illegible on
purpose, self-contained, signed by the man, and made for no
publisher. What his hand changed was the register: he dissolved the
line he was famous for into the grain of the ground. The
"undetectable retouch" is not just a technical anecdote; it is the
aesthetic. The retouching is invisible because the whole point was
that the plate should read as a night, not as a drawing.

## Quality notes

What makes this work good, decision by decision:
- The small boards carry the DRAWING; the large boards carry the
  NIGHT. The drawing is the commission's property; the night is
  Rops's. A derivative that copies the small boards gets
  illustrations; a derivative that copies the large boards gets
  the compositional species: full-bleed tonal field, one pale
  figure, faint title inside the plate.
- The title inside the plate, written faint, is load-bearing. The
  small boards keep the title in the margin with the publisher's
  apparatus, and the picture stays a page illustration. On the
  large boards the title written in the field makes the plate a
  self-contained object, like the Sataniques signature in red
  pencil. A derivative with no title is a mood; a derivative with
  a bold title is a poster.
- The ground on the large boards never rests at mid-tone. It runs
  deep everywhere or it fails into the overcast. The pale figure is
  the one event. This is the same discipline the Kubin and Redon
  studies found, now executed in grain instead of line.
- The best large board (Rideau) is the one he recomposed, not
  translated. The faithful ones (Bonheur, Diner) are good; the
  recomposed one is great. Compositional loyalty to the source
  drawing is a ceiling.

What would make a derivative fail:
- Line illustration with a tonal wash laid over it. The species is
  grain all the way down; a line drawing with a dark filter on top
  reads exactly as what it is.
- Mid-tone everything with one vignette. The large boards are not
  vignettes; the dark runs to every edge.
- The bold in-plate title. Faint or nothing; the letters are part
  of the weather, not a label.
- Copying the small boards' compositions and calling it Rops. The
  small boards are Evely's engraving of Rops's drawings, and Rops
  himself said they say nothing. The thing to learn from is the
  rework, not the source.

## The delegation question, honestly

The comfortable story, "Rops was a genius betrayed by delegation,"
does not survive the letters. Rops delegated the small boards
himself, deceived his engraver about their status, deceived his
publisher about their completion, and sold proofs on the side. Then
he spent six years reworking the large boards, partly with a friend
inventing new soft-ground formulas, while running up debts his
publisher may have been punishing. The large boards are better art,
but the story of their making is not the romantic one. The honest
quality reading: the small boards say nothing not because Evely's
hand was inferior but because the CONDITIONS were wrong, fast,
linear, legible, for a book he had no stake in. The large boards
are his because he gave them the conditions his work needed: time,
darkness, tone, no margin apparatus, nobody else's name on them.
The lesson for the pieces: the register you give a piece is a
condition, not a style choice, and the wrong condition will silence
any hand, even your own.

The one contemporary who saw the distinction exactly was Huysmans,
writing in Certains (1889), reprinted in the 1896 La Plume special
number: "Je ne parle pas ici, bien entendu, de ces eaux-fortes si
miserablement reduites pour les besoins du tirage de l'edition
Lemerre, mais bien des originaux, des vernis mous, dans lesquels
l'artiste a pu, en se liberant de la minutie d'un format de poche,
travailler sur de valables planches." He was not speaking of the
etchings miserably reduced for the Lemerre edition's print run, but
of the originals, the soft-grounds, in which the artist, freed from
the fussiness of a pocket format, could work on worthy plates.
Mirbeau, announcing the publication in Le Matin on 19 February 1886,
gave the verdict the whole century kept repeating: "Felicien Rops
n'illustre pas, il fait oeuvre a cote d'une oeuvre" (he does not
illustrate, he makes a work beside the work).

## Avoid-list additions

- Small-board compositions presented as Rops's own work: they are
  Evely's engravings, and Rops's own verdict stands.
- The "Lemerre delegated the engraving" story: it was Rops. (The
  Musset plates were the Lemerre delegation; the Diaboliques were
  Rops's.)
- Dating the large series to 1886: the large series ran 1886 to at
  least 1892, probably unfinished.
- Treating the two series as one work: they are a commission and a
  reclamation, different objects with different authors' hands in
  them.
- The bold title, the mid-tone ground, the vignette as night.
- Barbey as collaborator: he never met Rops, never saw the drawings
  until October 1885, disliked them, and the illustrations were
  sold separately from the book, never bound in.

  Exsteens numbers were not pinned down in the dossier pass.
- "Le Vol & la Prostitution dominant le monde," the postface
  variant Rops mentioned in 1891: Exsteens says Pellet's
  reproduction is from an original drawing, not from the postface
  plate. Not located this session.
- The exact division of labor on the large boards (which passages
  are Evely's engraving, which Rops's retouch) is unknowable from
  the Rassenfosse "undetectable" varnish, which is the point of it.
  The comparison in this study is between editions, not between
  hands on one plate.
- The Louvre RF 40087 Sphinx and the LACMA impressions were located
  but not fully compared plate-state by plate-state.
- The 1889 Salon des XX showing: Delaporte and Rouir cite it, but
  it is not settled whether the exhibited series was small or
  large.
- Re-render families are single-pass schematics with crude figure
  glyphs; they test the register structure, not the drawing.

## Re-render families (single pass, inspected)

Family A, the tonal rework: A1 (species) is full-bleed dark grain
with the pale reserved figure and the faint in-plate title. A2 (the
overcast failure) keeps the same composition on a shallow mid-tone
ground and reads as unfinished, not night. A3 (the illustration
failure) keeps the dark ground but draws the figure as a hard
outline, and the outline collapses the atmosphere back into a
diagram. A4 is the comparison anchor, the small board in line
hatching on white. Finding: the ground must commit, the figure must
be reserved not outlined, and line-on-white is a different species,
not a lesser one.

Family B, the title inside the field: B1 (species) is the faint
title written in the dark field. B2 (the commission failure) puts
the title in a white margin below the plate with publisher credits,
and the plate immediately becomes an illustration. B3 (the poster
failure) writes the title bold inside, and the letters stop being
weather. B4 (the floating failure) drops the title entirely, and
the composition loses the anchor that makes it an object. Finding:
the faint in-plate title is what makes the large board a thing
instead of a page.
