# On Kawara: Study Notes

**Profile:** On Kawara (Kariya, Japan, 1933, to New York City, July 10,
2014). The other 1966-68 New York pole in the LeWitt/Hesse/Martin triangle,
and the first-named doorway from the Agnes Martin study: her repetition
discipline set against his date discipline. Settled in SoHo in 1965 and
never left, refused interviews, refused to be photographed, and spent 48
years registering his own existence. The best-known move: from January 4,
1966, the Today series of date paintings, nearly 3,000 of them in 112
cities, a project planned to end only with his death. Alongside it the
information series: I Got Up (1968-1979, picture postcards rubber-stamped
with his rising time, sent every day from wherever he was), I Went (a red
line drawn on a city map each day tracing where he walked), I Met (lists
of names), I Am Still Alive (telegrams to friends, 1970 into the 2000s),
One Million Years (1969, the two-million-year reading project), the One
Hundred Years Calendars (yellow, green and red dots marking lived days,
painting days, and multi-painting days), and 48 journals with a paint
swatch, a sequence number and a size letter for every date painting.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on five artworks at source full resolution,
two local re-render studies built and inspected. Read end to end:
Wikipedia (full biography, all series, the birth-date discrepancy in the
100 Years Calendar), Anne Rorimer's 1991 Art Institute of Chicago essay
"The Date Paintings of On Kawara" via the emilyfuhrman.co PDF (Jan 4 1966
first painting, more than 1,900 canvases by 1991, the hand-drawn
"stenciled" lettering, four to five ground coats and six to seven date
coats, the day-against-night color reading, the subtitle history from
headlines to weekdays, the box and newspaper as the painting's
cross-reference to flux), the Ikon Gallery "Eternal Return" guide (four
coats rubbed down between layers, outlines drawn then filled with tapered
brushes, ruler, set square, blade and dusting brush; registered in the
journal; stored in a handmade box; seven years of making time total), the
theartsdesk Guggenheim "Silence" review (Gill Sans early, Futura later;
the country-by-country date formulas; Esperanto where the alphabet is not
Roman; the refusal to write in Japanese), the David Zwirner 2012 "Date
Painting(s) in New York and 136 Other Cities" page (the road paintings
all five by seven inches, new paintings added during the show, the
subtitle convention "Sunday" through "Saturday" in the work titles).
Visually inspected: JUNE 8,1966 (blue, early elongated sans, Apollo
Magazine reproduction); JULY16,1969 (the Apollo 11 painting, near-black
ground, David Zwirner install photo, punctuation visible: no spaces,
period after the day); the Zwirner 2012 installation (the big painting
flanked by small road paintings); the Nov 14, 1972 I Got Up postcard
(Brooklyn Bridge front; rubber-stamped back: "I GOT UP AT 10.24 A.M.",
addressed to Jean Ferrero, Nice); the February 1970 I Am Still Alive
Western Union telegram to Sol LeWitt (pasted ticker-tape strips, 1970
FEB 11 postmark, LeWitt's signed receipt); I Went, April 28, 1977 (thin
red route on a Xeroxed Manhattan map, his stamped date at the bottom).
NOT seen: any original in person, any of the 48 journals or the One
Hundred Years Calendars, the I Got Up / I Went / I Met / I Read books,
the full subtitle corpus, a live One Million Years reading.

## The core finding: the grammar is the composition

Everything about the Today series looks like one idea repeated, and the
re-render study proved the opposite. Study A put eight documented date
formulas on one contact sheet: "JUNE 8,1966", "JULY16,1969",
"NOV. 8, 1981", "15 AGO. 68" (Mexico City), "13 JUIN 2006" (Monte Carlo),
"26. AG. 1995" (Reykjavik), "6 AUG. 1993" (Tokyo, Esperanto),
"24. Marz 1976" (Germany). At a glance the sheet reads as one painting
eight times. On reading, every punctuation choice, every abbreviation,
every word order differs. The date formula of the country where he stood
is the composition. The background color, which viewers notice first, is
the day's least deliberate decision: Rorimer shows it is explicitly
"unspecified with regard to particular associations," and subjectivity
and objectivity of selection cancel out, so that a red is as meaningful
as a gray. What is strictly ruled is the grammar. The instrument is a
localization engine run by hand.

The hand is real and load-bearing. The lettering gives the impression of
having been stenciled, but every character is drawn: four or five coats
of Liquitex for the ground, each rubbed down before the next, then the
outlines meticulously drawn and filled in white with tapered brushes, a
ruler, a set square, a blade, and a brush for dusting. Six or seven coats
of white for the date alone, and "a considerable amount of time spent
eliminating imperfections." The early lettering is an elongated
Gill-Sans-like sans; the later is Futura, quintessentially modernist. In
the study-A sheet the difference reads immediately: the early ones are
delicate, the later monumental. Rorimer's line on the typeface choice is
worth keeping: subjectively chosen by the artist, "not determined by an
objectively definable rationale or system." The rules are strict and
the taste is personal. That combination is the whole practice.

The midnight rule is the generator, and the calendar is the real drawing.
"A work must be started and completed on the actual day of its date" is
the only production system he imposes; anything unfinished by midnight
is destroyed. Study B rendered one synthetic year in the One Hundred
Years Calendar species (yellow for a lived day, green for a painting
day, red for more than one) and the finding is structural: the painting
days cluster into travel streaks and discipline runs, the gaps sit there
load-bearing. A year with no gaps would read as compulsion, not
discipline. The 3,000 paintings are a survival record. The dates on the
canvases are the instances; the dot calendar is the composition.

## Work by work

### The Today series: Today, "Sunday" to "Saturday"

Eight predetermined sizes, 8x10 inches to 61x89 (the Apollo paintings of
July 16, 20 and 21, 1969 are the three largest, made while the world
watched the moon landing). Letters and numerals scaled to the canvas,
centered laterally. The subtitle practice is the series' quiet history:
early paintings carry long quoted headlines from the day's newspaper
("Man Walks on Moon" for JULY 20, 1969; the Long Tieng withdrawal text
for MAR. 18, 1970), and after 1971 the subtitles collapse to the day of
the week. The Zwirner titles show the late convention: "NOV. 8, 1981
Sunday", "APR. 7, 1986 Monday", "SEPT. 29, 1982 Wednesday". The trajectory
is the finding: the world shrinks out of the work on purpose, until the
date refers to nothing but its own position in the week, and the
newspaper in the box carries the world instead.

Each painting, when not shown, lives in a handmade cardboard box sized to
it, with a clipping from a local newspaper of that day and city pasted
inside the lid. Rorimer: the box "affirms the painting's definition as
an object in its own right," while the newspaper grounds the date "in
the world of constant flux and continuing events." The painting and the
news are cross-referenced but never grafted together, the cubists'
newspaper integrated into the picture plane, Kawara's kept physically
separate with the line between art and nonart drawn clean.

### I Got Up (1968-1979)

Two picture postcards a day from wherever he was, the front a tourist
view of the city, the back rubber-stamped: "I GOT UP AT", the time,
the date. The Nov 14, 1972 card in the visual set is addressed to Jean
Ferrero in Nice; the stamps are gray-blue, the address in his hand. The
technique is the instrument: the personal content (his handwriting,
his addressee) is confined to the address block, and the message is
struck by rubber, repeatable, unvarying. The irregularity lives one
level up, in where he woke: 28 cities in 1973 alone. The card is a
fixed form filled by a wandering life. The stamp is the composition;
the city is the variable.

### I Am Still Alive (1970 onward)

Telegrams to friends bearing the three words, plus his name. The
February 1970 Western Union to Sol LeWitt is pasted ticker-tape strips
on the company form, "I AM STILL ALIVE / ON KAWARA.", with the 1970 FEB
11 postmark and LeWitt's signature acknowledging receipt. The medium is
the whole technique: the telegram is the most expensive, most formal,
most public way to say the least. Three words cost real money to send
and arrive as an event. The period after KAWARA is the only punctuation
in the entire practice, and it lands like a hammer.

### I Went (1968 onward)

A Xeroxed city map each day with the day's route drawn in thin red ink.
The April 28, 1977 sheet: Manhattan, one long red line up the east side
corridor with a small loop at the bottom, "APR 28 1977" stamped below in
his lettering. The line is tentative, unpracticed, honest. This is the
counterweight to the date paintings' perfection: the hand is allowed to
be a hand here, and the map supplies the order. Two instruments, one
practice: the map walks and the painting sits still.

### One Million Years (1969)

Two volumes listing every year for the million years before the work's
conception (dedicated "to all of those who have lived and died") and
the million years after (dedicated "to the last one"). Read aloud in
pairs, male and female voices, in performances that run for days: the
longest public reading at documenta 11 in 2002 ran the full hundred
days; Trafalgar Square in 2004 ran seven days and seven nights; the
Hessischer Rundfunk radio version is 32 CDs. The finding for generative
practice: enumeration is trivial, but reading it aloud is not. The
voice turns the list into duration. The Dia Center's 1993 reading put
the sound of the future years against the sight of the past years on
the page, and the date paintings hung nearby as the middle term.

### The journals and the One Hundred Years Calendars

48 journals, each painting registered with its size, color and date,
with a swatch of the actual paint mixture glued in, a sequence number
for the year and a size letter beneath. The calendars mark each day of
his life with a yellow dot, a green dot for a painting day, a red dot
for a day with more than one. The obituaries honored the system by
using it: they reported he was alive for 29,771 days rather than giving
birth and death dates. The calendar even corrects the record: it gives
his birth as December 24, 1932, not the official January 2, 1933.

## The two re-renders

Both built in PIL and visually inspected (renders kept with the session
evidence, not shipped).

**Study A: the date grammar.** A contact sheet of eight documented
inscriptions on dark grounds. Finding: the sheet reads as one idea until
you read it, then every grammar differs. The grammar is the composition;
the color is the day. The elongated early lettering versus the Futura
late lettering is a second, visible parameter: same instrument, two
type eras.

**Study B: the midnight rule.** A synthetic year page in the calendar
species. Finding: the travel clusters, the summer gap, the November
streak, the rare red day. The gaps are load-bearing. The calendar is the
real drawing; each painting is one green dot, expanded.

## What makes it sing

- The localization engine run by hand: the date formula of the country
  where he stood is the composition, researched and painted fresh every
  day, never templated.
- The midnight rule: destroy what is not finished. The corpus is a
  survival record, and the gaps are part of the work.
- The hand inside the machine: stenciled look, hand-drawn reality. Six
  coats of white, imperfections hunted with a blade. The discipline is
  visible only because the finish is.
- The physical separation of art and world: painting in the box, news in
  the lid. Cross-referenced, never grafted.
- The subtitles' deliberate emptying: headlines, then personal notes,
  then only the weekday. The world leaves the painting and the box
  keeps it.
- The telegram economy: three words, maximum formality, one period.

## Avoid list additions

- Kawara-style date paintings presented as new work. That is his
  signature, not a technique to borrow. The studies derive the
  instrument, never the look.
- Machine-perfect date text with no grammar research and no midnight
  rule behind it. The perfection without the practice is decoration.
- Fake headline subtitles chosen for irony. His newspapers are a boxed
  physical practice, not captions.
- Esperanto as a quirk. It is a rule: where the alphabet is not Roman.
- The "still alive" line used as a slogan. It cost a telegram each time.

## Honest gaps

- No original handled; every work from a web reproduction.
- The 48 journals, the One Hundred Years Calendars, and the I Got Up /
  I Went / I Met / I Read books never seen firsthand.
- The subtitle history follows Rorimer and the Zwirner catalogue notes;
  the full subtitle corpus was not reviewed.
- The Gill Sans to Futura reading follows museum labels and
  photographs, not measured letterforms.
- Series counts (nearly 3,000 paintings, 63 to 241 per year, 1,500
  postcards, the 68 cards to Sesteer) follow Wikipedia and the
  Boijmans record, not the catalogue raisonne.
- No live One Million Years reading attended; the documenta and
  Trafalgar Square accounts are secondary.
