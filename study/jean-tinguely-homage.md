**Date:** 2026-09-28 (study pass)
**Work:** Homage to New York, self-constructing and self-destroying machine, performed once, MoMA Sculpture Garden, evening of March 17, 1960, 6:30 to 7:00 P.M.

## The technique, stated plainly

Homage is not a sculpture that happens to self-destruct. It is a timed relay score wearing a junk sculpture as a costume. Kluever and Bell Labs technician Harold Hodges built special step relays that closed eight electrical power circuits at successive intervals across 27 minutes. The circuit list, from Kluever's account via Frohnert: play the piano, turn on the radio, activate the chain saw, cut the playing radio in half, start one of Tinguely's metamatic drawing machines, run an Addressograph machine, set off the smoke chemistry, and the fireworks. Every "event" in every eyewitness account maps to a relay firing. The composition is the gate schedule.

The failure mechanics were engineered with the same care as the motion. Hodges embedded resistors in Wood's metal (a low melting point alloy) so that closing a circuit would melt the piano's supports and drop it on cue. An overheated resistor lit a candle sitting on the piano keyboard. The machine poured gasoline on itself. None of this is failure of the work. Kluever's doctrine, worth quoting because it is the whole aesthetic: "as a scientific experiment can never fail, this experiment in art could never fail." And to a New Yorker writer: "All good machines destroy themselves." The malfunction narrative that the Tate pack repeats (nearly two hours, fire department intervention) is the tourist version. Kluever's own words: the thing was over in twenty-seven minutes, the audience applauded, then descended on the wreckage for souvenirs.

## What the photos teach

Everything is white, and that is a visibility strategy, not a style choice. The event ran at night in March; white-painted junk against black sky is the only way 250 guests in slush can read the motion. The white unifies a bathtub, a piano, a bassinet, bottles, a toilet, an American flag, bicycle wheels, and a weather balloon into one tonal field, so the eye reads silhouette and gesture instead of inventory.

Wheels are the dominant motif. In both inspected photos the machine is a wheel cluster: at least a dozen bicycle wheels at every orientation, some spinning, some structural. Tinguely's circular-motion vocabulary carries over directly from the metamatics. The balloon floats at the visual apex on a taut tether, a huge pale circle that rhymes with the wheels and gives the eye one quiet shape against the clatter. The assemblage is wide and shallow, a frontal wall about 23 feet long, built to be read by a crowd from one side.

Mid-performance details visible in the wide shot: real flame at the machine's center, a long twisted ribbon or scroll hanging, partial painted lettering on a panel, the child's go-kart at the base (it scurried in front of the sculpture), smoke rising in a column. In the close shot: the "Yin is Yang" scroll caught mid-unfurl, its text legible.

## The event sequence, as documented

Pieced together from Canaday's NYT review, McCray, and Kluever:

- The piano mechanically played three forlorn notes.
- Smoke from ammonia and titanium tetrachloride mixed in a bassinet drifted toward the audience.
- A resistor lit the candle on the piano's keyboard. A radio turned on.
- The machine poured gasoline on itself. A scroll reading "Yin is Yang" unfurled.
- The smoke turned yellow and the piano caught fire.
- Exactly seven minutes in (Frohnert's timing), Robert Rauschenberg's mascot contribution fired: a gunpowder-charged money-throwing machine scattered silver dollars into the crowd.
- The chain saw cut the playing radio in half.
- The piano collapsed (Breer gave it a helping shove when the solder was slow).
- Guests picked through the wreckage for souvenirs; the remains went back to the dump. MoMA kept one fragment, later shown in the 2017 Rauschenberg exhibition.

The guest-artist circuit matters: Rauschenberg's Money Thrower makes the collaboration itself a relay gate in the score. Kluever watched Rauschenberg get pulled into the process and wrote that he thought the machine was "so real, so interesting, so complicated, so vulnerable, and so lovely as life itself." This is the seed of Experiments in Art and Technology (1966), of 9 Evenings, of the whole artist-engineer wave. The line from Homage to E.A.T. is direct and documented: same engineer, same collaborators, same doctrine that the collaboration is the experiment.

## Sound design

The piece is a noise composition, not just a visual one. Piano, radio broadcast, chain saw, gunpowder, breaking glass bottles with noxious chemicals, the audience murmur. Tinguely's cruelest sonic joke is the chain saw cutting the radio in half mid-broadcast: a machine silencing a mass medium on a timer. Sound was an event on the score, with an off switch built into the score.

## Smaller machines escape

Kluever's account includes a detail the summaries drop: smaller machines shot out of the sculpture and ran into the audience. The machine has satellites. This is the same gesture as Kanayama's radio-controlled paint car leaving the canvas edge in 1957: the mobile element that crosses the boundary between work and witness. Worth tracking as a lineage beat, not a coincidence.

## What to take, what to avoid

Take: the relay-ladder structure. A time-based piece where the composition is a schedule of circuit closures, each gate triggering a device, some of which attack the other devices. Take the whiteness as visibility engineering. Take the guest circuit, a collaborator's device wired into your score at a fixed minute. Take the satellite machines that leave the work and enter the audience.

Avoid: the malfunction myth. "It broke" is the laziest reading and it is wrong; the interesting version is the engineered one. Avoid spectacle-for-spectacle framing. What sings here is not that it exploded but that the explosion had a score, a conductor, and a doctrine behind it. Avoid the souvenir ending as mere anecdote: the audience dismantling the work for keepsakes completes the composition. The work's final state is distributed across 250 coat pockets. That is a designed outcome, not an accident.
