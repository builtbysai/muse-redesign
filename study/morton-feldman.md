# Morton Feldman: Study Notes

**Profile:** Morton Feldman (Queens, January 12, 1926, to Buffalo, September 3, 1987). Named as next doorway by the 2026-10-03 Erik Satie study, and earlier by the 2026-10-03 John Cage study. The quality-first choice after the chance-protocol and smallness studies: where Cage removed intention and Satie removed ambition, Feldman removed rhetoric. He composed as if music were a surface you could walk across: quiet, slow, gridded, and repeating almost. The study question was why his quietness reads as discipline rather than timidity, and why his repetitions feel like memory rather than pattern.

**Depth:** deep on text, four artworks visually inspected at source resolution, two local re-render families built and inspected, single pass each (both held on first inspection). ## The core finding: strict container, free content

Every quality claim below reduces to one sentence. Feldman put all of the strictness into the container and all of the freedom into the content, and never mixed them up. In the Projections the time boxes are exact, the thirds are exact, the number of attacks is exact, and the pitch is free. In the late works the staves, the durations, and the near-silent dynamics are exact, and the patterns are stitched by hand. His freedom is never the freedom to do anything. It is the freedom to choose the one uncomposed thing inside a composed frame. A derivative that randomizes everything has the mechanism backwards. Randomize the frame and you get scatter. Randomize nothing and you get wallpaper. He randomized exactly one thing.

## Quality notes: what makes his work good, and what would make a derivative fail

1. **Time is exact, content is free.** The Projection page proved it at a glance. Every dotted box is the same width because every box is the same four pulses. The thirds are drawn, the lanes are labeled, the event squares sit where they sit. And then: any tone within the range may be sounded. The re-render proved the division of labor. The species (A1): fixed dotted boxes, sparse small squares, flat dynamics. The same events with no boxes (A3) are scatter, a diagram, not a score. The grid is not a background. It is the composition, and the freedom only reads as freedom because the time is exact.

2. **The empty boxes are composed.** Look at Figure 1 again: most boxes hold nothing. This is not padding between events. The paper notes that the hand-written date at the end of the scores (every Projection but the fifth) carries real musical semantics: it means no empty measures follow. That is how seriously the emptiness is taken. A derivative that fills the quiet boxes to keep things interesting is doing the opposite of the piece. The emptiness is the frame, and the frame is exact. Compare Satie's count: both men made the container carry the meaning so the content could stay small.

3. **Quiet is structural, not timid.** The re-render's expressive control (A4) proved this by failing. Growing event sizes plus a crescendo wedge turn the same grid into rhetoric, a piece with a vested interest, and it stops being Feldman instantly. His dynamics are equal and very quiet, attacks blunted, because he wanted "sound coming from nowhere." The flat surface is the point, the way a Rothko is frozen and vibrating at once. A derivative that adds one swell "for shape" has imported the one thing the whole practice exists to refuse. Loudness is not emphasis here. It is a category error.

4. **Almost-repeat keeps memory awake.** This is the whole late practice. The re-render proved the three regimes. Mechanical repeat (B1) is wallpaper: the eye files it and leaves. Random variation (B3) is noise: nothing is promised, so nothing is missed. The species (B2): the diamonds repeat, recognizably, and each repeat carries one small deliberate weaving error, a halved diamond, a shifted shade, an insertion on one side only. The eye catches that something is off and has to look again. That second look is the piece. "The one force commands uniformity while the other continually pulls this uniformity apart." The Berlin streets passage says it plainly: all the buildings look alike, even if they are not. A derivative with exact loops is decoration. A derivative with random loops is static. The repeat must be recognizable enough to engage memory and wrong enough to disorient it.

5. **Abrash: one color, many shades.** The most useful technical idea in the study. Rural rugs get an apparently extensive color-scale from yarn dyed in small quantities: the same red, in slightly different shades, reads as a whole palette. His aural equivalent: enharmonic spellings, minute rhythmic variations, subtle articulation changes on essentially simple devices. The lesson for generative work is a palette rule. Do not reach for more hues. Take one hue and vary it by degrees, and the field will look richer than a rainbow. A derivative that varies everything varies nothing. The constraint is what makes the variation visible.

6. **Scale, not form.** "Up to one hour you think about form. But after an hour and a half it is scale." And: "The decision as to where the outer edge is, is final, not initial." He did not decide the duration and fill it. He worked until the piece had become its size, the way a painter finds that a canvas is becoming ten by ten. Duration is material, not container. For generative work the translation is direct: a piece that runs long must earn its length by becoming, not by looping. A loop that fills time is form pretending to be scale.

7. **The unwritten rules are the piece.** The Dickinson anecdote is the load-bearing story. Nothing in the instructions forbade the pianist's minor triad. Feldman rebuked her anyway, and his written reply is four conditions that all reduce to: did you hear it. The score deliberately under-specifies, and the aesthetic is carried by judgment, not by rules. This is the opposite of a generator with parameters. Any generative piece in this species needs a taste gate: a human pass, or an explicit aesthetic filter, that rejects the triad. The system alone will always play the triad eventually. He knew it, and he sat in the room.

8. **Surface over construction, between categories.** "I prefer to think of my work as: between categories. Between Time and Space. Between painting and music. Between the music's construction, and its surface." "My obsession with surface is the subject of my music." "I am not a clockmaker." "I am interested in getting to Time in its unstructured existence... how this wild beast lives in the jungle not in the zoo." Do not build a machine that demonstrates its cleverness. Prime the canvas with an overall hue and let time be. The construction should be inaudible and the surface should be everything. If the viewer can see the system, the system is too loud.

## Avoid-list additions

- Sparse quiet grids with no exact time container (scatter is not a time canvas; the dotted boxes are the composition).
- Exact repetition presented as Feldman-like (the repeat must never land; mechanical repeat is wallpaper).
- Random variation presented as his indeterminacy (his freedom lived inside strict frames and was policed by unwritten taste rules).
- Crescendos, swells, or dramatic gestures inside a quiet piece (rhetoric breaks the surface; loudness is a category error).
- Minimalist pulse pastiches (his patterns are not Glass or Reich pulse; he despised the label, and the difference is the point).
- Rug, craft, or textile signifiers as decoration (the rugs taught him abrash and crippled symmetry as methods, not texture as a look).
- The garment-district biography or the comfortable chair as branding (he worked a day job until 47; biography is not material).
- Filling a long duration because the piece is long (scale is found while working, never a container to fill).
- Demonstrating the system (if the viewer can see the mechanism, it is too loud; the surface is the subject).
