# Robert Ryman: Study Notes

**Profile:** Robert Ryman (Nashville, Tennessee, May 30, 1930, to New York
City, February 8, 2019). The other white of the Coenties Slip generation,
named first among next doorways by the 2026-10-03 On Kawara study and by
the 2026-10-02 Agnes Martin study ("the other white: facture as the whole
content"). The territory break the queue needed: after a month of black
ink, grids, typewriter type and date discipline, the strictest white
painting practice in American art, which turns out to be about everything
except white. Arrived in New York in 1953 intending to be a jazz
saxophonist (lessons with Lennie Tristano, army reserve band in Korea),
took a day job as a security guard at the Museum of Modern Art to make
ends meet, stayed 1953 to 1960, and worked shifts alongside Sol LeWitt
and Dan Flavin. Watched Rothko, de Kooning, Still, Pollock and Newman go
up new, bought art supplies at a local store, started painting in his
apartment in 1953. First professional work: Untitled (Orange Painting),
1955-59. Married Lucy Lippard 1961 (son Ethan, born 1964), divorced,
married the artist Merrill Wagner 1969 (sons Cordy and Will Ryman, both
artists). Preferred "realist" to minimalist, because he was not making
illusions: "I am not a picture painter. I work with real light and
space, and since real light is an important aspect of the paintings, it
always presents some problems." His most quoted sentence: "There is
never any question of what to paint, only how to paint."

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on seven artworks at source full resolution,
two local re-render families built and inspected. Read end to end:
Wikipedia (full biography and work sections); Robert Storr's "Simple
Gifts" essay and the catalogue entries in the 1993 MoMA/Tate retrospective
catalogue via the full MoMA PDF (the Winsor build, the Standards and
Generals series, Classico, the support and fastener lists, the signature
and naming passages, the LeWitt/Flavin guard-shift history); the Dia
Beacon Ryman laminate (the "getting the paint across" doctrine, "What is
done with paint is the essence of all painting," "What painting is, is

## The core finding: the ground is the composition

Every Ryman painting looks like a study of white paint, and the re-render
proved the opposite twice. Study A rebuilt the 1966 Winsor species in
two passes. Pass 1: naive full-coverage white rows, no gaps. It reads as
nothing, wall paint, a blank page. Pass 2: close-knit strokes with the
warm brown linen showing between every stroke, the margin all around,
bristle-track striations inside each stroke, clotted edges, thick crests
at overlaps, occasional skip patches. That one reads instantly as a
Ryman, and the finding is structural: the white is the minority content
and the ground is the composition. Storr's catalogue sentence says it in
prose: the paintings result from "the tiered addition of strokes pulled
across the canvas in close-knit formation, leaving the warm brown of the
canvas showing between the strokes, all around the margin, and wherever
the paint skips." Study B rebuilt Classico 5. Pass 1: a regular grid of
white rectangles on cream paper. Spreadsheet. Pass 2: the same grid with
gloss blocks tilted off the sheet axes, laid-paper wobble on the block
edges, tape-removal notches of bare paper at the sheet corners, the
sheets visibly abutted rather than joined. The composition is the
nonconcentric nesting, the wobble, and the evidence of the tape. The
white is only, as he said, the means of exposing other elements.

## The generative doctrine hiding in the catalogue

Storr's sentence on the Winsors is the closest thing to a generative
manifesto in the whole conceptual-generation run of this study program:
"In the 'Winsors' Ryman sets his course, monitors the distribution of
pigment and discovers in the brush tracks the structure of concentration
and the fascination of random incident. All the works in the series have
the same design; each depends for its identity on minor shifts in
painterly emphasis." One design, many instances, identity in the
variance: that is a seeded series, executed by hand, with the
distribution of pigment as the only free parameter. Pair it with his
comparison to Stella, who had the same black-painting procedure in 1959:
"Stella tries to make things happen to painting; Ryman paints in order
to see things happen." And the rule that makes the variance honest: in
the Standards of 1967, thirteen four-foot steel squares loosely swabbed
with enamel, he painted some fifty sheets and destroyed the failures:
"when errors occurred or the sweep of the brush lost its intensity, he
destroyed the painting and started again." A generative series with a
survival record, exactly the Kawara rule from the 2026-10-03 study,
applied to one brushstroke instead of one day. Storr's cello comparison
for the Standards is the technique: "intended modulations of tone result
from relaxing or increasing that pressure," like a cellist sustaining a
note, "the overall feel of paintings like the 'Standards' depends upon
such fluctuating exactitude." And the one-traversal rule: even when the
enamel is opaque, "the onetime-only traversal of the surface is
essential to the painting's effect." No undo, no second pass, the stroke
budget is the piece.

## The support as filter, the material code

The Dia laminate gives the support doctrine in one quoted line: "The
gray of the steel comes through; the brown of the corrugated paper comes
through; the linen comes through, the cotton, all of those things are
considered." The support is not a background, it "filters and modifies
the hue." His support list, from the catalogue, reads like a hardware
store inventory: newsprint, gauze, Chemex coffee-filter paper, Kraft
paper, wallpaper, wax paper, tracing paper, Bristol board, corrugated
cardboard, handmade rag papers, cotton duck, linen, jute, Featherboard,
plywood, hollowcore panels, Plexiglas, Mylar, vinyl, Acrylivin, Gator
board, fibreplate, fibreglass mesh, honeycomb fibreglass panels,
Lumasite, anodized aluminium, cold-rolled steel, copper, plaster walls.
The early jute painting (Untitled, 1959, inspected) makes the principle
physical: "I cut it open. That's why there's stitching here, because it
was part of the way it was sewn together... So when I was painting it, I
used the seam as part of the structure of the composition." And the
material-code sentence, which belongs in the print-voice practice as a
standing rule: "Every material, Ryman argues, has 'a built-in code', or
way of reacting, that suits or illsuits it for a given purpose. The
choice of media thus preconditions aesthetic opportunity." The
composition begins before the mark, in the choice of what the mark is
laid on.

## The Generals: the painting as a membrane

Fifteen paintings, 1970, from 55 inches square down to 44 in half-inch
decrements. Each: a bright white square surrounded by a margin of dull
exposed primer exactly the width of the 3.5-inch wooden stretcher bars,
one unsanded enamel coat over four sanded ones. "Fully symmetrical,
although not precision-ruled, the paintings have neither tops nor
bottoms, neither left nor right sides." Every canvas substitutable for
any other, each "incorporates the same contrast of absorbency and
reflectiveness and thereby functions in the same efficient manner as a
light-sensitive membrane." The painting as a sensor, a light instrument,
not a picture. Surface Veil I (1970, inspected at 1243x1280) is the
membrane at its thinnest: loose white brushed over fibreglass, faint
blue chalk undermarks still visible through the paint, the pigment
forming what the catalogue calls a skin over the support, its
disruptions marking the literal pauses between working sessions. The
orange painting inspected (1955-59) is the same discipline before the
white: an entirely orange field of impasto, with the brown ground
showing at the top edge, no composition, only the built-up surface and
the support edge.

## Fasteners as punctuation, line as channel

From 1976 he mounted paintings with designed fasteners, custom-made per
piece by a local metal fabricator, and the catalogue treats them as
composition: "emphatically real points of contact between painting figure
and environmental ground," "a bridge to the wall, and back." In Embassy
I (1976) they are "spatial punctuation marks"; in the Plexiglas
drawings they are joined by fine pencil lines. The fasteners' physical
details are color elements: "the bluish edges of the vinyl straps,"
"the shape of a screw's head," "the natural tint of metal," "the kind of
paint with which they have been retouched." The 1976 painting inspected
(white sheet pinned by four dark screws at its corners, paint nearly
vanishing into the wall) is the fastener as the only drawing on the
surface. Expander (1983): four screws placed halfway between center and
rim, deliberately not on the square's major coordinates, "gently
destabilizes this most stable of shapes," and "the accumulation of those
imaginary lines creates a template, or grid," a Mondrian with the bars
erased and only the marginal marks left. Administrator (1983): blank
white interior, thin black border, asymmetrically spaced screw heads, the
viewer traces armatures that are not there. And the line doctrine,
stated against Newman: "lines only serve their own end, which is to
travel from here to there... line is not a spatial artifice for defining
the areas that fall on its either side, but an actual space within which
a given material has been channelled." The signature follows the same
rule: "I used the signature as a line, and I generally put it up the
side or on the end, just to make it more abstract so that it would read
more as a line and not so much as my name... I used my name because that
was an accepted element of all painting." A signature is a material that
happens to be letters. He preferred "naming" to "titling": the names
come from supply brands (Classico, Lugano, Surface Veil, Winsor),
trucking firms (Allied), steel suppliers (Capitol), hardware stores
(Acme), lumber companies (General). The name is an inventory label, and
it never describes the painting.

## What makes it sing, and what is overdone

What sings: the confidence that one variable is enough. He ran the same
white for sixty years and every room of the retrospective is different,
because the variable was never the color, it was the support, the
stroke, the fastener, the light. The Generals membrane idea is the
deepest: the painting as a light-sensitive instrument with no image on
it. The fastener-as-punctuation is the most borrowable move for the
print-voice practice: the thing that holds the work up is part of the
work, and its exact shape and tint are compositional decisions, not
hardware. Overdone: the white itself, divorced from the material
discipline, is now a gallery shorthand for "serious painting," and any
white-on-white field without a ground showing through is decoration in
his clothes. Also overdone: monochrome-as-concept, where the single
color is the whole statement; for Ryman the single color is precisely
what frees attention for everything else. The danger for our practice is
making white squares that are about whiteness, which is the one reading
he explicitly refused: "The white is just a means of exposing other
elements of the painting."

## Avoid-list additions

- White-on-white fields where no ground shows through and no support
  discipline is visible: decoration in Ryman's clothes.
- "Minimalist" stroke grids as a style badge: his stroke fields are a
  brush-load and ground-tooth study, not a look.
- Fasteners, brackets or mounts treated as neutral hardware in a piece
  that claims the Ryman lineage: for him the mount was the drawing.
- Machine-perfect white squares with no material code: the built-in code
  of the medium is the composition.
- Naming pieces with descriptive titles while invoking his naming
  practice: his names are inventory labels, and they never describe.
- The signature as branding: his signature is a line, placed like any
  other material.
