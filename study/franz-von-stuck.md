# Franz von Stuck, the oversize figure and the hard step (1889-1895)

Deep study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as
the next doorway by BOTH the 2026-10-06 Rops Diaboliques study and the
2026-10-06 Alfred Kubin study. One honest correction up front, from
the dossier: the Kubin study's premise of a documented 1898-99 Munich
print-room encounter in which Kubin named Stuck does NOT check out.
The canonical encounter story in Kubin's own autobiography names
Klinger, not Stuck; the Deutsche Biographie's Kubin influence list
runs Bosch, Brueghel, Goya, Rops, Klinger, Redon, Ensor, with no
Stuck. What is documented is Stuck's presence in Kubin's milieu: the
2025 Art Newspaper review of the Kubin catalogue "The Aesthetics of
Evil" reports Natalie Lettner naming Stuck among the influences
shaping Kubin's early imagery. So the doorway stands, but as a milieu
line, not an encounter line. The quality-first choice regardless:
after a long run of print studies, Stuck is the lineage's first
painter, and he turns out to be a compositional species the studies
had not seen. Where Kubin made his giants stand among tiny affected
figures and Ensor made his subjects small inside a weather, Stuck
makes ONE figure BIGGER THAN THE FRAME and sets it on a ground that
commits to a single flat decision. The figure strains against the
container; the ground never explains anything.

## Sources

Biography, technique, publishing facts, key works with holdings, the
Kubin line, criticism, conflicts, and gaps via a delegated research
dossier (text-only pass; all seven visual inspections and both
re-render families are mine). Key facts, carried:

- Biography: born 23 February 1863, Tettenweis, Lower Bavaria, a
  miller's son. Munich from 1878; Munich Academy 1881-85, with
  Wilhelm von Lindenschmit the Younger confirmed as a teacher by
  the Staedel (Hackl and Seitz come from the standard biography,
  not independently re-verified). Self-supporting from his teens
  on commercial drawing; Fliegende Blaetter illustrations
  1887-92 and the Jugend cover work made his first reputation.
  Co-founded the Munich Secession in 1892. Royal professorship
  1893, teaching at the Academy from 1895 for over two decades;
  students included Klee, Kandinsky (both briefly), Purrmann,
  and Albers. Married the American widow Mary Lindpaintner in
  1897; designed and built the Villa Stuck 1897-98 as a
  Gesamtkunstwerk. Ennobled December 1905, registered 1906, the
  two dates split across sources. Died 30 August 1928 in Munich;
  his funeral address called him "the last prince of art of
  Munich's great days."
- Technique: oil and tempera, often mixed (the Pinakothek's
  Kaempfende Faune is "Mischtechnik auf Leinwand"; the Frye
  Suende is Syntonos tempera on canvas; late panels are tempera
  on wood). An 1894 review already noted his "new water-soluble
  paint technique" judged as oil "because they look like oil
  paintings." He worked from photographic studies of posed
  figures (Birgit Jooss's 1996 volume documents this), which is
  why the bodies read as staged relief. The 1911 Britannica's
  verdict is the key period line: his style is "based on a
  thorough mastery of design, and... sculptural rather than
  pictorial." The modeled face against a strong dark outline
  flattening the body is his illustrator's training showing
  through. The frame was part of the composition: gilt carving,
  panels, and inscriptions made for each major painting, the
  1893 Suende frame carrying DIE SUENDE (other versions DIE
  SVENDE with a V-E ligature) across an architectural
  temple-form front, and his own Villa Stuck Suende enshrined
  in a marble altar of his design.
- Key works: Der Waechter des Paradieses 1889 (oil, 250 x 167
  cm, Glaspalast gold medal 1889, now Museum Villa Stuck); Die
  wilde Jagd c. 1889 (oil, 53 x 84 cm, Lenbachhaus G 1405; the
  Lenbachhaus page says "um 1888"); Luzifer 1890 (oil, 161 x
  152.5 cm, bought 1891 by Ferdinand I of Bulgaria, now the
  National Gallery, Sofia); Kaempfende Faune 1889
  (Mischtechnik, 85.8 x 148.5 cm, Pinakothek 11867); Pieta 1891
  (Staedel SG 99); Die Suende 1893 (oil, 94.5 x 59.5 cm,
  Pinakothek 7925, Chicago gold medal 1893; eleven versions in
  total, four missing); Der Kuss der Sphinx 1895 (oil, 160 x
  144.8 cm, Museum of Fine Arts Budapest; reproductions were
  ordered out of Munich gallery windows by police). He was also
  a printmaker: the Luzifer and Kaempfende Faune etchings
  c. 1889-90 circulated the motifs widely.
- Criticism: the "Malerfuerst" (painter prince) title is real
  in the secondary literature, but Franz von Lenbach held the
  senior claim; Stuck inherited into it. No specific
  Meier-Graefe attack on Stuck could be verified, only the
  structural conflict of Meier-Graefe's French canon against
  Munich symbolism. Postwar reassessment runs through
  exhibitions: Trento 2006-07, Munich 2008-09, the Belvedere's
  "Sin and Secession" 2016, and the Villa Stuck reopening in
  October 2025 after a 13.5 million euro renovation, with the
  current scholarly line studying him as a strategist of his
  own fame. On the Nazi period, handled honestly: he died in
  1928, so there is no accommodation question; two solid facts
  show the regime's taste found his heroic idiom congenial (a
  Stuck portrait earmarked for the planned Linz Fuehrermuseum,
  looted 1945, withdrawn from a 2017 Cologne auction; a bronze
  Kaempfende Amazone that stood at Goering's Carinhall). The
  "Hitler's favorite painter" line is an unverified book claim;
  no Degenerate Art exhibition appearance was found.

## Visual inspections (7)

All from Wikimedia Commons source scans, inspected at full
resolution. No original handled; color balance unverified on all
seven.

1. Die Suende (1893), in the original frame. The file is the
   shrine, not just the painting: a temple-form golden frame with
   fluted pilasters, a carved foliate inner molding, and DIE SUENDE
   lettered on the plinth between two roundels. Inside, the picture
   is almost all dark: a pale female torso rising from a black
   serpentine hair mass, the face a pale frontal oval dissolving at
   every edge into the dark. The serpent coils at the left, barely
   legible. The face crop shows the rendering concentrated on the
   oval alone; the contours give up into the hair. The frame does
   half the compositional work: the picture is an object of
   veneration, and the title is architecture, not a label.

2. Luzifer (1890), National Gallery Sofia scan at 4700x4961. A
   single crouching male figure filling the frame, the head near the
   top edge, the knees and feet dissolving into black at the bottom.
   The eyes are two blank white ovals staring straight out; the eye
   crop shows the gaze is the only crisp thing in the picture. One
   finger raised to the lips, the shushing gesture, the one loaded
   limb. The body is modeled in gray-green half-light; everything
   below the waist gives up into the dark. The figure is frontal
   and confrontational, oversize for its container.

3. Der Kuss der Sphinx (1895), Google Art Project at 4194x4693.
   Two interlocked bodies cascade down the left side on a diagonal,
   from the sphinx's arched back at top to the man's dangling foot
   at bottom. The ground is two flat blocks: blazing red-orange
   above, near-black below, the step between them hard and
   horizontal. The bodies carry all the modeling; the ground carries
   none. The man's outstretched arm runs up along the diagonal,
   aiming into the red. The bodies crop at the left edge. Signature
   small at lower right, tucked into the dark.

4. Die Wilde Jagd (1889), Lenbachhaus G 1405 at 2000x1248. The
   hunt rushes as a horizontal band across the top third, left to
   right: pale struggling bodies and horses dissolving into mist,
   the leader at right with the one hot accent, a red banner
   streaming. Below the band, two thirds of the frame is pale mist,
   empty. The composition is a frieze over a void. The red banner
   is attached to the rider; it is the only saturated color in the
   picture.

5. Der Waechter des Paradieses (1889) at 1256x1711. The angel
   fills the frame edge to edge: the dark wings touch both side
   edges, the head near the top, the feet near the bottom. The
   flaming sword is a load-bearing vertical on the left third,
   running the full height. A thin gold halo circle is drawn behind
   the head, the halo crop showing it as a single unmodeled line.
   The ground is pale gold and ivory with a teal-blue worked band
   at the top; the figure's robe is one flat pale mass. This is the
   pale key of the same grammar: the ground commits to light, the
   darks are rationed to the wings.

6. Pieta (1891) at 5496x3216, in a plain fluted gold frame. A
   wide horizontal: Christ's pale body lies as a horizontal band
   along the bottom third on near-black; the Madonna is a dark
   vertical mass at center-right, her pale hands covering her face
   the only light in the upper field. A thin gold halo circle
   behind her head. The column profile shows the body band
   registering at 85.5 against the 30-40 dark field. The two bands
   cross: horizontal pale body, vertical dark mourner. The upper
   two thirds are void.

7. Mary Lindpaintner (portrait of his wife) at 2548x2532. The
   head fills the frame, nearly touching the top edge, cropped by
   the bottom. The face is the only fully rendered thing, frontal
   and symmetrical; the hair is a dark mass against a flat warm
   ochre ground with vertical striations; the dress below is loose
   indication, barely drawn. The ground is worked but unmodeled:
   one warm value, striated, doing no describing.

## Tonal arc (painting-area crops, 0-255)

luzifer 32.1/23.9 darkfrac 0.906, sphinxkiss 25.6/29.0 darkfrac
0.909, pieta 45.2/32.7 darkfrac 0.842, suende 72.2/55.9 darkfrac
0.613, wildejagd 81.7/47.0, mary 105.3/39.4, waechter 154.1/62.9
palefrac 0.343. The value key is free per composition, as every
study in the lineage has found, but the palette always commits:
either near-black grounds with the pale fraction rationed to flesh
and one hot accent (the dark pictures run darkfrac 0.61-0.91 with
palefrac under 0.04), or the pale gold key with the darks rationed
to the wings (waechter the only palefrac above 0.05). No picture
sits in the middle. The sphinxkiss column profile shows the red
block at bands 0-3 (27-61) stepping hard to 6-14 black at band 4:
the step is a decision, not a gradient.

## Re-render families (PIL, single pass, crude glyphs)

Family A, the oversize figure on the committed ground. A1 the
species: flat dark ground to every edge, one large pale figure
cropped by the frame edges, the frontal face with the two blank
eyes, one hot red accent placed with the figure. A2 the emblem
failure: the same figure small and centered with margins all
around on an even mid ground. A3 the illustration failure: the
figure standing in a rendered landscape with gradient sky, trees,
and a ground plane. A4 the spotlight failure: the figure on a
vignette ground, lit center fading to dark corners. Findings: the
flat committed ground is load-bearing; shrinking the figure with
margins turns it into a portrait miniature; rendering a room or a
landscape behind it turns it into an illustration; the vignette
turns the figure into a stage prop. One honest schematic finding:
the accent reads as arbitrary when it floats free, so in the
species it must be attached to the figure or be the ground
itself, as it always is in the paintings.

Family B, the body as the diagonal on two flat blocks. B1 the
species: the hard red-to-black step, one pale body mass crossing
the step on the diagonal, cropped by the frame edges. B2 the
named-filter failure: the same body on a smooth red-to-black
gradient. B3 the illustration failure: the small body centered in
a big rendered landscape. B4 the emblem failure: the body
vertical, centered, symmetrical. Findings: the hard step is the
composition; the gradient visibly turns the ground into applied
atmosphere and the body dissolves into it; the diagonal is doing
the work that a horizon would do, and straightening the body
uprights it into an emblem. The step must stay hard and the body
must stay diagonal and cropped.

## Quality thesis

Stuck's compositional discipline, as the seven inspections show
it:

- The oversize figure. The figure is always drawn larger than its
  container: Lucifer's head near the top edge, the Waechter's
  wings touching both sides, Mary's head nearly touching the top,
  the Suende torso pressed into its shrine. The frame is a
  container the figure strains against, never a window the figure
  sits inside. This is the inverse of the quiet-subject grammar:
  where Ensor made the hero the smallest palest thing in a
  weather, Stuck makes the figure the weather's full size and
  lets the frame cut it.
- The committed ground in color. The ground commits to one flat
  hue and is forbidden to model anything: near-black night, the
  blazing red block, pale gold, warm ochre, the mist void. The
  sphinxkiss step is measured hard. All form is in the body; all
  ground is decision. This is the Rops flat-color-ground finding
  carried into oil paint, and it is stricter than the print
  version because paint tempts modeling everywhere.
- Flesh as the only light. In the dark pictures the pale flesh
  is the only luminous incident, with the light rationed further
  to the face, the eyes, the halo line, the hands. Light is never
  a lighting design; it is a ration. The Pieta hands and the
  Lucifer eyes are the whole lighting plot.
- One hot accent, attached. The red banner on the rider, the red
  ground behind the bodies, the glowing eyes in the face, the
  gold halo circle: exactly one saturated incident per picture,
  and it is always attached to the figure or is the ground
  itself. A floating accent is decoration; an attached accent is
  punctuation.
- The face as loaded event. The faces are frontal, symmetrical,
  mask-like, and they confront the viewer: Suende, Lucifer, Mary.
  The gaze is the crispest thing in pictures whose every other
  contour dissolves. A turned or profile face would unplug the
  picture.
- The body as the diagonal. Where there are two bodies, they
  make one diagonal cascade (Sphinxkiss) or crossed bands (the
  Pieta's horizontal body against the vertical mourner). The
  diagonal does the work of a horizon, and it always crops at
  the frame edges.
- Dissolve at the edges. Every contour except the face, the
  accent, and the step is allowed to give up into the ground:
  the Suende face into the hair, Lucifer's legs into the black,
  the hunt into the mist. The dissolve is the finish; a fully
  rendered contour everywhere would be academic.
- The frame as architecture. The Suende shrine shows the
  discipline extended past the canvas: the fluted pilasters, the
  carved molding, the title lettered on the plinth. The picture
  is an object of veneration, and the frame is composed, not
  bought.

## Avoid-list additions

- The small centered figure with margins as his composition.
- Rendered or gradient grounds behind the figure (the
  illustration and the named-filter failures).
- The vignette spotlight as his lighting.
- The hot accent floating free of the figure (must be attached
  or be the ground).
- The hard step softened to a gradient.
- The loaded face in profile or turned away.
- Reading the serpent, the sphinx, or the shushing Lucifer as
  mere eroticism or spookiness without the shrine discipline:
  the flat ground, the rationed light, the attached accent.
- The "first German symbolist" label and the Nazi-period
  appropriation story told as settled fact (per the dossier's
  honest handling).

## Seeds

474. **The Oversize Figure** : from Stuck's figure/frame
    relation (Lucifer's head near the top edge, the Waechter's
    wings touching both frame edges, Mary's head nearly
    touching the top, the Suende torso pressed into its temple
    frame) and Family A (A1 the oversize figure cropped by the
    frame edges on the committed flat ground is the species; A2
    the small centered figure with margins is the emblem
    failure; A3 the rendered room is the illustration failure;
    A4 the vignette spotlight is the stage-prop failure). A
    generative portrait piece where the figure is always drawn
    larger than the frame: the head near or cropped by the top
    edge, the mass touching the sides, the ground one committed
    flat value, the flesh the only light, one hot accent
    attached to the figure. The frame is a container the figure
    strains against, not a window it sits inside.
    Success: the figure reads as pressing out of the frame;
    shrinking it with margins visibly turns it into a portrait
    miniature; rendering a room behind it visibly turns it into
    an illustration.
    Distinct from 458 The Scale Shock (one figure oversize
    against its frame vs a giant with tiny affected figures at
    its feet), from 466 The Black Sun (the oversize frontal
    figure vs the inverted black light source), and from 469
    The Frieze on the Flat Field (one figure straining the
    frame vs a mask frieze on a flat field).

475. **The Hard Step** : from Der Kuss der Sphinx (the measured
    column profile: the red block at bands 0-3, 27-61, the hard
    step at band 4 down to 6-14 black, no gradient) and Family
    B (B1 the two flat blocks with the hard step plus the pale
    body crossing on the diagonal is the species; B2 the
    gradient is the named-filter failure; B3 the small body in
    a rendered landscape is the illustration failure; B4 the
    vertical centered body is the emblem failure). A generative
    piece built as two flat color blocks meeting at one hard
    step (hot above, black below, or pale gold above and dark
    below), one pale body mass crossing the step on a diagonal,
    cropped by the frame edges. The ground is a decision, never
    weather.
    Success: the step reads as a composed decision; softening
    it to a gradient visibly turns the piece into atmosphere;
    centering the body vertically visibly turns it into an
    emblem.
    Distinct from 467 The Seam (two flat blocks with a hard
    step and a diagonal body vs two worked fields meeting on a
    diagonal with figures at the seam), from 470 The Sky Does
    the Expelling (the hard hue step with a diagonal body vs
    two tonal blocks staging an event by proportion), and from
    460 The Flat Color Ground (the hard step between two blocks
    vs one flat wash with line-only drawing).
