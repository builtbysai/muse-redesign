# Robert Rehfeldt: Study Notes

**Profile:** Robert Rehfeldt (Stargard, Pomerania, January 5, 1931, to
Berlin, September 28, 1993). The other half of the Mendelstrasse hub,
and the doorway named first by the 2026-10-03 Ruth Wolf-Rehfeldt study:
after her strictest grid machine, the artist who ran the loosest,
loudest instrument of the pair, the postal system itself. Born in a
city that changed countries under him (German Empire at his birth,
Poland after the 1945 expulsions), a war-time child evacuee to Bad Ischl
1940-45, drafted into the Volkssturm and deserted, back to Berlin in
1946 at fifteen, reunited with his mother; his father died when he was
two. Stonemason and transport worker, rejected by the Weissensee academy
in the East, so he studied at the Hochschule fur Bildende Kunste in
West Berlin 1948-53, where the encounters with Karl Schmidt-Rottluff and
Alexander Camaro set him on art as open action rather than ideology.
Freelance graphic artist, press illustrator, picture-journalist. VBK
member, which meant studio space, tax registration, and state
building-art contracts, in what the sources describe as an uneasy
alliance of mutual low-level antagonism with the authorities; on one
occasion his public-sector contracts were cancelled after he mailed
postcards carrying "Sei Kunst in Getriebe" (Let art set the agenda).
Married Ruth Wolf-Rehfeldt in 1955. Late-1950s realism with metaphysical
features under the influence of his friend the painter Ernst Schroder,
then experiments on all levels: pop artist, assembler, installer,
activist, cinephile, in ChertLudde's phrase, "on all fronts of the art
movements that did not exist in the GDR." From 1961 he helped organize
illicit exhibitions. The Honecker liberalization after 1971 opened the
crack he needed: through Klaus Groh he entered the international mail
art circuit in the early 1970s, and his Pankow studio became the
information office on Western art for East Germans, a hub where people
gathered for news from outside and to look at the mail art publications
coming through the post. Correspondence with Nam June Paik, Wolf
Vostell (who dubbed him the "Fanfare of Pankow"), Robert Filliou, Dick
Higgins, and from 1975 Paulo Bruscky in Recife. In 1975 he issued the
"Art in Contact" postcard call and, on the model of his own Galeria
Teatru Studio show in Warsaw, staged the first mail art exhibition in
the GDR; it seeded the Arkade 1978 and EP Gallery 1979 shows and, in
1986, the East Berlin meeting of the first Decentralized International
Mail-Art Congress. He directed the "Palette Nord" drawing circle
1972-87. The 1991 Ephraim-Palais retrospective showed his own work
beside his mail art project; Parterre Gallery did another retrospective
in 2008. His work is in the Berlinische Galerie. ChertLudde has the
estate: "Denken Sie jetzt bitte nicht an mich" 2021, "Mail Message
from my Studio" 2026, and the mail art archive (two fat volumes
published, only through the letter B).

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on four artworks at source resolution, two

## The core finding: the network is the medium

Every other artist in this study sequence owns an instrument: a
typewriter, a plotter, a date formula, a Markov chain. Rehfeldt's
instrument was the postal system, and his technique was everything you
can do to a sheet of paper so that it survives the post and arrives
changed. The stamps, the slogans, the serial copies, the costumes, the
one-sheet lithographed newsletters (Contart, Artworker Actual News):
each is a different answer to one question, what can a sheet carry
across a surveilled border. The re-render studies proved the mechanism
twice over, and both times the finding was the same: his compositions
are designed for degradation. The stamp grid expects uneven inking; the
word-salad poster expects to be read at three distances; the
ARTWORKERS UNITE self-portrait expects the matrix to die. He did not
fight the copy. He composed with it.

**Study A, the stamp species: the stamp never lands twice.** I drew my
own postman-on-bicycle motif (not his; the species is the point, not
the drawing) and stamped it in a 4x5 grid with per-impression rotation
(plus or minus 7 degrees), offset (plus or minus 14 px), ink density
(0.55 to 1.0), and an 18 percent chance of a ghost double-strike, with
scattered six-digit serial numbers like his "100176" and a big
"MAIL ART IS FINE ART" hand stamp over the middle. Three findings.
First, imperfect repetition reads as hand instantly: remove the jitter
and the sheet becomes a font. Second, the slogan must be smaller than
the field it unifies: mine came out too large and flattened the middle
into a banner, where his sits inside the field as a signature. Third,
the scattered numbers do compositional work his sheet shows and mine
confirmed: they break the grid's march without breaking the grid.

**Study B, the word-salad species: the skeleton and the flesh.** I
rebuilt the "Art in the Mail" poster as a study: five giant anchor
words (ART, IN THE MAIL, 1988, CONTACT, PEACE) with 190 small
multilingual snippets packed between, plus circled words in his
rubber-stamp habit. Three findings. First, the big glyphs are the
skeleton: at ten feet the sheet reads ART / IN THE MAIL / 1988 and
nothing else; at ten inches it is all snippets. Second, the snippets
need air: his original keeps the cutouts separated with white space
around them, and my first dense pass mushed at the top where overlaps
ran past about twenty percent. Third, the circled words work as
punctuation marks, little seals that close a phrase the way his rubber
stamps close an envelope.

**The graphic exchange and the copy as dissent.** The Bruscky
correspondence is the technique's laboratory. Photocopiers were Stasi
controlled, so Rehfeldt approximated the Xerox aesthetic with
antiquated means: stereotype (cliche) newspaper printing, lithography,
potato printing, children's stamp kits. The November 3, 1977
ARTWORKERS UNITE serial self-portrait was repeatedly copied from the
same matrix until his figure distorted into illegibility; he then
stamped "Original" on the most faded copy. Bruscky answered in kind
with the August 1975 envelope series: empty envelopes, his photocopy
self-portrait on the inside flap, mailed daily for four days, each one
more faded. This is the opposite of Ruth's carbon-copy economy. Where
her edition model designed the sheet to survive the third carbon, his
designed the image to die beautifully in the open. The decay is not a
loss of the work. It is the work.

**The costume Polaroids: identity as the material.** Over fifty
three-quarter-length self-portraits in costume, American GI to NVA
officer to astronaut to pilot to the big army coat covering half his
face, shot on Polaroid because instant development could not be
monitored (mere ownership of film was subversive). The four-outfit
postcard to Bruscky is stamped "Impress your stamp on the future."
The satire is exact: a man born in the German Empire, raised through
the Nazis and the division, performing how easily a citizen changes
disguises when the climate demands it. Read against Ruth's camouflage
(the typist's anonymity as cover), his is the mirror move: visibility
as cover. The most legible man in the room is the one nobody has to
look at twice.

**The slogan inventory.** CONTART (contact plus art: the worldwide
network of solidarity), COMART, "Dada is dead contart living in your
mailbox", "Art in Contact. Its Life in Art!", "HELP OVERCOME THE
EGOCENTRIC PART IN ARTISTS BY COOPERATION", "MAKE A CREATIVE WORLD
NOW", "FOR A LIBERATED ART, FOR A HUMANE ART", "Sei Kunst in Getriebe".
The Gilbert essay makes the key point: the slogans ambivalently echo
the ethics of communism, which is why they passed. "COMART" hardly
reads as dissent. The ambiguity is load-bearing, not decorative: in a
country intercepting 90,000 letters a day, a message that can be read
two ways is a message that arrives.

**The rubber-stamp bureaucracy.** He cut stamps with long
official-sounding company names, hoping they would intimidate the
postal staff handling his mail. This is the smallest and maybe the
sharpest of his instruments: the state's own visual language, the
rubber stamp, turned into a mild spell against the state's readers.
Bruscky did the same from the other side, stamping envelopes into
chaos until the authority drained out of the mark.

## What makes it sing

The designed degradation: nothing in his practice assumes a clean
channel, and everything is better for it. The slogan ear: CONTART is a
better word than anything in the manifestos. The network discipline:
every sheet is a packet, addressed, stamped, and built to be handled by
strangers and censors alike. The humor that survives the archive:
"my first stamp this day", the children's postman, the official
company names that never existed. The double economy with Ruth: her
sheets survive copying, his sheets perform it.

## Avoid list (additions)

- Rubber-stamp grids with perfect repetition: a font wearing a stamp
  costume. The jitter is the hand.
- Mail-art pastiche: envelopes covered in random stamps with no
  network behind them, no addressee, no third reader.
- Copy-decay irony without a real copy process: a Photoshop fade with
  "Original" stamped on it is a joke about his joke.
- Slogan art without the bureaucracy it was written against: his
  slogans are addressed to a censor, not a gallery wall.
- Costume self-portraits as dress-up: his are identity satire under a
  regime that assigned identities on demand.
- The Stasi story as branding over the stamp work: the surveillance
  explains the instrument, never the quality.
- The camouflage story as branding stays on the list from the Ruth
  study; his version rhymes with it and is just as quotable.
