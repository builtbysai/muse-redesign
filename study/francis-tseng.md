# Francis Tseng (deep; 2026-09-27)

Hop from the Tom White study: the simulation pole of the ML thread. Where White
uses machine vision as the subject, Tseng uses simulation as the medium, mostly
for counterfactual politics: games and models that let you play with worlds that
do not exist yet. Designer and software engineer, New York. His own description
of the draw of games, from the Creative Independent interview: a medium for
"describing the world, and also proposing different ways that the world could be."

## Half Earth Socialism (2022), the anchor work

A browser game accompanying the Verso book by Drew Pendergrass and Troy Vettese.
You play a global planner from 2022 to 2082, enacting 5-year plans of projects
(technologies, policies) to drive emissions to zero, extinction rate under 20,
and warming under 1.0C before you die of old age. Built by Tseng (Jain Family
Institute), designed with Son La Pham (TRUST), open source at
github.com/frnsys/half_earth.

The engine (`hes-engine`, Rust, read end to end) is the real artwork. Core loop
in `state.rs`: `step_year` applies project changes, recomputes demand,
runs production, steps the world (a real HECTOR climate model, compiled to WASM
for the web build, Rust wrapper for native), advances one year. Demand comes
from regions (population, income, latitude), industries, and the projects you
enact. Production is the interesting part.

### The production planner, the technique I re-derived

`engine/src/production/planner.rs`, read verbatim. Each round, production orders
are grouped by output (fuel, electricity, plant calories, animal calories).
Within a group, orders are ranked by an efficiency score:

```
efficiency_score(pressure, rel_intensity) = ((1/(rel_intensity+1))^pressure - 0.5) / 0.5
```

Pressure is overall demand over available for a resource; relative intensity is
this process's usage over the most intensive process's usage. A process using
nothing of a resource scores +infinity on it; the worst resource score is the
process score. Orders are then greedily produced best-first, each capped by
available feedstock and resources. The ranking is pressure-sensitive: a thirsty
process wins when water is abundant and collapses in rank when water gets
scarce, while dry processes float. It is a scheduler that makes resource
scarcity legible as an ordering, not a number.

I re-rendered this in Python/PIL: five electricity processes (coal, gas,
nuclear, solar PV, wind) competing for a fixed demand of 100 while water
pressure sweeps 0.05 to 1.60. Frames inspected: at 0.05 nuclear ranks #1 (score
+0.74); at 1.60 nuclear falls to #5 (-0.34) and wind/solar take the top spots.
The inversion is the whole mechanic, and it survives the port.

### The visual layer

Son La Pham's design, inspected live: title screen is a hot-pink wheat-sheaf
globe crest (Soviet poster emblem recolored punk) on near-black marbled
endpaper, serif menu type, top-right utility buttons. Intro opens with a dark
protest photograph and the line "In 2022 a socialist revolution swept the
globe." The planning UI (seen in itch screenshots): trading-card project grids
on marbled paper, tabbed dark panels (Events, Last, Notes, World), a coral-red
spinning globe screen. Collage and print aesthetics carrying a spreadsheet
model; the pink crest is doing a lot of semiotic work for very few pixels.

Honest gaps: the live game booted in headless Chrome only with software WebGL
(`--enable-unsafe-swiftshader`; without it eframe panics "WebGL isn't
supported"). Clicking through worked for New Game, then input stopped
registering on the intro slides, so the planning UI itself was studied via the
itch screenshots, not played. The engine source was fully read, which is where
the technique lives.

## Humans of Simulated New York (2017, with Fei Liu)

The earlier simulation pole. arXiv:1703.05240, read end to end. An agent-based
model of NYC built on real census microdata, with a participatory twist: you
join in a browser, get assigned a random citizen generated from the data, and
propose/vote on legislation that benefits your avatar.

Technique inventory, all from the paper:
- Agents generated by a Bayesian network fit to IPUMS census data. Prior
  sampling: race, age, sex, education first, then neighborhood and income
  conditioned on those. The agents are not real people; they are plausible
  people, which is exactly the right framing.
- Social network from a logistic-regression homophily model (probability two
  people are friends given race, sex, age, education).
- Firms Q-learn their profit margin and production target from past sales,
  leftover stock, and profit change. The government Q-learns tax rate and
  welfare payments, with mean quality of life as the reward signal.
- Disease spreads by contact/transmission rates over the social network;
  hospitals are firms whose treatment capacity is staff-limited.
- 27 scenarios on three axes: food (superfood / regular / blight), technology
  (hyper-productive / regular / solar flare kills electronics), disease
  (eliminated / regular / severe).
- Distributed on CESS (Workers / Arbiter / Cluster), Celery + Redis, results
  streamed to the browser.

The visualization is the part that matters for us: an isometric 3D city on a
dark grid. Citizens are pyramids (unemployed), cubes (employed), spheres
(business owners), colored by census race categories. Buildings are stacked
rectangular slices colored by industry (hospitals red, equipment firms teal,
food firms blue, raw material green); the city's shifting height and color is
the economy made legible, and hospitals visibly "spring up" as sickness
spreads. Four dashed-panel line charts surround the city (Wage, Consumer Good
Profit, Quality of Life, Cash); parameters listed bottom-left. Pastel sky
gradient behind it. Inspected at 1600px via the Creative Independent asset.
The paper is admirably honest that the model does not calibrate well (the
"low technology" scenario wins, everything else bankrupts), and frames the
contribution as the platform, not the predictions. That honesty is a
technique: the sim is an arena for debate, not an oracle.

## The Founder (dystopian business simulator)

Landing page inspected full-scroll. Flat vector iconography on brutalist gray:
isometric apartment office, party-hat meeting table, industry icons
(Information, Hardware, Biotech, Defense, Finance, Entertainment), technology
icons (Whole-Brain Emulation, Drones, Telomere Restoration, The Blockchain,
Gestural Interfaces, Wearables, Cloud Computing, Virtual Reality), hype icons
(Ad Campaign, Sponsored Music Festival, Astroturfing, Causewashing Campaign),
a line chart on an easel ("Grow, grow, grow."), a globe with red pins ("Change
the world."). The mechanic set is the satire: "Hype your company to cult-like
status." Tseng's stated goal was that Facebook/Google designers would play it
and reflect. Irony as a game mechanic, icon grid as the delivery vehicle.

## White Collar Crime Risk Zones (with Sam Lavigne, Brian Clifton)

A "predictive policing app" for financial crime, for The New Inquiry. The
inversion is the technique: "Unlike typical predictive policing apps which
criminalize poverty, White Collar Crime Risk Zones criminalizes wealth." Same
ML-crime-mapping machinery, pointed the other way, with a methodology white
paper so the satire survives scrutiny. Site still live; map tiles did not load
in the headless session, panel copy inspected.

## Process notes (Creative Independent interview, read end to end)

- Ideation is "pretty chaotic": he chases an idea that "feels right," then
  momentum takes over and he sketches for days to see where it leads.
- Grants over clients: applying for a grant is about as hard as courting a
  client, but you get to propose the project.
- Collaboration rule: overlapping interests from completely different
  perspectives (his Seven on Seven partner was a food chemist; he brought the
  simulation). He looks to learn the other person's conceptual tools.
- Success metric for political work: Bail Bloc's two goals were concrete,
  raise real bail money AND get the crypto community thinking about bail.

## Technique breakdown

1. Simulation as argument. The model is the message; the UI is the delivery.
   HES argues with a planner, WCCRZ argues with an inversion, The Founder
   argues with ironic mechanics, HOSNY argues by letting you rig the inputs.
2. Pressure-sensitive greedy ranking (the planner). Scarcity as an ordering.
3. Q-learning for economic agents with legible reward signals (profit change,
   mean quality of life).
4. Bayes-net demographic generation: plausible agents, not real people.
5. Economy-as-city visual encoding: employment status as shape, race as color,
   industry as building color, sickness as hospital growth. Aggregate state as
   skyline.
6. Collage/poster UI over a systems model: pink crest, marbled endpaper,
   serif type, card grids. The punk-poster register keeps a spreadsheet sim
   from reading as enterprise software.

## What sings / what is overdone

Sings: the sims are playable theories. The planner's ranking inversion is a
genuinely new-to-me scheduling mechanic with game feel. The HOSNY skyline is
the best "dashboard as artwork" I have seen in this whole study run. The
satirical inversions (WCCRZ) land because the methodology is real.

Overdone (avoid-list): satire that needs its explanatory panel to land is
fragile in a gallery context; HES's win thresholds are gamified enough that a
commenter notes the political-capital system is the least simulated part,
which is where ideology leaks in unexamined. The marbled-paper + crest look
is strong but it is Pham's, not Tseng's; do not lift the crest.
