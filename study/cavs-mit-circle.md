# CAVS at MIT: Study Notes

**Depth:** deep. Read the MIT News 50th anniversary account of the
founding, the ACT page on the CAVS special collection (1967 to 2009,
over 200 fellows), the Wikipedia biography of Gyorgy Kepes, the ZKM
page on Centerbeam (documenta 6, 1977, and the 2017 reconstruction),
the MIT List Visual Arts Center page on Otto Piene: Lichtballett, the
New Scientist piece on the Kepes photograms, and the Stanford Cantor
account of The New Landscape show. Visually inspected five images at
full resolution: Kepes Magnetic Fields (1938, printed later), Kepes
Untitled (Cone and light) (1938), Kepes Untitled (Hands and string)
(1940), Kepes Solid Forms (1939 to 1941), and a Piene Light Ballet
installation view with the Light Drum below and the Light Satellite
sphere above throwing dotted light across the wall. Built two local
re-renders and inspected them: a dipole field model with nine thousand
filing dashes, and a three frame rotating drum projection.

## The founding idea

Gyorgy Kepes founded the Center for Advanced Visual Studies at MIT in
1967 and directed it until the early 1970s. He had come to MIT in 1946
from the New Bauhaus in Chicago, where he ran the Light and Color
department under Laszlo Moholy-Nagy. The center was a fellowship
program, not a department in the usual sense: artists were brought in
to work alongside scientists and engineers, in the open, on work meant
to reach civic scale.

Two words from Kepes carry the whole program: interseeing and
interthinking. The claim is that a scientific image and an artwork can
be looked at with the same eyes, and that putting them side by side at
the same size teaches a shared pattern language. His 1956 book The New
Landscape in Art and Science did exactly that, pairing modern painting
with images made by X-ray machines, electron microscopes, sonar,
radar, and stroboscopic cameras. The pairing only works when the two
images share a real structure. When they merely both look abstract,
it is decoration.

Otto Piene, a founder of Group Zero in Dusseldorf with Heinz Mack, was
the first international fellow in 1968 and directed the center from
1974 to 1994. Under him the work widened to steam, laser, holography,
environmental sculpture, computer graphics, video, sound, and dance,
and the center ran a series of Sky Art conferences. The Master of
Science in Visual Studies became MIT's first graduate degree in the
arts in this period.

The fellow list is a map of this whole study series: it includes
Takis and Piotr Kowalski, both studied in the two sessions before this
one, plus Wen-Ying Tsai, Stan VanDerBeek, Nam June Paik, Charlotte
Moorman, Aldo Tambellini, Maryanne Amacher, Alan Sonfist, Jack Burnham,
and Krzysztof Wodiczko, who directed the center last. CAVS is not one
artist. It is a node where many of the lines in this sketchbook cross.

## Kepes: the photogram as an instrument

Kepes made photograms from the late 1930s on: objects placed directly
on photosensitive paper, then exposed to light. No camera. The image
is a record of contact and distance. What touches the paper prints
sharp and white. What hovers above it prints softer and greyer,
because light leaks around it. Distance becomes softness, and the
print holds a small depth map without ever describing it that way.

Magnetic Fields (1938) is the clearest case. Two white bars stand
vertical on a black field, one above the other with a gap between them.
Around the gap, a cloud of white speckles and short hair like strokes
bursts outward, densest at the facing ends of the bars. Below, a net of
thin white string loops across the dark. Three systems share one print:
the solid bars, the field cloud, and the literal string. The bars are
the cause, the cloud is the effect made visible, and the string is a
reminder that a net is also a field, one you can tie.

Nothing here is drawn. Iron filings near a magnet align themselves.
The composition is set by where the poles are and how long the exposure
runs. The artist chooses the arrangement and the proxy, then lets the
physical process fill in the detail. That is why the cloud clumps and
thins the way it does. A drawn version would space the marks evenly.

Untitled (Cone and light) (1938) is the opposite lesson in restraint.
One pale cone hangs in black space, lit so that its point is the
brightest thing in the print. Below it, a dozen hair thin lines run
nearly horizontal, gathering into a bright smear at the bottom of the
frame, like light recorded over time. One solid form, one point, one
field of trajectories. Most of the print is empty black, and the
emptiness is load bearing: it gives the point somewhere to aim and the
lines room to read as motion.

Untitled (Hands and string) (1940) layers dark hand silhouettes with a
white string tangle printed over them. The string is opaque white
where it lay on the paper, grey where it crossed a hand shape. The same
mark changes value depending on what is underneath it. Solid Forms
(1939 to 1941) puts two smooth white solids over a ground of close
horizontal stripes, so the forms are read by how they interrupt the
regular field. Four prints, four versions of one idea: put a real
thing between light and paper, and the print becomes a measurement as
well as a picture.

Palette across all four: gelatin silver black and white, full black to
paper white, no mid tone heroics. The white bar on black is the loudest
note and it is used once or twice per print, never more.

What makes it sing: the phenomenon is real. The viewer is looking at
evidence, not at an illustration of evidence. What to avoid: cleaning
it up. The moment the filings are evenly spaced or the cloud is
symmetrical, the print stops being a record and becomes a diagram.

## Piene: light with dosage

Piene's Lichtballett (light ballet) began around 1959 as hand held
lamps shone through perforated stencils. By the mid 1960s it was
mechanized: revolving lamps, grids, globes, and perforated discs run
from switchboards, projecting moving dotted light into a dark room.
Electric Rose (1965) is a polished aluminum globe studded with bulbs
that light in four sequenced phases. Light Ballet on Wheels (1965)
projects through a revolving disk from two lamps. Electric Anaconda
(1965) stacks seven black globes of decreasing size and lets light
climb the column until the whole stack is lit.

His own writing gives the rule that matters: art as a sender of energy,
not of messages, and dosage as the control. The real sun burns, an
artistic sun can calm and heal. The light ballet is calm and quiet by
design. In the installation view I inspected, a perforated drum sits
low and throws hundreds of soft oval dots up the wall, smallest and
sharpest near the drum, larger and softer higher up, with a chrome
sphere hanging above catching the same pattern in miniature. The wall
is the screen, the room is the frame, and the dots never sit still.

Three things do the work. First, perforation: the pattern comes from
holes in a physical object, so dot size and spacing are set by metal,
not by code. Second, rotation at more than one speed, so the combined
pattern does not repeat on any short cycle. Third, sequencing: things
light in phases, unfurl, dim, and vanish. Nothing is simply on.

What makes it sing: slowness and restraint with real optics. The soft
edge on every dot is the give away that light traveled and spread. What
is overdone in weaker light work: everything at full brightness at
once, constant speed, and saturated color laid over the top to fake
energy that the dosage was supposed to provide.

## Centerbeam: the method at civic scale

Centerbeam (1977 at documenta 6 in Kassel, 1978 on the National Mall in
Washington) is CAVS as a single object. The idea came from fellow Lowry
Burgess, and Piene directed the build. A spine about 44 meters long,
built around a water prism, carried contributions from more than
fourteen fellows plus MIT scientists, engineers, and students: laser
projections, holograms that tracked the sun, steam screens, neon,
video, and inflatable sculptures lifted by helium.

The composition has two states. By day it reads as a processional,
almost a greenhouse or an aqueduct, with prismatic reflections in the
water and holographic effects, and visitors taking part. By night the
same spine becomes a stage: laser images on rising steam, concerts,
performances, and sky events overhead. Piene called the night state a
friendly inferno, and called the whole work a metaphor for a community
of volunteers forming daily symbioses.

The technique worth keeping is not any single medium on the spine. It
is the spine itself. Each fellow contributed exactly one element type,
in one register, to a shared structure that was decided first. Remove
the spine and the work is a list of technologies. With it, the list
becomes a procession, and the day and night states prove the structure
is doing the compositional work, because the same frame holds two
completely different pieces.

## What holds across the circle

One rule in three forms. Kepes lets a physical process fill in detail
inside an arrangement he chose (filings, string, exposure time). Piene
lets real optics fill in softness and spread inside a perforation and
rotation he chose. Centerbeam lets individual fellows fill in one
element each inside a spine the group chose. In every case the artist
builds the constraint and the instrument, then steps back and lets the
system produce marks no hand would space that way. That is the CAVS
method, and it transfers directly to code: choose the field, the proxy,
and the dosage. Do not place every mark yourself.

A second constant: the room and the site are part of the piece. A Piene
ballet needs a dark room with walls at real distances. Centerbeam needs
a horizon and a day night cycle. Kepes is the exception that proves it,
compressing the same idea onto paper: black ground as the room, depth
as softness.

## Re-render findings

My dipole model placed nine thousand small dashes along computed field
lines between two bar poles. It produced clean nested arcs, brightest
in the gap, thinning evenly outward. Next to the real Magnetic Fields
it reads as an illustration. The real filings clump into chains, leave
irregular voids, and throw a few strays far out. The mess is the
evidence. Finding: if a simulated field is too clean, the viewer reads
a diagram. The proxy needs its own small physics, attraction along the
field and a little clumping, or it will not convince.

My drum projection drew six rings of evenly spaced dots rising from a
drum. At three rotation offsets the rings read as stacked caterpillars.
The real installation varies dot size with facing and height, lets
neighbors overlap into washes, and bends the whole pattern with the
room. Finding: regular spacing is the failure mode. Vary the dot by its
angle to the wall, overlap freely, and let at least two rotations run
at incommensurate speeds so the eye never finds the repeat.

## What to avoid

Collaboration used as a press release: a list of media (laser,
holography, steam, video) with no shared structure underneath. Centerbeam
escapes this only because of the spine. The list is not the work.

The scientific image as decoration. The New Landscape pairing fails
whenever the two images share nothing but a general abstract look. The
shared pattern has to be nameable: branching, field lines, grain,
interference.

Light without dosage. Constant brightness and constant speed turn a
ballet into a lamp demo. If nothing ever dims or vanishes, nothing can
unfurl.

## Honest gaps

No CAVS work seen live in this session. Centerbeam was read and seen in
stills, not watched in motion; the 2017 ZKM reconstruction is a proposal
for a Centerbeam 3, not the 1977 original. Kepes's unpublished Light
Book was not read, and the CAVS digital portal was not browsed fellow by
fellow: this pass covers the founder, his successor, and their one big
joint object, not all 200 fellows. Wen-Ying Tsai's cybernetic sculptures
and Elizabeth Goldring's Retinal Projector are named here from center
records only. Takis and Piotr Kowalski appear on the CAVS fellow list;
both were studied on their own in the two sessions before this one, and
their CAVS period work is not covered here.

Next doorways: the Signals London bulletin network, the Espaces
musicaux rooms, the La Defense forests at full resolution, and inside
CAVS itself: Wen-Ying Tsai's vibratory sculptures at the center, Lowry
Burgess and the Centerbeam drawings, and Muriel Cooper's information
landscapes from her CAVS years.
