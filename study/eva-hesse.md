# Eva Hesse: Study Notes

**Profile:** Eva Hesse (Hamburg, 1936, to New York, May 29, 1970). Postminimal
and process artist, best known for soft materials made to do serial work:
cord, wire, rubber, latex, washers. This study takes the doorway the Sol
LeWitt session left open: LeWitt's Wall Drawing #46, "Vertical lines, not
straight, not touching, uniformly dispersed with maximum density covering
the entire surface of the wall," was made two days after her death, in her
honor, installed by LeWitt himself. It was the first wall drawing with a
"not straight" line, and that line became a recurrent motif in nearly one
hundred later wall drawings (#219, #325, #797 among them) and the Scribble
series of 2005-7. She is, in a real sense, the author of the most generative
line in LeWitt's whole practice.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on two artworks at full resolution, two local
re-render studies. Read end to end: the caareviews "Converging Lines"
review of the 2014 Blanton exhibition (Veronica Roberts, curator), the
caareviews "Irrational Judgments" review, the art21 "Friends and Family
Plan" piece, the socks-studio piece on the circle drawings, the thefreelibrary.com excerpts of the catalogue raisonne review and the 1965 period piece,
the Oberlin record for the Washer Table photograph, and the frieze
Hesse/Wilke review. Visually inspected at full resolution: two Hauser and
Wirth Institute estate records of her circle works (1579x2048 and
1496x2048). NOT seen: Metronomic Irregularity II except as reproductions
(it is no longer extant), the Washer Table (text only), any original in
person, the March 1965 LeWitt letter beyond quoted passages, the 1965
mechanical drawings at reading resolution.

## The core finding: seriality with the hand left in

Hesse's method is a three-step machine: take a strict order (grid, rows,
columns, tight serial arrangement), take a pliable material or a human
wrist, and let the two fight. Lippard's line about the circle drawings,
quoted in the socks-studio piece, is the whole recipe: "rows of circles
with or without centres," from which she "extracted an endless internal
vitality that made each one different." Her own words: "repetition does
enlarge or increase or exaggerate an idea or purpose in a statement." And
the crucial asymmetry: the repeated forms never completely adhere, so the
order becomes a vessel for confusion rather than a display of rationality.

This is directly generative. Every one of her circle works reads as an
algorithm executed by a body: same unit, same order, one execution, and the
execution noise is not a bug. Compare LeWitt's doctrine: he writes the
instruction and the drafter's body is noise to be minimized ("the fewer
decisions made in the course of completing the work, the better"). Hesse
inverts it: the body's noise is the only decision that matters. #46 is the
one wall drawing where LeWitt let her doctrine run on his machine.

## Work by work

### The circle rows, 1966-67

The two estate images are the study's core. The first (signed "Eva Hesse
1966") is a vertical band of 8 columns by 22 rows of circles on graph
paper, centered on a blank gridded field. Each circle is an ink wash with
a hand-wobbled rim. The columns have character: the middle columns are
darker, some with heavy ring outlines; the middle rows carry small center
dots. The outer columns are pale washes, almost ghosts. The second image
is 5 columns by 5 rows on a dark wash ground: circles with center dots,
and down the first column a drawn thread hangs from one center dot to the
next circle, where it ties a small spiral with radiating dashes; other
circles in the column carry short hatch marks on their rims. The right
side of the grid is plain circles, the left side is handled.

Technique read: the composition lives in the columns (tone, ring weight,
dot or no dot), not in any single circle. The rim wobble is texture, the
column tonality is structure. The 5x5 piece concentrates its hand work in
the first column and the top row, a gradient of handling across the field.
And the hanging thread is a rehearsal: Metronomic Irregularity II's laced
wire, and the later suspended sculptures, all begin as a drawn thread
between two circles.

### Metronomic Irregularity II (1966)

Three square gray panels, sixteen feet long, cotton-covered wire manually
laced back and forth between them for Eccentric Abstraction at Fischbach
Gallery (Lippard, 1966). LeWitt helped her mount it and later said it had
a "strong and direct and specific effect" on him: "It was really a
magnificent piece and a way of liberation for me in my own work." The
work is no longer extant. What matters for the study: it was never the
same twice, because the wire's sag and crossings changed at every
installation. Contingency as a feature, not a conservation problem.
Roberts calls its gifts to LeWitt's practice "organic linearity,
contingency, and impermanence." For generative work, the lesson is sharper:
a piece whose physical state is never identical twice, where the medium
itself re-executes the drawing at every hang.

### Washer Table (1967)

The best documented exchange of the friendship. LeWitt built a low
plywood table, painted white with a gray grid, and gave it to Hesse. She
painted it black, covered it with around 5,700 rubber washers in tight
rows, put glass over it, and gave it back, joking that he now owed her a
new table. Hesse considered the washer placement a form of drawing.
LeWitt then used it as an actual coffee table. The caareviews note is the
technique read: "despite the serial, orderly layout of the industrially
manufactured circular forms, the pliable rubber material produces subtle
ripples that lend the tabletop a gentle dynamism."

This is the print-voice parable in one object: a machine-made grid plus
machine-made units, and the material itself refusing to lie flat. The
irregularity is not added by the hand, it is inherent in the material at
scale.

### Ringaround Arosie (1965) and Accession V (1968)

Ringaround Arosie: two circles built from precise coils of cloth-covered
cord on gray Masonite, from the Kettwig period, showing the cord circle as
her signature unit across media (cord, ink wash, pen on graph paper,
rubber washers). Accession V: a 10-inch steel cube with hand-threaded
black rubber tubes through perforations in its sides. The art21 note: from
outside, repetition and order, slightly organic; peering inside, messiness,
"the innards of all the tied tubes." Order on the outside, chaos on the
inside, and the piece is engineered so the viewer has to move to see both.
Another generative principle: the same rule, different readout depending
on where you stand.

### The friendship as technique transfer

The Converging Lines facts worth keeping: Drawing Series I/3241/A&B
(1968) is inscribed "For Eva" in LeWitt's hand; 39 postcards from LeWitt
to Hesse were shown; her side of the correspondence was absent, a noted
gap. LeWitt included one of Hesse's hand-drawn circles in "Paragraphs on
Conceptual Art" as an illustration: "a bit shaky and uncertain but
arranged in a tight grid." That caption is the whole Hesse program in one
sentence.

## The two re-renders

Both built in PIL and visually inspected (renders kept with the session
evidence, not shipped).

**Study A: the circle band.** 8 columns by 22 rows of hand-wobbled
circles on graph paper, rim wobble from two low-frequency sine terms plus
a slight elliptical squash, per-column tone and ring weight, center dots
at column-dependent probability. Finding: the render lands in her species
at once, and it does so because the columns carry the composition while
the wobble stays quiet. The lesson is hierarchical: tone of column first,
ring weight second, rim irregularity third, individual circle variation
last. A generator that spends its randomness on per-circle shape and none
on column character gets the look wrong. Also: uniform per-circle wobble
is fine, the hand does not need row-to-row correlation to read as hand;
what reads as hand is the pressure variation, the tone.

**Study B: the not-straight line.** Wall Drawing #46's instruction
rendered twice: once with low-frequency meander (two sine terms, slow
wavelengths, amplitude just under the inter-line gap) and once with
per-step jitter. Finding, and it is decisive: the smooth version is the
species, the jitter version reads as television static. The not-straight
line is an arm motion over seconds, not a hand tremor over milliseconds.
Any generative reimplementation that adds noise at the segment level gets
#46 wrong; the wander has to live in the low frequencies. Second finding:
at "maximum density" with "not touching," my meandering lines do touch,
because amplitude near the gap width guarantees occasional crossings. The
drafter of #46 must actively steer each line away from its neighbors.
"Not touching" is not a spacing parameter, it is a behavior: maximum
density is negotiated, line by line, by a body watching the wall.

## What makes it sing

- The fight between a real order and a real body, both visible at once.
  Never the order alone (that is LeWitt without her), never the body
  alone (that is expressionism).
- Tonal columns as composition: the eye reads groups before individuals.
- Materials that will not lie flat: rubber, cord, wire, wash.
- Impermanence as medium: the wire piece that is never the same twice.

## Avoid list additions

- Hand-wobble as a noise slider: jitter is static, not hand.
- The grid-plus-circles look without the column tonality; per-circle
  randomness is not the composition.
- Quoting "the idea becomes a machine" while erasing the body that runs
  it. Hesse's half of the bargain is load-bearing.
- Metronomic Irregularity pastiches without the never-twice contingency;
  laced wire that is identically placed every run is a costume.
- The tired LeWitt-rational / Hesse-emotional split. The review dismantles
  it, and the work dismantles it better: her circles are more algorithmic
  than his late scribbles.

## Honest gaps

- No original handled; all works from reproductions.

  reproduction note; no installation photo at full res.
- Washer Table from text sources only; its ripple structure not visually
