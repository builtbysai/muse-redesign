## The two houses

VILLA LENBACH (Luisenstrasse 33, by the Propylaea, Munich). Architect Gabriel
von Seidl, 1887 to 1890, finished 1891. Sequence matters: the atelier wing was
built FIRST, with studios and finely appointed reception rooms for the
clientele, and the central residential wing second, in the Tuscan villa
vocabulary. The commercial apparatus came before the home. Garden by Max Kolb
with a fountain to frame the ensemble. Situation was the statement: directly
outside the Propylaea, within sight of Konigsplatz and the royal collections.
Interiors: the Lenbachhaus's own page says the residential and representative
rooms were "lavishly decorated in various historic styles," furnished with
precious ancient sculptures, medieval paintings, rare carpets and tapestries,
and copies of artifacts "when originals could not be obtained." Behind the
historicist costume, one of Munich's most modern homes: electric light from its
own generator, steam heating, a bathroom, a photo studio. Survival: bomb damage
1944-45 destroyed the studio wing interiors; only the foyer and the first-floor
representative rooms were reconstructed (1952), the entrance hall colors
restored 1994, the rooms recreated from old photographs with the original
furniture in 1996. What visitors see is an honest 1990s reconstruction of an
1890s interior, and the Lenbachhaus says so itself.

VILLA STUCK (Prinzregentenstrasse 60, Bogenhausen). Architect: Franz von Stuck
himself, every level from layout to door handles. Completed 1898; the Art
Newspaper's 2025 reopening piece says two stages between 1897 and 1915, and the
second stage's contents are unverified. Strict Neoclassical temple facade
outside, his own total interior inside: the facade does not predict the rooms,
the mask first, the private cosmos second. His furniture won a gold medal at
the 1900 Paris World Exposition. The frames are the sharpest detail: a handmade
frame for each painting, gilt carving, panels, inscriptions, so integral that
frame and picture read as one statement. The Suende altar is original Stuck,
conserved in the 2025 restoration: the painting enshrined in a marble altar of
his design, the Kuenstleraltar, the artist's altar, with antique and modern
sculpture as attendants. The atelier held the Serpentine Dancers relief (1895)
and a custom red chair to his design. Survival and now: donated to the city,
museum status formalized 1992 (a secondary source says public access began
1968, unreconciled), reopened 18 October 2025 after an 18-month, 13.5 million
euro renovation. The 2025 rehang openly uses "new furnishings, a new
arrangement, and new acquisitions": the vermillion-red silk curtains in the
salons are new, not Stuck. The historic rooms as visitors see them are a
curatorial composition about Stuck, not a frozen Stuck composition.

The Lenbachhaus states the relation plainly on its own 19th-century collection
page: Stuck "emulated the model of Lenbach, his senior by a generation, but
under more modern auspices." Lenbach invented the template (the artist as
aristocrat, the house as proof of the title). Stuck copied the template and
upgraded it, replacing historicist quotation with a single personal grammar.

## What the rooms actually look like (verified)

The 1909 plates, Stuck's own book, in order:

- VILLA STUCK, plate 147: the temple facade from the street, poplars as dark
verticals, parapet figures, strict symmetry. The facade is a flat frontal
composition, the poplar columns doing the work of Stuck's painted verticals.
- VESTIBUL, plate 148: patterned tile floor with star motifs, a niche holding
a classical figure, the staircase rising, framed works hung on the wall. The
niche is the load-bearing center; the figure is the one lit thing.
- EMPFANGSZIMMER, plate 149: dark paneled room, marble fireplace with a
painting above it, heavy curtains, decorative tile panels, checkerboard floor.
The mantel plus painting form the dark horizontal band; the room reads like a
Stuck canvas you can stand inside.
- MUSIKZIMMER, plate 150: the panel wall, dark wood divided into framed
compartments holding small pictorial panels (animals, figures, an inscription),
a draped classical statue at the center. The wall is composed as a polyptych,
the statue as the central cult image.
- WOHNZIMMER, plate 151: dark wood, a curtained doorway with a scalloped
valance (the doorway as a dark void, like the dark ground in his paintings),
pictures hung in rows, panel insets. The densest room, the most worked.
- ATELIER, plate 152: the shrine cabinet, the money plate. A built-in wall
unit: framed portrait top center, three niches below holding two standing
figures and a bust, a small chest beneath, a patterned rug, the coffered
ceiling with wave motifs above. The whole wall is an altarpiece in furniture.

The modern photographs (Commons, one uploader's series) confirm the grammar
survived: the Musikzimmer with the BEETHOVEN inscription, the tall draped
statue standing in a curtained doorway niche flanked by red-and-gold patterned
curtains, chairs lined up in a row like a frieze; the panel walls with the
dancer and circular-medallion paintings; a dark room with a huge
decorative-glass window, gold pilasters, a chaise. The red-and-gold curtains
are the one hot accent in a dark room, the Stuck rule, attached to the
doorways.

The Lenbach side: the entrance hall (the 1996 reconstruction, original
objects) is a strict shrine composition, central mosaic niche with a Greek-key
border holding a bronze figure on a pink marble pedestal, dark paneled double
doors flanking left and right, arched lunettes with painted hippocamps above,
marble mosaic floor. Symmetry is the entire composition. The atelier wing
courtyard: ochre Tuscan walls, the fountain with its dark bronze putto, the one
dark incident against the committed ochre field, the Lenbach ground color at
architectural scale.

Plate-area tonal stats (photo areas cropped): the 1909 interiors are dark,
darkfrac 0.33 to 0.63, palefrac under 0.16. The period villa was a dark-wood
house. The committed dark ground of the paintings is the committed dark room.

## The compositional contrast, stated plainly

Lenbach composed by collection: many styles, one taste, the authority of
accumulation. His rooms say "I have seen everything and can afford it." The
compositional skill is editorial, the skill of the arranger, and it works only
if one ruthless taste governs every acquisition.

Stuck composed by invention: one grammar, every object, the authority of
consistency. His rooms say "I made everything and it all rhymes." Frame,
furniture, wall painting, door handle: one formal DNA. The house stops being a
container for art and becomes the largest artwork in it.

For generative work the lesson is brutal. A piece with five beautiful systems
and no shared grammar is a Lenbach room without the taste to hold it together.
A piece with one grammar enforced at every scale, frame included, is the Stuck
method. Most failed generative pieces fail the Stuck test: the ornament has no
common DNA with the composition.

## The quality thesis: the shrine grammar

Both houses, the senior's and the junior's, keep arriving at the same
composition: a central niche or framed unit, one loaded figure, flanking
verticals, a patterned border. Lenbach's entrance-hall mosaic niche. Stuck's
atelier shrine cabinet. The Suende marble altar. The Musikzimmer's statue in
the curtained doorway. The grammar is older than both of them, it is the
chapel, but each man enforced it as a personal compositional law.

The generative translation: give the piece a focal object and build the whole
composition as its shrine, every element subordinate to the enthronement. The
frame is load-bearing, not a container. The margins, the ground, the accents
all answer to the focal point. A derivative fails the shrine the moment the
frame is an afterthought, or the moment there are two focal points competing
for the niche.

## Re-render families (single pass, crude, inspected)

Family A, the shrine grammar: A1 the niche plus the loaded figure on its
pedestal plus flanking pilasters plus the patterned border is the species; A2
the figure alone with no architecture is the emblem failure; A3 the even grid
of niches is the inventory failure; A4 the niche with no figure is the empty
shrine failure. Finding: the architecture is what makes the figure loaded. A
figure without a niche is a statue; a niche without a figure is a hole.

Family B, the room as a Stuck panel wall: B1 the dark paneled wall plus the
inset pictorial panels plus the one hot band plus the central figure is the
species; B2 the gradient wall is the illustration failure; B3 the red
everywhere is the unrationed failure; B4 the identical empty panels are the
wallpaper failure. Finding: the panels must vary (animal, figure, inscription),
the accent must be rationed to one, the figure must be central.

## Avoid-list additions

- The "Art Nouveau/Art Deco" label for the Villa Stuck interior as a settled
fact (loose English-Wikipedia phrasing; the period vocabulary is in Ostini's
1900 Innendekoration survey and the German monographs).
- Treating the Lenbachhaus historic rooms as surviving originals (the 1994-96
reconstruction from photographs, with original objects, documented by the
Lenbachhaus).
- Treating the 2025 Villa Stuck rehang as a frozen Stuck interior (new
furnishings, new arrangement, the vermillion curtains are new).
- The 1968 vs 1992 Villa Stuck museum founding as a settled date (unreconciled).
- The Villa Stuck second building stage 1897-1915 as known (contents unverified).
- My own phantom reads: the pale-blue salons, the fireplace, the dining room,
the red leather chair, none of which exist in the source files. The corrupted
renders are struck from the record; only the verified thumbnails count.
- A Stuck-derivative interior with pale or gradient walls (the period house was
dark wood; the pale rooms are a misreading).
- A shrine composition with two focal points, or a focal figure with no
architecture around it.
