# Ad Reinhardt: Study Notes

**Profile:** Ad Reinhardt (Adolph Frederick Reinhardt, Buffalo, December 24,
1913, to New York City, August 30, 1967, heart attack in the studio, age 53).
Named first among open next doorways by the 2026-10-03 Ellsworth Kelly study,
the master of the un-ruleable half of composition and the quality-first
choice after a run of rules, grid, and type studies. The territory break the
practice needed: after a month of mechanisms, this is a study of pure
compositional judgment, restraint as an instrument. Born in Buffalo, raised in
New York, Columbia University 1931-35 studying art history under Meyer
Schapiro, close friends with the poet Robert Lax and the theologian Thomas
Merton, the three of them developing similar ideas of simplicity in different
directions. Turned down art school scholarships, feeling he already had the
technique. Member of the American Abstract Artists 1937-47, in the Betty
Parsons circle that became Abstract Expressionism, member of The Club. Taught
at Brooklyn College from 1947 until his death, twenty years. Cartoonist for
the leftist press, PM and New Masses, including the famous 1946 "How to Look
at Modern Art in America" tree. Friend of Lee Krasner since the WPA days,
respected her work when few men in the circle did, buried near Pollock in
Springs at her arrangement.

**Depth:** deep on text, deep on four artworks at source full resolution,
two local re-render families built and inspected (one re-lifted pass after
an over-stretched contrast pass inverted hues, corrected). Read end to end:
the Smarthistory "Abstract Painting" page in full (the edge-of-perception
passage, the pupil-dilation account, the 1963 MoMA 60x60 painting); the MoMA
1991 retrospective press release (the chronology: all-over 1940s, symmetrical
geometry early 1950s, blue and red monochromes 1952, black pictures from
1954, the final five-foot-square nine-square format adopted 1960, Rubin on
the faint light as the vestige of the refiner's fire); the Art Story
biography (the 1946 PM cartoon, the 12,000 symmetry slides); the Smithsonian
American Art Museum biography (the tree's trunk labeled Braque, Matisse,
Picasso, the Mondrian to Grosz scale at the branch junctures, the weights
labeled subject matter, Mexican art influence, War Art); the Brooklyn Rail
"Ad Reinhardt and Pedagogy" (the Twelve Rules for a New Academy, "No
chess-playing", the erased self-portrait assignment, the 1961 de Kooning
exchange); the Brooklyn Rail Krasner piece (the June 2, 1946 PM date, the
tree caption verbatim); the dissertation transcription of the full tree
caption ("Here is a guide to the galleries, the art world in a nutshell,
a tree of contemporary art from pure (abstract) 'paintings' (on your left)
to pure (illustrative) 'pictures' (down on your right)... Be especially
careful of those curious schools situated on that overloaded section of
the tree"); the twelve technical rules via transcription (no texture, no
brushwork, no sketching, no forms, no design, no colors, no light, no
space, no time, no size or scale, no movement, no object, no subject, no
matter, no symbols); the twoinchbrush summary of the Art-as-Art statement
("Art is art, everything else is everything else", the 25 lines of 1955);
the greatgodpanisdead cartoon post (the tree image at 319x400). Visually
inspected: the Art Institute of Chicago black painting at 843x861 (blue
blacks, blue channel running 10 to 14 points above red, center column
lighter, bottom row lightest); the Walker Art Center print at 838x1000
(middle band darkest, warm top and bottom rows); the Steven Vail fine arts
1966 portfolio print at 1920x2434 (the 3x3 nine-square grid legible, middle
row darkest, warm corners against a cool center); the 1946 tree cartoon at
319x400 (trunk lettering, hanging weights on chains, the cornfield corner,
sun top left). NOT seen: any original in person, the Guggenheim black
paintings, the 12,000 slides, the selected writings firsthand, the tree at
a resolution where the leaf names read.

## The core finding: the refusal is the instrument

Reinhardt is the purest answer the study has found to the quality-first
question, what makes the work GOOD. The answer is not a technique. It is a
refusal carried out so completely that the tiny remaining decision becomes
the whole composition. The black paintings apply nine identical rules to
every canvas: five feet square, nine equal squares, no texture, no
brushwork, no sketching, no forms, no color, no signature, no light. Inside
that cell the only free parameter is a tint delta of a few percent between
one near-black and another, warm against cool. That delta is the entire
piece. The cruelty of the doctrine is what makes it work: because nothing
else is allowed to carry meaning, the viewer's attention has nowhere to go
but the delta, and the eye adapts until a ghost of a cross is there.

Study A rebuilt the species to the measured spec: warm-black corners,
blue-black center column, middle band darkest, value budget of about 15
units on a 22 to 37 scale. Panel 1 is the naive flat black, which reads as
nothing. Panel 2 is the true-spec render, which also reads as nothing at
first glance, exactly like the real thing. Panel 3 is the same panel with
a pure gain lift, the dark-adapted eye simulated, and there the structure
is: a cool vertical band against warm flanks, the cruciform's skeleton.
Panel 4 is the control, the identical tint budget shuffled into asymmetry,
and it reads as patchwork noise. The findings are three and they are all
compositional, not technical. First, the tint budget is so small that only
exact symmetry lets the structure emerge; asymmetry spends the same
budget on nothing. Second, the differences must be chromatic, not just
luminance: the AIC measurement shows blue running consistently above red,
and a pure gray-value grid would read as a printer test, not a cross.
Third, the matte surface is load-bearing, not a preference: Reinhardt
siphoned oil from his pigments to get the suede finish, and any sheen at
all would turn the edge-of-perception effect into visible texture and kill
the two-minute reveal. The reveal is priced in attention and paid in
adaptation. A derivative that shows its structure immediately has failed
before it starts.

Study B rebuilt the 1946 tree as a schematic, the opposite voice of the
same man. The real cartoon is a masterpiece of judgmental composition.
The trunk is Braque, Matisse, Picasso. The roots are Van Gogh, Seurat,
Cezanne, Gauguin, plus the Greeks, Persian miniatures, Japanese prints.
Left branches rise toward the sky, carrying the pure abstraction leaves.
Right branches sag and nearly break under hanging weights labeled subject
matter, Mexican art influence, War Art, business as art patron, Regionalism
illustration. The bottom right is a cornfield where Wood, Curry, and
Benton stand and no demand is made on the viewer. The caption is explicit:
start in the cornfields and work your way up. The control panel, a
symmetric tree with equal branches and no weights, says nothing at all.
The finding: the tree's compositional content is vertical morality plus
literal counterweights. Elevation is the value judgment, gravity is the
argument. A hierarchy diagram that treats all branches equally is a
taxonomy; Reinhardt's tree is a verdict. The same instinct that bans color
from the black paintings puts weights on the branches here: composition
as a set of decisions about what is allowed to rise.

The biography feeds the work without explaining it. The Columbia art
history training, the 12,000 slides of architectural symmetry collected
across Europe, Asia, the Middle East, India, Japan, Iran, Iraq, Egypt,
Turkey, Syria, Jordan, the Yucatan, show a man who spent his life looking
at how cultures organize the visual field, and the black paintings are the
most extreme organization he could defend: one square, nine squares, a
cross you have to earn. The Brooklyn College assignment, draw a
self-portrait in pencil and charcoal, then erase it and start over on the
same sheet all term, is the pedagogy of the black paintings: the ego is
the material being removed. The 1961 exchange with de Kooning, the refusal
to be part of the etcetera, is the same refusal that the paintings make.
Even the wit is disciplined: the PM cartoons never leak into the
paintings, because the doctrine forbids it, and the cartoons are where the
judgment goes that the paintings refuse to carry.

## Technique, palette, composition

Technique: oil on canvas, oil partially siphoned from the pigment for a
near-absolute matte surface, multiple thin layers, no visible brushwork,
no texture, conceived in its entirety before the brush is taken up. The
Guggenheim AXA conservation project exists because the technique is
fragile by design: the suede finish that absorbs light also refuses
cleaning. Palette: black, and then the shades that are not quite black,
ultramarine-leaning, red-leaning, green-leaning blacks, differing by
perception's smallest step. The early 1950s ran the same discipline in
red and in blue before the palette collapsed to black. Composition: the
60 by 60 inch square divided into nine equal squares, the cruciform
delineated by barely perceptible shifts in hue and tonality, symmetrical
to the point of invisibility. The earlier variants run oblongs and
rectangles, but the final format is the nine-square grid, the chessboard
reduced to near invisibility.

What makes it sing: the work does something no other painting in the
study does, it makes duration the medium. The Smarthistory account is
exact: your pupils dilate, the biology of sight does the revealing, the
painting is an instrument played by your own eyes. The reward is
proportional to the attention paid, which is the oldest contract in art
stated in its purest form. The matte black is a black hole for light and
for irony both: there is nowhere for the eye to rest except on the work
of looking itself.

What is overdone, the avoid-list: black monochromes with no tint
discipline, just flat black passed off as depth; visible brushwork or
texture in a near-black field, which reads as craft failure rather than
choice; asymmetric near-black compositions, which spend the tiny value
budget on noise; tint deltas wide enough to read immediately, which turn
the piece into a patchwork grid; the "last painting" rhetoric deployed
without the twelve rules behind it; the tree's judgmental tree-diagram
format copied with neutral or decorative weights, which turns a verdict
into clip art; matte-black-as-mood, the goth signifier with no
compositional decision inside it.

Quality notes, the doctrine's question: what makes this work GOOD is
that the refusal is total and the remaining decision is exact. Every
other monochrome in the study keeps something: Ryman keeps the fastener
and the support, Flavin keeps the tube and the wash, Martin keeps the
graphite line. Reinhardt keeps almost nothing, and what he keeps, a few
percent of chromatic difference between nine black squares, is placed
with a symmetry so strict that the eye assembles the cross on its own.
A derivative fails the moment it keeps one extra thing: a signature, a
texture, a wobble, an interesting asymmetry, a tint you can see in the
first second. The tree is the companion lesson: when Reinhardt wants to
judge, he judges in public, with labels, and the compositional device is
elevation plus weight. The two voices never mix. That separation is
itself a compositional decision, and it is the one most derivatives
would get wrong, because the temptation is to be witty inside the
restraint.

## Honest gaps

No original handled. All tint measurements come from web JPEGs of
reproductions, and the Smarthistory page itself warns that photographs
distort the paintings toward navy blue. The tint map in Study A is my
reconstruction from three reproductions (AIC, Walker, Vail), not a
traced painting, and the three sources disagree on which band is
darkest, so the map follows the majority reading. One image-search hit
for a MoMA Reinhardt black painting turned out to be an Albers Homage
to the Square, excluded from the evidence. The 1946 tree was inspected
at 319x400, enough for the compositional structure but not for the leaf
names. The twelve rules and the Art-as-Art statement were read via
transcription, not from the selected writings. The 12,000 symmetry
slides were not viewed. The Guggenheim AXA conservation findings were
not read firsthand.
