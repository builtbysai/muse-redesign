# Whistler's Nocturnes: the dark-ground species

Deep study, 2026-10-05. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as a
next doorway by the 2026-10-05 Whistler Venice Sets study ("the Nocturne
plates as the dark-ground species: Nocturne K184 as the hinge,
Nocturne: Furnace and Riva No. 2 as the deferred sparse end"), the
quality-first choice, and the close of the print-lineage run that went
Rembrandt, Segers, Goya, Redon, Daumier, the etching revival, and the
Venice sets. The question this session answers: what is the nocturne
as a compositional species, and what does it add that the lineage did
not already say.

Key facts, carried from the dossier:

- Nocturne, K184 (G222): 202 x 294 mm, etching and drypoint, 9 states,
  59 known impressions, the only nocturne in the First Venice Set
  (1880). Began in pure etching; drypoint added and rejected across
  the states, wearing away in the final printing. Plate tone wiped
  horizontally to carry the water and the darkening sky; more ink left
  on in the final state; on one impression the tone was pulled with
  fingertips away from the central subject. The catalogue calls it
  "the most extreme example" of artistic printing. The 1880 press saw
  the technique exactly: Daily News, he "has not trusted wholly to
  the etched line"; St James's Gazette, the effect "depends so
  entirely, not only for its effect but even for its substance, upon
  the printer"; British Architect, "brown smoke with clots of ink in
  diagonal lines"; Hamerton, "intended to convey an impression of
  night." Whistler reprinted the hostile lines, torn from context, as
  his own 1883 catalogue notes.
- Nocturne: Palaces, K202 (G200): 298 x 202 mm, the upright one, 12
  states in Glasgow against 8 in Kennedy (a Doyle lot said "ninth of
  nine," matching neither), 54 known impressions, Second Venice Set
  1886. The lines barely changed across the states; Whistler "used
  the incised lines as a framework for his painterly method of
  inking and wiping, making each impression unique in its
  combination of ink colour, paper colour and manipulated
  platetone." With K184 it is "the most complete synthesis of line
  and tone among his intaglio prints." Building News called the
  shadows "furry looking lines" and the sea and sky "rubbed in
  colour," meant as insult, and it is the most accurate technical
  description anyone wrote.
- Nocturne: Furnace, K213 (G208): 171 x 233 mm, 12 states in Glasgow
  against 7 in Kennedy, 46 known impressions. "Inking was as
  important as the framework of lines": plate tone and selective
  wiping set the furnace glow against subdued evening light. Salmon
  printed first; then Whistler took the plate back and printed the
  edition himself, 48 recorded impressions across 1886-87. The New
  York Mail and Express is the one contemporary who saw the
  composition: "the little figure ... of the man before the furnace,
  for which all the rest of the plate seems to be only a framing,
  ... how tenderly, how skilfully, how beautifully it is touched
  in!" The plate was cancelled in three stages and is now in the Art
  Institute of Chicago.
- Riva, No. 2, K206 (G230): 211 x 307 mm, 1 state in Glasgow (against
  drypoint evidence of a second), 44 impressions, the daylight foil:
  impression records note light, pale, even tone, not the heavy
  manipulated tone of the nocturnes. Whistler's 1883 catalogue paired
  it with the St James's Gazette line on "firm drawing" and the
  fuller verdict: "what his eyes can see, and not what his hand can
  do."
- Lagoon - Noon, K216 (G209): 127 x 204 mm, the smallest of the group,
  3 states, 30 impressions, Second Set 1886. The plate page spells it
  "Lagoon - Noon"; the subject page prefers "Lagoon: Noon"; Whistler
  himself wrote "Lagoon Noon." A daylight scene printed with the
  nocturne hand: "uneven medium print tone," "wipe marks in a
  variety of directions."
- The name came from his patron Leyland, a keen pianist, in 1872
  ("I can't thank you too much for the name 'Nocturne' as a title
  for my moonlights"). The Falling Rocket is YMSM 170, 1875,
  Detroit, 60.3 x 46.6 cm. Ruskin's Fors Clavigera line of 2 July
  1877 ("flinging a pot of paint in the public's face") led to the
  Queen's Bench trial of 25-26 November 1878: "The labour of two
  days is that for which you ask two hundred guineas?" "No, I ask
  it for the knowledge I have gained in the work of a lifetime."
  One farthing damages, no costs recovered, bankruptcy May 1879,
  and the Fine Art Society's Venice commission as the lifeline
  after. His courtroom definition: "an arrangement of line, form
  and colour first." The Ten O'Clock lecture (1885): "the artist is
  born to pick, and choose, and group with science"; the evening
  mist "clothes the riverside with poetry, as with a veil"; and his
  working bar for the nocturnes, "like the breath on the surface of
  a pane of glass."
- The dark-ground chain, technically: no aquatint on any nocturne
  plate. The darkness is plate tone and selective wiping, never a
  resin ground. The documented backward link is Rembrandt, the
  etching revival's model (The Three Crosses as the drypoint-burr
  precedent), and Whistler's own 1858 French Set already held a
  street in Saverne at night, "the first of his so-called
  Nocturnes." The dossier found NO documented Whistler-Goya link:
  Goya's aquatint night grounds are the technical precedent
  Whistler pointedly did not use. The 1883 exhibition catalogue,
  written and designed by Whistler himself, is the primary document
  of how he wanted the etchings read: each plate twinned with a
  press cutting torn from context, hostile or admiring, plus his
  own butterfly-signed Reflections. The butterfly "imp." tabs on the
  impressions are the signature of his self-printing practice.
- Flagged conflicts carried: the Kennedy/Glasgow state-count splits
  (note whose count); the K184 page's impossible "January 1880"
  review dates, almost certainly typos for 1881; the Lagoon title
  variants; the trial-costs wording splits (uncontested: he
  recovered nothing); Swann's loose "edition of 30" for Palaces
  against Glasgow's 54 located impressions; the sets text saying 24
  Venetian scenes while listing 21.

## The surprise finding

The nocturne etchings are not the darkest plates in the lineage. They
are mid-value plates that work by reserve. The painted Falling Rocket
is the darkest picture in this session's set (mean 52.3); the etched
nocturnes sit at 140-190, lighter than the furnace interior and barely
darker than the daylight Riva. The species was never about darkness.
It is about the composed value key: in the etchings, the dark is a
ceiling or a room, the light is untouched paper; in the painting, the
dark is a washed field, the light is applied as punctuation. The
Redon study found the value key free; the nocturnes confirm it from
the other side, the etcher's side.

## The six artworks

1. Nocturne, K184, 1879-80 (Met 17.3.86, DP813608, 3650px). The hinge.
Three strict horizontal bands: a toned sky ceiling covering the top
two-fifths with a hard lower edge; a thin worked horizon where every
mark in the plate lives (masts, domes, the city scribble, one dark
ship as the accent, reflections dripping down); the bottom half
nearly blank paper doing the water. The signature tab reads "imp."
This is the band nocturne, the grammar everything else here varies.

2. Nocturne: Furnace, 1879-80 (Met 17.3.114, DP813640, 3685px). The
dark-ground species proper. A glass furnace interior at Murano built
from dense crosshatch over most of the sheet; the furnace mouth a
large reserved pale rectangle with hard edges, center-right, the
brightest thing in the picture because nothing was drawn there; a
smaller reserved window at upper left showing figures; the workman
drawn in line INSIDE the glow, not erased out of it. Bottom half
lightly worked paper. This is the inversion of the band nocturne: the
dark is the room, the light is the opening. Direct line back to
Redon's Caliban (the pale figure lifted out of black) and to Goya's
volante (the brightest value is untouched ground), with Whistler's own
turn: line drawing is permitted only inside the light.

3. The Riva, No. 2, 1879-80 (Met 17.3.107, DP813622, 3684px). The
daylight sparse end, carried as the control species. A long quay of
buildings and crowds across the top third, gondolas on a nearly blank
middle band, and the bottom two-fifths almost entirely blank paper
with a few standing figures who have no ground drawn under them. The
subtraction arc's far end in daylight: the figures float because the
paper is the quay.

4. Lagoon - Noon (Met 17.3.117, DP813636, 4000px). The far end of the
arc, full stop. One sailing ship drawn in fine line near the top,
distant sails, a tiny beach scene at lower right, and the rest is
paper. The subject floats on the void. Lightest and most compressed
plate in the session's stats (mean 210.9, std 15.9).

5. Nocturne: Shipping, 1879-80 (Met 40.63.6, DP813656, 3549px). The
sparse nocturne. A scribbled horizon band of ship silhouettes, one
dark ship mass at right as the anchor, reflections dripping down, and
nearly everything else blank. The tab reads "imp." with a state note.
The band nocturne reduced to its minimum: one mass, one edge, the void.

6. Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket, 1875 (Detroit
Institute, via Commons 4371px). The painted counter-species. A
near-black blue-green washed field; falling trails of gold sparks;
two brighter bursts with smoke at mid-left; a pale golden foreshore
band at the bottom holding two dark figures. The dark is the ground
wash, the light is applied as small punctual dabs. The compositional
agreement with the etchings: the incident is punctuation, never the
subject; the field does the picture.

## Re-render studies (2 PIL families, single pass, inspected)

Family A, the band nocturne (rerender_A_band_nocturne.png):
A1, toned ceiling band with a hard lower edge, the incident confined
to that edge, two dark ship masses with reflection drips, blank water
below: the species. A2, the same marks spread evenly across the sheet:
wallpaper, a fleet inventory; the incident must sit ON the edge. A3,
A1 under a global radial vignette: the named filter failure, darkness
with no composed edge, the architecture gone. A4, the furnace species:
dense crosshatch field with one hard-edged reserved opening and
figures drawn only inside the light: reads strong; the opening's edges
make it an event. Findings: the ceiling's hard edge is load-bearing;
a vignette is not a nocturne; the reserve must have edges to be an
opening rather than a hole.

Family B, the void does the water (rerender_B_void_water.png):
B1, one ship and a horizon scribble on blank paper: the species, the
Lagoon grammar, the ship floats. B2, the ship enlarged and centered:
an emblem, a stamp, not a scene. B3, B1 with rendered wave arcs across
the bottom: wallpaper, the void killed. B4, texture-everywhere sky:
static noise, the ship drowns. Findings: the void must stay void; the
ship must not be centered; rendering the water is the named failure.

Caveats: the A1 reflection drips are mechanically even; the B3 arcs
tile too regularly; the A4 crosshatch is loose. Species/control
contrasts read, which is what the schematics are for.

## Quality notes

What makes the nocturnes good, as compositional decisions:

- The darkness is architecture, not atmosphere. It is a ceiling with
  an edge (the band nocturnes) or a room with openings (the Furnace).
  Atmosphere is what you get when you skip the edge; the A3 control
  proves it.
- The brightest value is never rendered. In the etchings it is
  untouched paper; in the painting it is a dab of gold, a touch, not
  a rendering. Goya's rule holds here too, and the Furnace adds the
  corollary: drawing is allowed only inside the light.
- The incident is punctuation. One ship, one furnace mouth, one
  trail of sparks. The field does the picture. B2 shows what happens
  when the incident becomes the subject: it turns into a logo.
- The value key is free and chosen per plate. The nocturne etchings
  are lighter than the furnace interior; the painted nocturne is
  darker than all of them. "Nocturne" names the key, not the black.

What would make a derivative piece fail: dark filters or vignettes as
night; gradient skies; evenly spread marks; rendered water; the
centered ship; masts and gondolas as signifiers; more than one
incident; explaining the scene (the Ruskin-trial anecdotes as
branding).

## Avoid-list additions

- Vignette or crushed-black filters as a nocturne.
- Gradient skies as night.
- Rendered water (wave marks) under a sparse subject.
- The centered ship as composition.
- Masts, gondolas, fireworks as Whistler signifiers.
- Evenly spread marks across the sheet as his sparseness.
- More than one light event in a dark field.
- The Ruskin "pot of paint" story as the point of the work.
