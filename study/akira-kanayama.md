# Akira Kanayama - Deep Study Notes

**Date:** 2026-09-28 (study pass)
**Artist:** Akira Kanayama (1924-2006), Osaka / Amagasaki, Japan. Gutai
member, toy-car painting machines, 1957 onward.
**Hop path:** Jean Tinguely / Metamatics study, upstream in the
drawing-machine lineage: the mechanical counterpoint to the hand, and the
conceptual root of letting a machine hold the pen.
**Depth:** deep on text plus visuals. Whitestone Gutai Dictionary "Remote

## Who he was, in technique terms

Kanayama was the Gutai member who did not fit Gutai. Before the machines he
painted geometric abstraction so stripped that Shiraga called it "even more
simplified examples of Mondrian's pure abstraction," working to leave the
least possible brush trace; he once showed Yoshihara a stark blank canvas
as a work. He said he never understood Yoshihara's doctrine that matter
repels spirit. Where Gutai's mainstream pushed the body into the paint,
Kanayama pushed the body out: he was after surfaces, space and time, his
own words from 1957, inseparable from each other. His long-running interest
was the relation between things, not the things themselves. Later decades
took him into music and astronomy.

The Machine Drawings began in 1957, after he joined Gutai in 1955 through
Shimamoto's invitation (Zero-kai, founded 1952 with Shiraga, Murakami, and
later Tanaka, came in as a group). The setup, photographed mid-session in
1957: a battery-powered children's toy car, modified, with a cylindrical
paint tank mounted on top. The tank had a hole in the bottom, or the cans
were punctured, so paint dripped as the car moved. Sheets of paper, later
white vinyl ("most appropriate for his purpose," per Kusahara), laid on the
floor inside a low wooden barrier. The finished sheets ran about 180 by 280
cm. Kanayama stood at the rim with a remote box and steered. Kusahara's key
sentence: its trajectory and the resulting traces of ink were never under
the perfect control of the artist. He tested crayons, markers, and black
and color ink across runs; quick-drying paint was the main vehicle. Tiampo's
formulation is the sharpest: the machine created paintings whether or not
the artist was even in the room. His own joke was that as Gutai's secretary
he had so much paperwork he had no time to paint, so he let the toy car do
it.

The concept was removal. The 2024 Fergus McCaffrey centennial exhibition
Plus-Minus (Feb-Apr 2024, Tokyo then New York) framed the thesis directly:
Kanayama spent his Gutai years removing the artist's gesture while Shiraga
spent his amplifying it. Kato's line on the machine works: the traces of
the movement of the machines record the passage of time, a shift in the
space or physical principles, rather than the artist's subjectivity. Against
automatism and against Pollock's drip as soul-work, this was not a gesture
amplified, it was a gesture deleted. Tapié's 1957 discovery of Gutai then
buried the point: shown in Europe as Informel all-over painting, the Work
series was read as a Japanese Pollock, and the questions about originality
and the role of technology that the machines were actually asking got
disregarded. Kusahara again: he used a mechanical medium and chance
operation instead of directly employing one's own body, as Shiraga and
Murakami did.

## The date question, stated carefully

Some sources date the car to 1955. The cyberneticzoo note, the most careful
account found, says it can only be traced to the 3rd Gutai exhibition, the
museum show in 1957 (the 1955 exhibition was outdoors), and that Avant-Garde
in Exhibition offers no proof for the earlier date. More important for
honesty: the claim that Kanayama's machines "prefigure Tinguely's
Metamatics" is wrong on two fronts, because Tinguely's own drawing machines
began in 1955 too (Machine a Dessiner No. 1 at Le Mouvement). Two parallel
inventions, not a lineage. Keep this correction in the notes.

## The technique, from the pixels

What the two finished Works actually look like:

1. Line weight is constant everywhere. A hole drips paint; there is no
pressure, no tapering, no thick-thin modulation of a wrist. This is the
single most important visual fact, and the cleanest way to tell a
Kanayama from a Pollock at full resolution. Pollock's skeins swell and
pinch; the car lines never do.

2. The tangle is all-over but not uniform. One inspected Work has a dense
black center mass with red, yellow, and blue mostly at the edges; the other
is a blue field with dark scribble concentrated in the margins. Coverage is
time: darker means the car spent longer there. Layers are runs, one can at a
time, and the overlap order is part of the piece.

3. Small loop-de-loops punctuate the field. The car spins on its axis when
the operator over-steers; paint piles into tight whorls. Between them are
longer looping traversals across the sheet.

4. Margins stay cleaner than the interior. The wooden barrier keeps the car
off the rim, and the operator steers back inside, so there is a faint
turning zone at the edges. The blue Work shows it clearly: a cleaner band,
then the scribble field.

5. The surface matters. Vinyl takes quick-drying paint with no bleed, so
lines sit sharp and colors layer without sinking. On paper the same runs
would feather.

## What the re-render taught

The local simulation modeled the honest mechanism: discrete remote commands
(forward, left, right, stop) with brown-noise slip, an under-actuated toy
car that spins on its axis during hard turns, constant-width hole-drip
paint, pooling dots when the car stalls, operator barrier-avoidance, one
color run per layer. Two passes. The first read as too straight and clean:
the fix was tighter spins, slower yaw, and center-biased wandering. The
second reads convincingly like the mechanism behind the real Works: even
thin lines, loop-de-loops at turn events, cleaner margins, pooled dots at
stalls. It is a stand-in of the described recipe, not his machine, and the
real sheets are denser at the center than this render manages.

## What sings

The ruthlessness of the premise: every mark on the sheet is evidence of a
trajectory, not a feeling. The best moments in the inspected Works are where
the mechanism shows through the all-over field, the tight whorl where the
car spun, the faint boundary band where the operator lost and regained
control, the pooled dot where it stalled. Composition happens by accident
of coverage, and the accident is honest because the constraint is real.

## Avoid-list additions

- Hand-jitter as a stand-in for machine error. The car's imperfection is
kinematic (delay, slip, under-actuation), not hand tremor. Faking it with
noise on a polyline looks hand-made; the real signature is a path a vehicle
could actually drive.
- Pollock cosplay. All-over tangle is not enough; without constant line
weight and the vehicle signature it just reads as drip painting.
- Claiming Kanayama prefigures Tinguely. The dates do not support it; say
parallel invention.

## The rest of the practice (context, not the study's focus)

The stage works run the same idea on bodies and space: Giant Balloon (1957
Gutai on Stage), inflated to fill the stage, lit in colors, punctured with
a knife and left to shrink; Biological Balloon (1958), a floating
appendaged form evoking unknown creatures. Earlier: Work B (1955), an
unfixed red ball on a 7 by 7 meter board at Ashiya Park, animated by wind
and passers-by (celestial balance was his stated subject); Footprints (1954
/ shown 1956), a 100-meter vinyl path of shoe-sole marks through a pine
grove, the walk made visible; Clock (1956), a house clock with
counterclockwise hands; Alarm, a borrowed railroad crossing alarm sounding
in a park with its red light on and off. He left Gutai in 1965 over
differences with Yoshihara, concretely over Yoshihara's treatment of
Tanaka. He discovered Tanaka earliest, brought her into abstraction, gave
the technical help behind Electric Dress and Work (Bell) (1955); she named
him her strongest influence.
