# Adam and Eve Expelled from Paradise, Ensor 1887: the expulsion as weather

Deep single-work study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted.
Named as the first next doorway by the 2026-10-06 James Ensor study
("Adam and Eve Expelled from Paradise 1887 as its own study"). The
quality-first choice: the painting stages a biblical event as a weather
event, not a figure event, and it is the clearest single-canvas statement
of the quiet-subject thesis the Ensor study carried. KMSKA Antwerp, inv.
2072, oil on canvas, 205 x 245 cm (pubhist) vs 206 x 245 cm (Bridgeman,
the Royal Academy "Intrigue" education pack, one centimeter, likely a
measurement or rounding difference, unresolved), signed and dated lower
left "Ensor 1887" (Tricot 2009 no. 287). The French title "Adam et Eve
chasses du Paradis terrestre" comes from the 1910 Rotterdamsche
Kunstkring exhibition record (DoME catalogue no. 17); the Dutch title in
the same record carries an apparent period spelling variant, and the
French form is well supported rather than certain. Provenance is a gap:
only the 1910 Rotterdam appearance is documented, no acquisition date,
donor, or earlier owner found. A research dossier on the painting's
biography and reception was delegated in-session (text-only, 2026-10-06,
zero em dashes; in the evidence dir); its key findings are folded in
below, all visual inspections, the tonal measurements, the four detail
crops, and both re-render families are mine.

## What the painting is

Painted in 1887, the year his father and his maternal grandmother died,
and the same year as The Tribulations of Saint Anthony (MoMA, Tricot
288, the catalogue raisonne neighbor). On the deaths: the standard
reading (McLellan on the 2009 MoMA retrospective) ties them to the
macabre turn, the skulls and skeletons of 1888-89; no scholarship found
links grief to THIS painting, and the documented evidence points at
light, not death, so no such claim is made here. Période claire work:
full-spectrum impasto, wet on wet, no shading anywhere. The title in
the KMSKA record is the Dutch "Adam en Eva uit het paradijs verjaagd".
The painting was included in Luc Tuymans's 2016 Royal Academy
"Intrigue" survey, where the artsdesk review singled it out: "the pair
fleeing through the Belgian countryside, the sky ablaze with divine
fire". (The artera.ae AI-generated page calls it watercolor; it is oil
on canvas, the page flagged as unreliable.)

The career placement, from the KMSKA's 2024 exhibition narrative and
the Prestel catalogue essay (Herwig Todts, ed.): the Oyster Eater (1882)
is the summit of the dark period; the winter 1886 Les XX salon, where
Ensor showed his dark work next to Monet, Degas, Renoir, and Pissarro,
is the crisis; the 1885 series of six large Christ drawings is the
laboratory where he finds the vibrating line that suggests light
suffusing objects; then 1887, Adam and Eve and the Saint Anthony works,
the first full-scale paintings of the new adventure, paint straight
from the tube, the earthy shadows and the beige-grey intermediate tones
of the underdrawings gone for good. Todts's formulation is the sharpest:
"It is not until Adam and Eve Expelled from Paradise (1887) that Ensor
linked Redon's hallucinatory images with the technique and palette of
Monet." The KMSKA hangs the painting with The Oyster Eater and The
Intrigue as one of three milestones in its permanent Ensor galleries.

The April 1887 letter, the dossier's most compositionally important
find, quoted by KMSKA: "I hardly leave the house lately... I had
started work on Adam and Eve Expelled from Paradise, now it has become
The Angel Announcing the Birth of Christ to the Shepherds. This
indecision is horrible. How the painting will end up, I don't know. I
am satisfied with the sky, which sparkles." And: "The angel has left an
emeraldgreen trail there." The subject was unstable in his mind; the
two scenes share almost nothing except an angel arriving in a blaze of
light. The Bible story was a pretext. What held the painting together
was the spectacle of light itself, which is exactly the museum's
conclusion: the painting "is essentially a study of light".

The borrowings, from KMSKA research: the tiny-figures-under-a-vast-sky
structure comes from John Martin's 1827 Paradise Lost lithographs;
the two figures are modeled on Rembrandt's etchings; Gabriel's pointing
gesture comes from Michelangelo's Creation of Adam; the lightning sword
recalls Rubens's Last Judgement. The one thing Ensor did not borrow is
the sky itself, his own experiment in paint. The sword is dossier-
carried; I did not visually resolve it as a distinct object in the
radiating burst.

The horizon surprise: KMSKA identifies three small pink shapes on the
horizon as giant mammoths, and reads them as Ensor casting doubt on
biblical literalism at the moment nineteenth-century paleontology was
making Genesis harder to take literally. I inspected the horizon zone
and confirm small pinkish incidents exist at the horizon (visible in
crop-mammoths.jpg); the mammoth identification is single-source and is
not independently verified here.

## Visual inspection

Source: the KMSKA 2072.002 scan on Wikimedia Commons, 5000 x 4193, aspect
matching the 245 x 205 canvas, CC0 via Art in Flanders, carried as
authoritative. Five crops made and inspected.

- The full view: the canvas belongs to the sky. Roughly the top two
  thirds is one luminous worked field of pink, pale yellow, cream, and
  light blue impasto, laid in short hatched strokes. Below it sits a dark
  band of brown and green ground. The two figures are tiny, lower right,
  nearly dissolving into the ground. The proportions are the composition:
  heaven is the weather of the picture and the event is what the weather
  does.
- The angel (crop at 1500,600-3000,1700): not a figure but a burst. The
  body is a salmon-pink horizontal mass dissolving at every edge into
  radiating white and pink strokes; the wings are not drawn, they are
  implied by the radiating impasto. The ONE crisp human element is an
  outstretched horizontal arm pointing right, toward Adam and Eve. The
  gesture aims the whole radiant mass; without the arm the burst would be
  decoration.
- The figures (crop at 3100,2800-4300,3700): two nudes in loose warm
  impasto, one bent forward, one upright and cropped by the right edge,
  painted in the same salmon-ochre as the angel's body so the expeller and
  the expelled share a color. They are incidents in the ground, not its
  subject.
- The horizon band (full width, rows 2500-3300): the step is sharp. Above
  it the swirled pastel field; below it the dark ground with a band of
  saturated dark green (a wood, right) and two thin dark verticals at
  center-right that read as dead trees or gate posts, the only vertical
  punctuation between the blocks.
- The sky texture (crop at 3300,500-4500,1400): Turner-like impasto, short
  strokes, no flat passages, the blue-gray ground showing through nowhere.
- The horizon right (crop at 2400,2700-4600,3250): small pinkish incidents
  sit on the horizon line left of the dark green wood, consistent with the
  KMSKA mammoth identification but not confirmable as mammoths at this
  resolution; the two thin dark verticals nearby read as dead trees or
  gate posts, my reading only.

## Tonal measurements

Full-field: mean 138.8, std 34.6, darkfrac 0.009, palefrac 0.009. The
drama is all in chroma, not value: almost nothing goes near black and
almost nothing near white. Row bands: a luminous plateau bands 1-7 at
150-170 (the sky block), then a cliff at band 8 (125.7, std 32.7, the
horizon incident zone), bands 9-11 at 90-98 (the ground block). Two blocks
and a step; no middle transition, no vignette. Columns nearly flat
(133-147): the picture has no left-right value drama, the composition
works by hue and scale.

## Re-render families (PIL, single pass, schematics)

Family A: the sky-dominated expulsion grammar. A1 (species): luminous
hatched sky block owning 68% of the frame, one radiating incident with
the pointing arm, two tiny figure-blobs low in the dark block. A2
(emblem failure): figures scaled up and centered, the sky becomes
wallpaper and the piece turns into a poster. A3 (illustration failure):
the sky as an empty value gradient, figures drawn crisp, the event
reads as a landscape with people. A4 (night failure): the same scene in
vignette darks, the expulsion needing the dark to read, which is
cheating.

Family B: the angel as weather. B1 (species): the angel dissolved into
radiating strokes with one crisp arm. B2 (diagram failure): a crisp
outlined angel with a head, the weather turned into a character. B3
(emblem failure): the radiance centered as a sun disc, the pointing
gesture lost. B4 (nothing failure): no radiant center at all, figures
alone in a pastoral, the expulsion gone and nobody noticing.

Honest schematic caveat: my radiate() draws too symmetric a sunburst;
Ensor's radiance is a tangle, not a star. The symmetry is my failure,
not his. A derivative piece must earn the radiance from tangled,
overlapping marks, never from a geometric star.

## The quality thesis

- The expulsion is a weather event. The painting gives the sky seventy
  percent of the canvas and the subjects less than five; the event is
  staged by proportion, not by figure size. A derivative that enlarges
  Adam and Eve has misunderstood the picture.
- The divine figure is dissolved and given one crisp gesture. The
  angel's body is barely a body; the outstretched pointing arm is the
  only drawn element in the whole radiant mass, and it aims the weather
  at the figures. The gesture is borrowed from Michelangelo's Creation
  of Adam; everything else in the figure is Ensor's invention. One
  legible gesture inside a turbulence field carries more narrative than
  a fully rendered figure.
- Color without shading, enforced. Darkfrac under one percent: the
  ground reads dark but never approaches black, it is dark by saturated
  hue (green, brown), and the sky reads luminous but never approaches
  white. The période claire discipline is a value budget, not a mood.
- Two blocks and a step. The luminous plateau and the dark ground meet
  at a sharp horizon with no transition; the figures live at the bottom
  of the dark block, nearly swallowed. No gradient, no vignette, no
  middle.
- The expeller and the expelled share a color. The angel's body and the
  figures are the same salmon-ochre impasto; expulsion is painted as a
  separation within one flesh, not as two opposed species.
- The pretext subject is the method. The April 1887 letter proves the
  biblical scene was unstable in his mind while the sky stayed fixed:
  a generative piece can likewise hold one strong weather system and
  let the nominal subject flicker, because the weather is the actual
  content.

## What is overdone (avoid-list additions)

- The enlarged biblical figures (emblem reading). The gradient sky
  (illustration reading). Night/vignette as drama (the value cheat). The
  crisp rendered angel (character, not weather). The centered radiance
  (sun emblem, the gesture lost). Geometric star symmetry in the
  radiance (my schematic's failure). Calling it watercolor (the AI-page
  error, carried as a warning about secondary sources).

## Honest gaps

No original handled; the KMSKA scan's color balance is unverified against
the canvas. The two thin dark verticals at the horizon (dead trees or
gate posts) are my reading, not a sourced identification. The mammoth
identification is KMSKA's, single-source, not independently verified.
The sword in the angel's burst is dossier-carried, not visually resolved.
The French title form is well supported, not certain; the 205 vs 206 cm
dimension unresolved. Provenance: only the 1910 Rotterdam appearance is
documented. No contemporary reception of this specific work found. No
published technical imaging for this painting found. Schematics
single-pass, crude glyphs. The expulsion paintings of the
Masaccio/Michelangelo tradition were not compared in-session; the claim
that Ensor refuses the figure event rests on this canvas alone, with
Martin, Rembrandt, Michelangelo, and Rubens as the documented borrowings.
