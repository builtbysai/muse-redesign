The technique, stated plainly

E.A.T. is not a style and it is not a medium. It is an organization form: an engineer who says yes. Kluever's recurring move, from Homage to the Pavilion, is the same. The artist brings an impossible request; the engineer builds a device that makes the request ordinary; the device ends up being the artwork's real content. The black box diagrams Bardiot drew for the Langlois pages are the score: signal chains rendered as floor plans, with the electronics sometimes hidden in the control booth (Open Score) and sometimes sitting in plain sight on stage (Variations VII). Either way the audience is not supposed to admire the engineering. Rauschenberg's doctrine per Bardiot: all effort concentrated on the effect, not on the means, no matter how complicated the means.

Four control grammars recur across the ten performances, and each one is a portable idea:

  1. The body as switch. In Variations VII, thirty photocells at ankle level aimed at lights under the facing tables. Four performers walked the aisle between two long tables of equipment. Breaking a beam toggled a sound source: ten open telephone lines to live New York (Luchow's restaurant, the NYT press room, the ASPCA stray dog pound, the Con Edison power station, Cunningham's studio, Terry Riley's turtle tank), twenty radio bands, two TV bands, two Geiger counters, six contact mics on the platform, twelve on household appliances (blender, juicer, toaster, fan), oscillators, a pulse generator. The sources were all "in the air at the moment of performance"; the beams were the chance-operation that selected them. Locally re-rendered this session as an aisle plan view: thirty beam lines, four wandering performers, a source rack driven purely by beam breaks, an event log. What the render teaches: the composition is a sparse matrix. Nothing plays until a body interrupts light. The music is foot traffic.
  1. The game as dimmer. In Open Score, tennis racquets with contact mics and miniature FM transmitters (Bill Kaminski's build, hidden in the handle, antenna wound around the head) sent each ball impact to the Armory speakers as a loud bong. Each bong killed one of 48 arena lights. The game ended when the hall was dark. Then 500 people walked onto the court in darkness, and infrared cameras projected their heat-shapes onto three screens: the audience could only see the crowd as images. Simone Forti sang an Italian folk song while Rauschenberg carried her across the floor. The technique: map one discrete event (impact) to a monotonic resource (light), so the game has a guaranteed terminus. Play is a countdown to black.
  1. The walk as mix. In the Pavilion, the tunnel descent had handsets picking up sounds from underfloor amplifiers, so the walkway itself was the mix. In the dome, lasers flickered in patterns set by punched paper tapes while computer-sequenced electronic music mixed with the sound of shuffling feet. Kluever called this live programming versus dead programming: the pavilion was a laboratory where the materials ran and the visitors shaped their own experience, not a fixed narrative. The corridor is the sequencer.
  1. The environment as instrument housing. Paxton's Physical Things was 20,000 square feet of taped polyethylene tunnels inflated by ten fans, a 150-foot tunnel, a Forest Room with artificial grass and projected trees, and every participant carried a pocket radio receiving Robert Ashley's soundtrack. Hay's Grass Field structured itself on three parameters: amplified biological phenomena, monochrome stage elements, robotic performers. Deborah Hay's Solo used remote-controlled carts (Larry Heilos's build) carrying dancers, and gave equal visual weight to dancers, props, lighting, and soundtrack. The move: make the venue into the apparatus and let the audience wander it.

What the photos teach

The Pepsi exterior photo is the thesis in one frame. Nakaya's fog does not decorate the dome; it eats it. White triangles surface from the cloud like icebergs, and the fog makes the most technological object in the frame read as weather. The Floats on the plaza are rounded white forms at human scale, drifting at under two feet per minute, reversing when pushed: furniture that behaves like animals. The four triangular towers for Myers' Light Frame are the only hard verticals, and they read as antennae. The lesson: when you want technology to feel humane, put weather and slow animals around it. E.A.T.'s whole aesthetic is the dome half-buried in cloud.

The Armory stills are the opposite: vast black space with a hard pool of light on the floor. Deborah Hay's Solo photo shows how little was on stage, a cart-platform and a few bodies, with the hall's darkness doing the compositional work. The scale gap is the point. Kluever's account mentions the 40-meter vaulted roof as the challenge; the photos show the solution: do not fill the space, let the space frame the bodies.

The real-image dome, stated plainly

The Pavilion's interior dome was fitted with a spherical mirror (architect John Pearce's fit; Kluever published the optics in Applied Optics, 1971: "Photographic recording of some optical effects in a 27.5 m. spherical mirror"). A concave spherical mirror has focal length f = R/2. An object beyond the center of curvature forms a real, inverted image floating in space in front of the mirror, at di = 1/(1/f - 1/do). Visitors waved at their own images in the ceiling and the images waved back. Because a real image is a fixed point in space emitting rays in all directions, a visitor could walk around it and see it from their own side: the hologram effect, achieved with 1960s optics instead of lasers.

Locally re-rendered this session as two panels. Panel A: cross-section with the three principal rays (parallel through F; through C returning through C; through F emerging parallel) converging at the floating inverted image. Panel B: plan view with three visitors at different positions, each receiving rays from the same image point. What the render teaches: the magic is not the mirror, it is the fact that a real image is a place, not a picture. A place can be walked around. That is why it felt like the future.

Sound design

Variations VII treated the entire electromagnetic environment as the instrument: telephone lines as long ears, Geiger counters as randomness with a half-life, household appliances as oscillators with bad manners. Tudor's modulation means (the program's phrase) organized it without composing it. Tudor's own Bandoneon! doctrine, quoted in the program: "uses no composing means; when activated it composes itself out of its own composite instrumental nature." In the Pavilion, the interior was spatialized electronic music mixed with feet, and the approach path was a handset soundscape over underfloor amps. E.A.T. sound is always site sound, mediated: the room, the city, the body, picked up and gated.

What to steal and what to avoid

Steal: the body-as-switch matrix. A beam-break, a pressure pad, a tripwire, any binary sensor the performer's own movement triggers, mapped to source selection rather than parameter tweaking. Discrete gating reads as composition; continuous control reads as demo. Steal: the monotonic resource countdown (each hit removes one light, one sound, one beam) as a way to give an indeterminate piece a guaranteed ending. Steal: the real image as a place. Any time a reflection or projection can be made into a fixed point in space that the viewer orbits, it will feel ten years ahead of the technique. Steal: live programming, the Kluever doctrine that the system runs and the visitor shapes it, no fixed narrative. This is the ancestor of every good interactive installation.

Avoid: the forced-marriage feeling critics reported at the time. When the technology is visible as effort, the piece becomes a demo of the apparatus. Rauschenberg hid the racquet transmitters in the handles for exactly this reason. The black box belongs in the booth or inside the prop, never on a pedestal. Avoid: the Armory's six-second reverb as metaphor. A giant resonant space makes every sound impressive and every idea muddy; in a procedural piece, the equivalent is reverb or bloom applied as a default, which flatters weak sources. Avoid: mistaking the guest list for the work. The 9 Evenings publicity leaned on names; the pieces that survive in the documentation are the ones with a clean control grammar, Variations VII's beams, Open Score's countdown.
