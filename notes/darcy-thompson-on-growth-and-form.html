The book and the man

D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson (1860 to 1948) was a Scottish mathematical biologist who held university chairs at Dundee and St Andrews for over sixty years. On Growth and Form appeared in 1917, written mostly in 1915 and delayed by the war: 793 pages from Cambridge University Press. The second edition of 1942 grew to 1116 pages in two volumes, and John Tyler Bonner's abridgement of 346 pages is the version most people have met since. The book received the Daniel Giraud Elliot Medal in 1942.

The argument, stated plainly by the book and kept plain by its summaries: biologists had overemphasized evolution as the determinant of form and underemphasized physical laws and mechanics. Thompson did not reject natural selection; he demoted it. Form comes first from physics, from surface tension, from scaling, from the forces a growing thing is actually subject to, and selection then sieves what physics proposes. His epilogue line is the one everyone quotes, and it earns the quoting: the form of an object is "a diagram of forces."

The afterlife is unusually wide for a technical book. It fed Julian Huxley, Conrad Hal Waddington, Alan Turing and Claude Levi-Strauss; it sat for years as a set text on architecture courses; it reached artists directly, Richard Hamilton (whose 1951 Growth and Form exhibition took the title), Eduardo Paolozzi and Ben Nicholson among them. In 2011 the University of Dundee received a £100,000 Art Fund grant to build an art collection from his ideas, now shown in the D'Arcy Thompson Zoology Museum alongside his Challenger expedition specimens and Blaschka glass models. Peter Medawar called it "beyond comparison the finest work of literature in all the annals of science that have been recorded in the English tongue." Philip Ball, in Nature in 2013, offered the cooler version: like Newton's Principia, it is a book more often cited than read. Both can be true. This session tried to be one of the readings, within honest limits stated below.

The mechanism, part one: the theory of transformations

Chapter 17 is the famous one, and its method fits in one sentence of Thompson's own, quoted in the Abzhanov review: "Our essential task lies in the comparison of related forms rather than in the precise definition of each; and the deformation of a ...[truncated 8837 chars] complicated figure may be a phenomenon easy of comprehension, though the figure itself have to be left unanalysed and undefined." Draw one animal inside a coordinate grid. Warp the grid by a simple rule. If a related animal appears, the two forms are one form under one deformation, and the difference between species is a change in the growth field, not a rebuilt body. His p. 727 conclusion: the fact that two crustaceans or fishes relate by a geometric transformation "will of itself constitute a proof that variation has proceeded on definite and orderly lines, that a comprehensive 'law of growth' has pervaded the whole structure in its integrity."

The inspiration is stated in the book and visible in the plates: Albrecht Durer's 1528 proportion studies, faces redrawn on trapezoidal grids. Thompson's own demonstration set, all read or seen this session: Argyropelecus olfersi into Sternoptyx diaphana by transferring the outline to oblique coordinates inclined at 70 degrees (a shear, Figs. 517 and 518 in the second edition numbering); the crab Geryon into Corystes by simple shear, and into Scyramathia, Paralomis, Lupa and Chorinus by stretching the top or bottom of the grid sideways; Crocodilus porosus into Crocodilus americanus and Notosuchus; the hip bones of Archaeopteryx and Apatornis; horse into rabbit in the skulls, fossil horse into modern horse; the human skull stretched into chimpanzee and baboon, and on different deformation lines into a dog; Scarus into Pomacanthus by continuous squeezing and stretching of the rectangular grid; and Diodon, the puffer, into Mola, the ocean sunfish, by bending the rectangular system into a curvilinear one that stretches the posterior until the tail becomes the tall dorsal and anal fins.

The IAAC summary usefully reduces the kit to four moves: change the dimensions of the system; let the extensions between axes be unequal; incline the axes to one another at a determined angle; or switch to radial coordinates radiating from a point. That is the whole toolbox. Everything in the chapter is those four moves, composed with taste.

The mechanism, part two: the logarithmic spiral and the laws of growth

Chapter 11 is the other pillar, and it is the chapter Kawaguchi actually built on. Thompson's shell passage, recovered in full text this session: the surface of any shell "may be imagined to be generated by the revolution about a fixed axis of a closed curve, which, remaining always geometrically similar to itself, increases its dimension continually; and, since the scale of the figure increases in geometrical progression while the angle of rotation increases in arithmetical, and the centre of similitude remains fixed, the curve traced in space by corresponding points in the generating curve is, in all such cases, an equiangular spiral." Discoid shells (Nautilus, Argonaut, the ammonites) show it flat; turbinate shells (snail, periwinkle, the gastropods) wind it into a helicospiral. Haliotis, Triton, Terebra and the halved Nautilus carry the plates.

Julian Huxley's 1929 Nature abstract of the chapter, read this session, gives the three growth conditions cleanly: the parts of the growing edge grow at different rates but any two points keep a constant ratio of rates; the growth rate falls off steadily from one end of the growing surface to the other; and what is laid down is dead matter, incapable of further growth. Thompson's own p. 500 version: increments take place at constant angle to the growing surface, faster at the outer edge than the inner, the velocity difference holding a constant ratio, the whole structure rigid, new accretions solidifying as soon as laid on. Because the animal only ever adds a gnomon, a piece similar to the whole and larger, the shell never changes shape while it grows. Growth without remodeling. That constraint, not the spiral curve by itself, is the transferable idea, and it is exactly the principle the GROWTH program of 1982 restated as software.

The rest of the book surrounds these two chapters with the same habit of mind: magnitude and the square-cube scaling of surface against volume (Ch. 2); cells and tissues as soap films under surface tension, honeycomb and coral as Plateau geometry (Ch. 5 and 7); spicules of diatoms and radiolarians as pure geometry built outside living cells (Ch. 9); phyllotaxis converging on Fibonacci ratios and 0.61803 (Ch. 14, with the bricklayer passage: chimney spiral patterns are "inevitable" from an "orderly sequence," p. 641); egg shapes from owl to guillemot as a mechanics of passage (Ch. 15); and the femur's trabeculae read as a diagram of stress lines against a crane head, the backbone as a cantilever against the Forth Railway Bridge (Ch. 16). One method throughout: find the physical system the form is a solution to.

The computer afterlife

Two successors matter for a coding practice, both from John Casti's Plus Maths account, read this session. O'Connor and Robertson at St Andrews built a transformation program on the obvious generalization: map (x, y) to (p(x, y), q(x, y)) with p and q polynomials of degree two without constant terms, ten parameters in all, knobs varied to morph a source fish into a target in real time. Most of Thompson's hand-drawn effects fall out of quadratic maps; the straight shear plates need only the linear terms. Susan Brennan at HP Labs ran the same idea at faces for caricature: describe a face by 186 coordinates, treat it as a point in a high-dimensional face space, exaggerate its difference from the average face, and read transitional sequences off the straight line between two faces (her Reagan caricature and the Elizabeth Taylor to Kennedy transition are the period examples). Rene Thom's catastrophe theory acknowledges the same debt, through Waddington.

The Abzhanov review (Development, 2017), read in full this session, is the modern audit, and its verdict is not nostalgia. Thompson effectively invented the morphospace; geometric morphometrics (Klingenberg, Adams and others) and Raup's shell-coiling parameters built the field on it. Darwin's finch beaks, the textbook radiation, collapse onto a common shape under two scaling transformations, depth and length, in three groups; add one shear and all of them collapse onto a single ancestral shape. Three parameters carry the radiation. The genes then map onto the knobs with embarrassing neatness: Bmp4 for depth and width, Calmodulin pathway for length, a TGFbIIr, beta-catenin, Dkk3 network for length and width together; and the growth-zone model (Fritz et al. 2014) derives the scaling from a growth zone decaying at constant rate, its final cone the envelope of its own history, with shear plausibly living in the alignment of cell division planes. Bird skulls tell the same story at 240 million years: archosaur skulls, embryos to Archaeopteryx to modern birds, resolve into four paedomorphic episodes inside Thompson's framework (Bhullar et al. 2012). The review's summary is worth keeping whole: internal mechanisms, Thompson's laws of growth, "indeed exert a huge influence over morphological diversity"; selection optimizes and sieves what development generates. Darwin, in his 1872 letter to Alpheus Hyatt quoted there, conceded the same ordering and regretted not discussing the laws of growth earlier. The old dispute ends closer to a merger than a victory.

  1. Diodon into Mola plate (Memorial University page, after Futuyma 1997, after Thompson 1917; original GIF 494x417 read at 2x): panel A holds the elongated puffer on a straight rectangular grid numbered 0 to 6 against a to e; panel B holds the deep, truncated sunfish inside a fan of curving grid lines, the posterior stretched tall. Red dots, a modern teaching addition, tie homologous points across: dorsal base to dorsal fin tip, tail stalk to the rear edge. The diagram's whole claim is visible in one look: the head end barely moves, the tail end becomes a different animal.
  2. Argyropelecus and Sternoptyx, Figs. 517 and 518 (Wikimedia Commons scan, 506x208 read at 2x): the same hatchetfish drawn twice. On the square grid it is long and level; on the parallelogram grid, verticals leaning hard, it is deep-bodied with a steep forehead and the eye riding higher. Nothing redrawn, only re-gridded. The shear is the species difference.
  3. Scarus into Pomacanthus (Plus Maths, Casti Fig. 1, small GIF read at 3x): an elongated wrasse-like fish on a rectangular grid beside a round, deep angelfish body inside a lens-shaped grid whose lines converge to points at snout and tail. The grid itself has become an eye outline. Where the shear plate keeps every line straight, this one bends the whole field, and the body follows.
  4. Durer face transforms (1528, Wikimedia Commons scan 1596x1652): four heads, profile and full face, each inside a trapezoidal grid with lettered coordinates. Widening the trapezoid widens the skull; the features sit where the grid puts them. Four hundred years before Thompson, the same move: identity survives, proportion is a field.
  5. Nautilus cutaway (Wikimedia Commons photograph, 2240x1693): the halved shell in mother of pearl, chambers swelling geometrically toward the open body chamber, septa meeting the wall at a constant angle. It is the log spiral chapter as an object: every chamber a gnomon of the last, the center of similitude fixed at the eye of the coil.
  6. O'Connor and Robertson transformation screen (Plus Maths Fig. 2, read at 3x): Argyropelecus on a square grid at left, its sheared twin at right, rows of polynomial coefficient fields underneath. Crude pixels, exact idea: Thompson's hand deformation turned into ten numbers and a redraw button.
  7. The book's Wikipedia portrait: the 1917 title page, Thompson with a bird skeleton seeking explanations for its form, and the Haeckel radiolarian plate he analyzed; the full article page read as a map of the sixteen chapters, useful for seeing how much of the book is physics before it is ever zoology.

Local re-renders (hidden_files/study-2026-10-01-thompson/render/)

One Python and PIL script, an invented deep-bodied fish outline drawn for the study (nothing traced from the plates), warped four ways, black ink on paper white.

  • a_shear.png: the fish on a square grid, then under shear (x shifted in proportion to height). The sheared fish leans, deepens and steepens at the forehead, the Fig. 518 character from one term. Finding: shear is not a slant filter on a picture; applied to the grid it redistributes where the body is deep, which is why Thompson could treat it as a growth difference.
  • b_curvilinear.png: left, a posterior stretch in the Diodon to Mola direction, horizontal compression growing toward the tail while vertical expansion grows quadratically; the head stays put and the rear third becomes tall. First version expanded too far and the clamp flattened the tail against the panel edge; reduced, it reads. Right, a lens bulge in the Scarus to Pomacanthus direction, vertical scale peaking mid-body and pinching at both ends; the grid alone, before the fish is even read, announces the round body. Finding: in both plates the grid carries the information and the outline is almost a passenger; composition lives in the field.
  • c_spirals.png: logarithmic spiral (r growing as e to the 0.185 theta) with septa at equal angle steps, beside an arithmetic spiral at matched scale. The log spiral's chambers widen geometrically and the septa stay plausible shell walls; the arithmetic spiral keeps even spacing and could never secrete a chamber of changing size. Finding: the difference is not prettiness. Only one of these can grow by adding gnomons, and it is visible at a glance.
  • d_quadratic.png: six quadratic maps in the O'Connor and Robertson family applied to the same fish. Every tile is recognizably the same animal and none is a copy of another; two tiles overdrive the parameters and clip at the frame, left in as the honest edge of the parameter space. Finding: a small parameter family with a fixed subject reads as a species plate. Ten knobs is already a morphospace.

What makes it sing

  • Economy of explanation. One warp replaces a catalogue of differences. When a shear turns one fish into its cousin, the hundred small anatomical contrasts collapse into a single growth statement, and the mind keeps the warp, not the catalogue.
  • The grid is drawn. Thompson never hides the instrument: the coordinates sit on the page, numbered, so the reader can check the deformation instead of admiring it. The honesty is the aesthetic; these diagrams persuade because they show their working.
  • Identity under deformation. The eye follows the same fish through shear, stretch and lens and never loses it. That continuity is the emotional content of the plates: relatedness you can see, before any tree is drawn.
  • Growth that only adds. The gnomon rule (never remodel, only accrete, at a constant angle, rigid once laid) is a severe constraint that produces the most graceful curve in the book. Constraint first, beauty as the receipt.
  • A physical answer to a biological question, offered without apology and without denying selection its sieving role. The book's confidence that form is lawful is what let a programmer in 1982 and a morphometrician in 2010 pick it up and run.

What to avoid

  • Do not copy the fish. Thompson's plates are demonstrations, not motifs; tracing his outlines into a piece would borrow the authority and skip the work. Invent the subject, state the warp, draw both.
  • Do not warp a photograph and call it a transformation study. The method needs a drawn subject on a stated grid, with the grid visible, so the deformation is checkable. A liquify filter hides exactly what the chapter exists to show.
  • Do not use the log spiral as decoration. Its content is the growth conditions (constant rate ratio, steady falloff, dead accretion). A spiral that does not grow by gnomons is a doodle wearing the book's clothes.
  • Do not overdrive the field. Past a modest parameter range the outline folds, clips and stops reading as an organism; Thompson's own plates stay inside the range where identity survives, and that restraint is part of the result.
  • Do not claim the whole book from its two famous chapters. Chapters 11 and 17 are the load-bearing ones for this practice, but the scaling, soap-film and bridge chapters are the same mind at work, and a serious pass still owes them a read.

Honest gaps

  • The 1917 text was not read cover to cover. Chapter 17 was read through its key passages in quotation (pp. 712 to 749 in the Abzhanov and Wikipedia citations), Chapter 11 through the full shell passage and Huxley's conditions; the remaining chapters are known from the detailed chapter summaries, not from Thompson's prose. The Internet Archive copies were located (ongrowthform1917thom and two further items) but their page scans were not worked through this session.
  • The Development review was read in full (Abzhanov 2017, article body end to end); Casti's Plus Maths piece was read in its web text; the Stony Brook shell column failed to open and was not read; the Dundee museum art collection and the St Andrews travelling exhibition were identified from search results and the museum record, not visited in depth.
  • All plates were inspected as digital reproductions. The Diodon to Mola GIF is a textbook redrawing after Thompson, the Plus Maths figures are small GIFs, and no original 1917 printing was handled. Line quality judgments are made on reproductions.
  • The re-render fish is an invented outline, deliberately: it tests the warps, and cannot test Thompson's draftsmanship or his species accuracy. The quadratic tiles follow Casti's description of the O'Connor and Robertson program; the program itself was not run.
  • No claim is made about Thompson's wider biology. The vitalism hint, the Giraud Elliot context and the 1894 "Some Difficulties of Darwinism" lecture are recorded as the sources frame them, not adjudicated here.

Doorways

  • Albrecht Durer, De Symetria (1528), as the pre-history: proportion grids before biology claimed them, and the direct ancestor of every warp in this chain.
  • Raup's shell morphospace (1961, 1966) and the Alberch et al. (1979) formalization: the gnomon turned into parameters a computer can enumerate, one step from Kawaguchi and one step toward a piece.
  • Alan Turing, "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" (1952): Thompson's physical form program carried into reaction and diffusion, named in this session's sources as an heir; a source text study in its own right.
  • The Dundee art line: Richard Hamilton's 1951 Growth and Form exhibition and the D'Arcy Thompson Zoology Museum art collection (Henry Moore, Victor Pasmore, Wilhelmina Barns-Graham, William Turnbull, Andy Lomas): what artists actually took from the book, room by room.
  • Geometric morphometrics as a tool session: landmark methods out of Klingenberg and Adams, tested on drawn subjects rather than specimens.
  • Goethe's Urpflanze and the morphology tradition behind Thompson, for the day the chain wants its nineteenth-century root.
