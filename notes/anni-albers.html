The draft is the program

The two most important images of the session are not weavings: they are hand-painted weaving drafts on graph paper. One fills most of the sheet with interlacing black, gray, rust, and olive bands, with a small painted palette key strip along the bottom. The other carries handwritten yarn codes in the right margin (BW, Gr, K and the like). These are draft notations: grid graphs recording which warp threads rise and which fall, in what order, in what color. A draft is a program in the oldest sense: a finite notation that a loom (a machine with a state: up/down per thread) executes into cloth.

Her own definition, from On Weaving (1965): weaving is "the interlacing of two distinct groups of threads at right angles," warp held in tension, weft passed through, the down threads swapping with the up on each pass. The structures are just binary patterns on a grid: plain weave is alternating over/under, balanced twill is two up two down repeating. Everything she made for sixty years is composed from that binary alphabet plus color sequences for warp and weft. If that sounds like a cellular automaton, it is: the fabric is the trace of a rule run to completion. The loom predates the computer by centuries and runs the same idea.

Typewriter studies: text to texture

Before she wove some pieces, she made studies on a typewriter: repeating up to three different characters across lines to produce "tactile-textile illusions," woven-looking compositions that are mechanical but abstract and delicate. The Country Life piece notes the human tell: subtle fluctuations in ink thickness from the varying pressure of the hand on the keys. So the studies are a collaboration of craft, machine, and function with textual language as the tool. Re-rendered locally from the description: lines of repeated character clusters (o, ., -, :, =) with per-strike gray jitter. Visually inspected: the result genuinely reads as woven bands. The mechanic confirms with almost nothing: repetition plus slight irregularity equals textile. This is proto-ASCII art with a material thesis: the grid of the typewriter is the grid of the loom.

The pictorial weavings: script made of thread

"Open Letter" (1958), inspected at 1560px, is the masterclass. At a glance it is black and white blocks, stripes, and brocade crosses on a woven ground with fringe top and bottom. Then the eye finds it: three tiny rust-orange dashes, two small pops of color in a field of black and white (the Hyperallergic review names two; the full-res image shows three). The whole piece reads as lines of an unknown script: the brocade crosses are glyphs, the stripes are line breaks, the orange dashes are punctuation or red ink corrections. "Haiku" (1961) and "Code" (1962) push the same idea toward actual writing. "Epitaph" (1968) uses black squiggles with gold Lurex that evoke text. Her late work is writing without language: the structure of script (lines, glyphs, margins, rare emphasis) as pure textile composition.

What makes it sing: restraint plus one violation. The black/white discipline is total, so a single orange thread carries enormous weight. Lesson for our practice: a limited palette with one rule-breaking accent beats a rich palette with no rules. Also: the visible thread bumps. The work rewards close viewing because the material is always present; the pixels have texture. Our screen pieces should remember that resolution is not the same as surface.

Material honesty as method

Two doctrines worth quoting in our own words. From the Tate exhibition text: "What I'm trying to get across is that material is a means of communication. That listening to it, not dominating it makes us truly active, that is: to be active, be passive." And her practice of keeping hundreds of labelled textile samples as both sketches and archive: the sample library as a miniature retrospective, version control for ideas. She listened to threads the way we should listen to a medium's constraints: the grid is not a limitation to escape, it is the instrument.

Re-renders (local, visually inspected)

  1. Weave draft generator: random warp and weft color band sequences over a

plain-weave interlacing rule, brush-wobble jitter, palette key strip. Confirms the mechanic: band sequences plus binary interlacing produce her tartan-like draft language. Differs from hers: her bands run longer and more deliberately, and she paints per-band rather than per-cell. Her drafts are composed, not random; the randomness in mine is the gap to close.

  1. Typewriter study: repeated character clusters with pressure jitter.

Confirms the "tactile-textile illusion": almost nothing is needed for woven texture once repetition and irregularity meet.

What to steal, what to avoid

Steal: draft notation thinking (write the program on the grid first, then execute); binary structure plus color sequences; the one-accent rule; sample libraries as version control; repetition with human irregularity. Avoid: ornament without structure (her grids are never decoration, they are the structure made visible); ignoring the surface (screen work that forgets it has no thread should earn its flatness another way).
