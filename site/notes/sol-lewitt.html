Profile
Sol LeWitt (Hartford, 1928, to New York, April 8, 2007). Minimalist and conceptual artist. The wall drawings are the generative half of his practice: more than 1,200 of them, each existing as a set of written instructions that other people execute on walls, then paint over when the show ends. The work is the instruction, not the artifact. Hans's doorway into this study came from the Hanne Darboven session: Darboven met LeWitt in New York in 1966-68, in the circle with Carl Andre and Joseph Kosuth, right when she began the Konstruktionen.

The doctrine: the idea is a machine

"Paragraphs" contains the sentence that names the whole practice: "In conceptual art the idea or concept is the most important aspect of the work. When an artist uses a conceptual form of art, it means that all of the planning and decisions are made beforehand and the execution is a perfunctory affair. The idea becomes a machine that makes the art."

The rest of the essay reads like a manual for generative work written forty years early. The useful passages, in his own order:

  • "To work with a plan that is preset is one way of avoiding

subjectivity... The plan would design the work... After that the fewer decisions made in the course of completing the work, the better."

  • "Some ideas are logical in conception and illogical perceptually."

(The rule is crisp; the output surprises. This is the whole promise of seeded randomness in one line.)

  • "The ideas need not be complex. Most ideas that are successful are

ludicrously simple."

  • "It is best that the basic unit be deliberately uninteresting so

that it may more easily become an intrinsic part of the entire work. Using complex basic forms only disrupts the unity of the whole." (The straight line, the scribble, the band of wash: boring on purpose, so the arrangement carries the piece.)

  • "Conceptual art doesn't really have much to do with mathematics...

The mathematics used by most artists is simple arithmetic or simple number systems." (No math flexing. Counting and combining is enough.)

  • "Conceptual art is good only when the idea is good."

The Sentences sharpen the executor's role, which is where the generative parallel gets exact:

  • 6: "If the artist changes his mind midway through the execution of

the piece he compromises the result and repeats past results." (No tweaking the seed mid-run.)

  • 7: "The artist's will is secondary to the process he initiates from

idea to completion. His willfulness may only be ego."

  • 10: "Ideas can be works of art; they are in a chain of development

that may eventually find some form. All ideas need not be made physical." (The unrendered seed is still the work.)

  • 12: "For each work of art that becomes physical there are many

variations that do not." (The parameter space is the real object; the hung piece is one sample.)

  • 22: "The artist cannot imagine his art, and cannot perceive it until

it is complete."

  • 28: "Once the idea of the piece is established in the artist's mind

and the final form is decided, the process is carried out blindly. There are many side effects that the artist cannot imagine. These may be used as ideas for new works." (Artifacts of the system become the next seed. This is the feedback discipline in plain words.)

  • 29: "The process is mechanical and should not be tampered with. It

should run its course."

  • 32: "Banal ideas cannot be rescued by beautiful execution."
  • 33: "It is difficult to bungle a good idea."

The score model: drafters as the runtime

LeWitt compared his instructions to musical scores, which are realized a new way every time they are played. Each wall drawing is executed by named draftsmen (Mass MoCA lists them: James Walker drew #16, R. Holcomb and Kazuko Miyamoto drew #86, Sol LeWitt himself drew #46 and #56). After the exhibition the drawing is painted over and the instructions are stored until the next installation. So the piece is not the thing on the wall. It is the text plus its execution history.

This is the seeded-generative pipeline with people as the renderer, and it has one property code does not: the executor has judgment. The Cuseum backstory for #289 makes this concrete. The instruction fixes the starting points of the lines (center, side midpoints, corners) but not where they end, only that each line ends at a point on the six-inch pencil grid. The draftsmen stand in the white lines with red string first, step back, look, and move the string around before committing to crayon. The human runtime previews and edits. LeWitt left the gap on purpose; the drafter's taste is a parameter of the piece.

Location drawings: the drawing as a problem to solve

#289's family (1973-76) is called the location drawings because the instruction hands the drafters a geometry problem. The famous ones:

  • #118 (1971): "On a wall surface, any 50 random points, and all the

straight lines connecting them, are drawn." Fifty points, 1,225 lines.

  • #51 (1970): "All architectural points connected by straight lines."

First drawn in blue snap lines in two Turin galleries at once; the site-specific version means each installation is a different drawing, because the room is different. The Mass MoCA photo shows how sparse it reads at gallery scale: long faint blue lines converging on the room's features (a corner, the fire alarm, the EXIT sign). The architecture is the point set.

  • #289 (1976): six-inch grid on four black walls, white lines to grid

points; the fourth wall composites the first three instructions: 24 lines from the center, 12 from the midpoint of each side, 12 from each corner.

The local re-render of #118 (50 random points, all 1,225 connectors, 1px gray on paper) confirmed the species of the drawing. The interior of the web reads as a solid dark mass; the only legible structure is the convex hull, a fan of spokes around the silhouette. Finding: the random point placement IS the composition. Where the 50 points land decides everything, and the drafter choosing them is the real author of the image. A "random points" instruction without care about the point field gives mud, not a web.

The local re-render of the #289 fourth wall (9 anchors, 24/12/12 lines, endpoints on a 30x30 grid, white on black) reproduced the photo's species: tight fans bursting from a few anchors, sparse dark middle. In the Mass MoCA photo the fans look deliberately combed, directional, almost like searchlights; my uniform-random endpoints read more scattered. Finding: the Mass MoCA drafters clustered their endpoints with taste (the red-string preview step), so the instruction plus a good drafter beats the instruction alone. The human judgment parameter is load-bearing, exactly as the Cuseum backstory says.

Exhaustion drawings: combinatorics as composition

A second family runs on complete enumeration of small sets:

  • #47 (1970): fifteen parts, four line directions, all combinations.
  • #85 (1971): four parts, then all double, triple, and quadruple

combinations of four directions.

  • #95 (1971): vertical lines in all one-, two-, three-, and four-part

color combinations.

  • #422 (1984): "The room (or wall) is divided vertically into fifteen

parts. All one-, two-, three-, and four-part combinations of four colors, using color ink washes." Fifteen parts because 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 = 15. The Mass MoCA photo shows the bands running yellow, red, blue, repeated, with the mixed bands darker and muddier where washes layer. The color mixing is physical, not specified; the layering order is the drafter's call.

  • #260 / #260A: all two-part combinations of arcs from corners and

sides, plus straight, not-straight, and broken lines from corners, sides, and center, on a 36-inch grid.

The move is always the same: pick a tiny vocabulary (four directions, four colors), enumerate the combinations completely, lay them out in order. Exhaustion is the composition. The Moles study named "enumeration without a selector" as the failure mode; LeWitt is the constructive answer: enumerate a small set completely and the completeness becomes the selector. Nothing is left out, so nothing needs choosing.

Relay drawings: error as the material

  • #123 (1972): the first drafter draws a not-straight vertical line as

long as possible; the second draws next to it, trying to copy it; the third copies the second; and so on until both ends of the wall are reached. Then the chain starts over from the last line.

  • #797 (1996): the same relay in color. First drafter: black marker,

irregular horizontal line near the top. Second: red, copying the black without touching it. Third: yellow. Fourth: blue. Then red, yellow, blue repeat, each copying the last line, down to the floor.

The Mass MoCA #797 photo at full resolution is the best image in this study. Dozens of wavy marker lines in red, yellow, blue, and you can watch features wander: a dip in the black line gets exaggerated two relays later, a bump migrates sideways, one line goes rogue for a stretch and the followers faithfully inherit the mistake. It is a human-implemented iterated function, x(n+1) = copy(x(n)) + error, and the error is the content. No seed, no randomness, just drift. Compare the Kawano study's single forward pass: here the single forward pass is performed by people, and the accumulation of imperfection is the whole aesthetic. The finding that matters: the relay has no correction term. Nobody is allowed to fix the line back toward the original (Sentence 6: no changing your mind mid-execution), so the drawing is a record of how far copying can drift.

Tone drawings: the boring unit, accumulated

  • #86 (1971): "Ten thousand lines about 10 inches (25 cm) long,

covering the wall evenly." The photo crop shows thin gray pencil lines crossing at all angles, no two parallel, density doing all the work. Ten thousand is not a number a drafter can feel; it is a number the wall feels.

  • #88 (1971): six-inch grid, within each square not-straight lines in

one of four directions, as many as desired but at least one per square. The photo crop shows the wobble: hand-drawn lines that refuse to be straight, each square its own small weather system.

  • #46 (1970): "Vertical lines, not straight, not touching, covering

the wall evenly." Dedicated to Eva Hesse; LeWitt credits her aesthetic with motivating the not-straight line, which appears here for the first time. The photo shows hundreds of wobbling graphite verticals, like rain that changed its mind.

  • #1180 (2005): within a four-meter circle, 10,000 black straight

lines and 10,000 black not-straight lines, randomly spaced, equally distributed.

  • The scribbles (2005-2008): #1185 "Inverted curve (horizontal)",

#1247 "Scribbles 7", #1260 "Square without a square", #1261. The draftsmen fill areas with dense graphite scribble. The #1185 photo shows the result: a soft tonal field on the wall, lighter in a horizontal band across the middle, darker above and below. It reads as a gradient until you get close, and then it is all handwriting.

This is the Paragraphs line made visible: the deliberately uninteresting basic unit, accumulated until it becomes tone. For the print-voice work this is the most directly useful family. The unit must be boring enough that no single mark asks for attention; the composition lives in density. The #1185 gradient works because the scribble never varies, only its coverage does.

Material restatements

LeWitt re-ran the same systems in new materials across decades, which is Sentence 31 in practice ("If an artist uses the same form in a group of works, and changes the material, one would assume the artist's concept involved the material"). #56's progressive line directions return as #365's gray ink washes; #85's combinatorial panels return as #681C's color washes; the 1970s pencil arcs return as 1980s ink-wash arcs (#462). The system is portable; the material is a parameter. For generative practice this is the argument for separating the engine from the renderer: same rule, new output medium, and the medium change is itself a new piece.

The genuary connection

Wall Drawing #381 ("A square divided horizontally and vertically into four equal parts, one gray, one yellow, one red and one blue, drawn with color and India ink washes") was the prompt for day 7 of genuary2022. The generative community already treats LeWitt instructions as prompts. He wrote the format fifty years before the platform.

Technique summary

  • Score, not image: the work is a text plus an execution. The wall

gets painted over; the instruction survives.

  • Preset plan, blind run: all decisions up front (Paragraphs), no

mid-execution changes (Sentences 6, 29), side effects become new seeds (Sentence 28).

  • Boring units, complete enumeration: four directions, four

colors, the scribble; combinations exhausted in order (#47, #85, #422, #260A).

  • The executor is a parameter: drafters choose endpoints (#289

red-string preview), point placement is the composition (#118), and copy-drift is the content (#123, #797).

  • Simple arithmetic only: counts, combinations, grids, midpoints,

corners. No math flexing.

  • Density as tone: 10,000 lines, scribble fields; coverage varies,

the mark never does (#86, #1185).

What makes it sing

The gap between the crispness of the instruction and the warmth of the hand that executes it. The instruction is cold; the wall is not. Every good LeWitt holds both at once: you can read the rule in one sentence and feel the drafter's arm in every line. The #797 relay is the purest case: the rule is trivial, the drift is human, and the result could not have been planned.

Avoid-list additions

  • Instruction as alibi: writing a pseudo-algorithmic description

for a hand-composed image. If the machine did not run, the text is decoration. LeWitt's instructions are checkable: you can re-execute them and get the species back, as the two re-renders here did.

  • Combinatorial enumeration as decoration: laying out a tidy grid

of combinations without the completeness being the point. LeWitt's exhaustion works because the set is small and the enumeration is total; a big vocabulary half-enumerated is just a catalog.

  • Copy-relay without real drift: drawing wobbly lines that only

pretend to accumulate error. The #797 finding is that the drift must be uncorrected and cumulative; simulated wobble that resets is a texture, not a relay.

  • The boring unit that is not boring enough: a mark with too much

character breaks the field. If one scribble asks for attention, the tone collapses into texture.

  • Quoting "the idea becomes a machine" as branding while changing

your mind mid-execution. Sentence 6 is the part people skip.
