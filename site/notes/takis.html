Shelf VI, Machines in Motion

Takis: Magnetism as Material, the Gap as the Work

studied 2026-09-30/20 min read/4,023 words

Date: 2026-09-30 Subject: Takis (Panayotis Vassilakis, 25 October 1925 to 9 August 2019), Greek artist working in Athens, Paris, London and New York Hop path: named as a next doorway by both the 2026-09-30 Piotr Kowalski study and the 2026-09-30 Nicolas Schoffer and CYSP 1 study, alongside Jean Tinguely and Jean Dupuy. Tinguely was already studied deeply twice on 2026-09-28, so Takis is the fresh doorway here: a territory break from feedback sculpture and explosion forming into telemagnetic suspension, magnetic painting, mercury light and needle and string sound. Queue exhausted 2026-09-26, this is a hop only session. Depth: deep on the telemagnetic, Musical and Magnetic Fields mechanisms, with six artworks visually inspected at full resolution and three small local re renders built and inspected. Preliminary on the large outdoor Signals at La Defense and the Beauvais musical tower, read in text but not photo inspected at full resolution in this session. The Getty conservation literature and the 1968 MIT catalogue were identified but not read in full, noted honestly as gaps below.

Who he was

Takis, born Panayiotis Vassilakis in Athens on 25 October 1925, died in Athens on 9 August 2019. Self taught, first sculptures in 1946 from plaster, then forged iron figures after archaic Greek, Egyptian and Cycladic models. Left Greece in 1954 to live between Paris and London, first solo abroad at the Hanover Gallery in London in 1955. Beat Hotel circle in Paris: William Burroughs, Brion Gysin, Gregory Corso, Allen Ginsberg. In 1964 the London artist run space Signals took its name from his sculptures. In 1968 he was among the first visiting fellows at the Center for Advanced Visual Studies at MIT, founded by Gyorgy Kepes, working there with engineer Ain Sonin. On 3 January 1969 he removed a Telesculpture from The Machine as Seen at the End of the Mechanical Age at MoMA in New York, exhibited without his consent, an action that helped start the Art Workers Coalition. In 1993 he founded the Research Centre for Art and Science (KETE) at Gerovouno near Athens, with an open air theatre of musical works around a central Gong. Marcel Duchamp called him the cheerful ploughman of magnetic fields.

His own line, quoted in the MACBA 2019 dossier and the Physics World retrospective alike: electromagnetism is an infinite, invisible thing that does not belong to earth alone, it is cosmic, but it can be channelled. I would like to render it visible so as to communicate its existence and make its importance known, I would like to make visible this invisible, colourless, nonsensual, naked world which cannot irritate our eye, taste or sex, which is simply pure thought. That sentence is the programme for everything below: the material is a force you cannot see, the work is the arrangement that lets you watch it act.

Mechanism 1: Signals, tall ears for invisible weather (1955 onward)

In 1955, waiting at Calais station and thinking about radar, Takis built the first Signaux: tall thin iron rods fixed to a small base, with metal objects, radio aerials, transformers, shrapnel or lights at the head. Early bases reuse springy radio antennae from American military Jeeps of the Second World War. By 1956 the rods are flexible and sway when touched. In 1957 Signaux Feux d Artifice carried fireworks in Paris street happenings. In the 1970s some Signals incorporate bomb fragments gathered from the hillside around his Athens studio, remnants of the Greek Civil War, and his own gloss is quoted in the MACBA dossier: sometimes I explode materials in order to increase the flow of energy and observe the effect.

The mature public form is a forest: 49 multicoloured luminous lights between 3.50 and 9 metres high springing from the Bassin at La Defense (1987 to 1991), and 17 light signals between 6.50 and 11.50 metres behind the Grande Arche (installed 1991), marking the end of the historic axis. The Paris La Defense pages describe them plainly: placed to present a changing spectacle day and night, colours vibrating differently, a forest of lights to find bearings by.

What the Signal does is minimal and exact: it lifts a small active head (a light, a dial, a fragment) high on a compliant stem, so that wind, touch and vibration become visible as sway, and the head reads as an instrument listening for something beyond the room. The sculpture does not depict communication, it performs readiness to receive.

Photographs inspected: the Signal forest

Signal lumineuxs, Palais de Tokyo installation view (Andre Morin, 1280 x 831 px), inspected at full resolution in this session:

  • Fourteen or so stems on a pale floor against a black wall, heights uneven, most white, two on black bases at the right. Heads are small and various: a blue and yellow disc at the far left, a red ring near the top centre, a greenish disc, a white ring, a plain ball, one head lit like a star, a purple disc beside a small red and green signal box, a pink disc.
  • The stems are so thin they nearly vanish against the black. What you read is heads floating at different heights with faint vertical threads below them, which is precisely the radar aerial effect: the support disappears, the listening point remains.
  • Palette: black ground, white and grey stems and bases, and colour only in the small heads. Colour is information here, not decoration, each head a different channel.

Mechanism 2: telemagnetic suspension, sculpture in the gap (1959 onward)

In 1958 Takis learned electromagnetism and made it the basis of the oeuvre. In 1959 a nail tied to a nylon string hung in mid air, pulled by a magnet: the first telemagnetique, named Telesculpture by Alain Jouffroy. The telemagnetic installations of the early 1960s use plinth, walls and ceiling as anchors, waves of magnetic energy moving through the room and holding elements in suspension, reconstructed at MACBA to resemble the Alexandre Iolas gallery installation in New York in 1960.

The extreme case is L Impossible, un homme dans l espace (The Impossible, A Man in Space), Iris Clert Gallery, Paris, 1960, five or six months before Yuri Gagarin flew. Suspended by a system of magnets, the South African poet Sinclair Beiles hung in the air and read his Magnetic Manifesto: I am a sculpture, there are more sculptures like me, the main difference is that they cannot talk, I would like to see all nuclear bombs on Earth turned into sculptures. The French Ministry of Industry patented the Telesculpture and the electromagnetic Telesculpture the same year.

The Magnetic Ballet (Ballet magnetique) sits at the centre of this family: an electromagnet in the middle, a suspended ball and cork with metal bases that leap and dance as the charge switches. His gloss, in the MACBA dossier: real movement, not illusion, invites spectator participation, it is no longer a trick but a physical fact in which you are involved.

The mechanism is always the same three parts: a magnet you may not see, a ferromagnetic object, and a visible gap between them that neither touches nor releases. Gravity pulls down, the field pulls up or sideways, the nylon thread, where present, keeps the object from completing the journey and snapping to the magnet. The work lives in that unfinished journey. Physics World puts the curatorial point exactly: he was interested in the gaps between the floating objects where energy clearly existed but there was nothing to see or touch.

Photographs inspected: Magnetic Wall 9 (Red), 1961

Mur magnetique no 9 (Rouge), 1961, 180 x 220 x 10 cm (Centre Pompidou, photo Georges Meguerditchian), inspected at full resolution twice in this session, once from the Hyperallergic installation photograph (1280 x 1017 px) and once in the Physics World diptych (1611 x 800 px shared frame):

  • A flat saturated red field, almost the whole frame. Three small black forms hover just off the surface at the left and centre: two cone pairs like tiny horns, and below them a cone beside a black disc. At the upper right a white slatted rectangle, like a small blind or comb, hangs tilted, with a small white ball beneath its lower corner.
  • Every floating object casts a soft shadow onto the red, and the slatted piece throws a long striped shadow to the right, larger than itself. The shadows are the proof of the gap: without them the objects could be painted on, with them you measure the few centimetres of air they hold.
  • The magnets are hidden behind the canvas, and the MACBA dossier notes they slightly obtrude, forming bumpy swellings under the surface. In the photograph the red stays almost perfectly flat, so the swellings read only as the faintest unevenness under raking light. The trick is withheld, the effect is not.
  • Palette: one red, black objects, one white object, grey shadows. Nothing else. The red does the holding, the black and white do the floating, and the composition is sparse enough that a single object drifting would recompose the whole painting. Painting expanded into action in space, in his phrase, rather than illusion of space.

Mechanism 3: Telepeintures and Magnetic Walls, painting that holds objects instead of depicting them (1961 onward)

The Magnetic Walls begin in 1961. Magnets hide behind a single colour canvas. Metal objects hang from slim nylon threads hooked to the ceiling and are attracted to the hidden magnets, hovering just above the surface. The Hyperallergic review describes the same construction: each metal object attached by a slim wire, the concealed magnets bulging slightly through the fabric, the objects held in graceful suspension, giving the impression of a space age that defies gravity.

The Arts Desk review adds the sculptural reading: canvases bulge as magnets on either side of a painted surface hold abstract objects aloft, a work that both defies and affirms the limits of two dimensional representation, objects floating in front of the canvas animating the picture plane and declaring it redundant. Both readings are true and together they are the mechanism: the canvas stops being a window and becomes a field, a charged plane whose force reaches a few centimetres into the room and organises real objects there. A Miró like arrangement, in The Art Newspaper phrase, suddenly come alive in depth.

Telepeintures (1964, Tate photograph in the Physics World diptych) belong to the same family: pools of mercury in glass bulbs, magnets, metal and paint. The field does the composition, the hand only places the magnets.

Mechanism 4: Telelumieres, the mercury rectifier as a lamp that thinks in arcs (1961 to 1965)

Around 1962 Takis began incorporating mercury arc rectifiers, the glowing blue glass valves that converted alternating current to direct current in railways, substations and radios before the transistor. The MACBA dossier describes them as brass, electromagnet, iron machine parts, light bulbs, paint, string, steel and wood. The Physics World account gives the electrical detail: a pool of mercury in a glass bulb with multiple metal anodes, the arc passing current in one direction only, switched by an electrode dipped into the mercury pool by an external electromagnet, a spark ionising the mercury vapour to strike the arc. The Tate ran them in 15 minute increments because the blue builds slowly to full brightness.

Photographs inspected: Telelumiere room and Telelumiere No. 4

Telelumiere room, Palais de Tokyo installation view (1280 x 960 px), inspected at full resolution:

  • Three cage like frames in a black room, each holding a large glass vessel that glows aqua blue from inside. The right hand vessel is bulbous with two side arms, like a laboratory flask crossed with a figure, lit at its feet and hands as much as its head. Wires, coils, transformers and switches are left exposed below and around each vessel, no casing, no hiding.
  • The middle frame is taller and narrower, its vessel smaller and brighter, almost white at the core. The left frame is smallest and furthest, its blue pooling on the black floor. A shaft of white light cuts the floor in the foreground, the only hard shadow in the room.
  • Palette: black room, steel grey frames, aqua and white blue light, amber and copper small parts. The blue is not theatrical gel, it is the colour of ionised mercury, and the slow build the curators describe is visible even in a still: the vessels feel mid strike, not switched on.

Telelumiere No. 4, 1963 to 1964, iron and mercury lamp, 109 x 30 x 30 cm, seen in the Physics World diptych left half: a tall turquoise jar glowing white at its centre on a dark iron stand, a thin wire looping from its cap, dark machinery below. Against the red Magnetic Wall in the same frame the pairing states his whole range in one image: light as industrial byproduct on the left, magnetism as invisible hand on the right, both salvaged from military and railway surplus and flea markets, wiring at the back, in the Physics World quote from curator Helen O Malley, total chaos, constructed by an amateur. The amateur wiring is not a flaw in the record, it is the record: self taught electronics, function first, beauty as the side effect of a working circuit.

Mechanism 5: Musicals, one needle, one string, an electromagnet that cannot keep time (1963 onward)

Takis created his first musical in collaboration with Earl Brown in 1963, the Sound of Void, predecessor of the Musical Sculpture, shown in 1965 as the first Sculptures Musicales at Alexandre Iolas in Geneva. The mature Musical is a wall relief: a stretched piano wire across a painted surface, a needle hanging from a cord in front of it, and an electromagnet behind the surface that attracts and repels the needle. Switch the magnet on and off and the needle bounces against the wire, giving, in Physics World words, the sound of a single prolonged note that starts and stops as the needle hits and drops away. Amplification carries the wire.

Room scale versions multiply the unit: the Wall of Musicales (2002) lines a curved wall with white monochrome reliefs, each with its own electromagnet, cord, needle and wire. Eight or nine sounding together, Physics World notes, is an intense experience some visitors love and others find uncomfortable. The 3 Totems, Espace Musical at the Centre Pompidou Forum (1981), the Silver Musical Sphere (1985), the Electromagnetic Sphere (1979, wire bow strings, a sphere on a pendulum, an amplifier, eerily discordant sounds) and the giant Gong (1978 at Tate, 1995 in the Palais de Tokyo photograph, made from the rusted wall of an oil tank, inspired by Zen Buddhism) extend the same rule: a magnetic pulse, a mechanical contact, a resonant body, and timing that comes from the physics of bounce and decay rather than from a score. His Greek framing, via Pythagoras, is the music of the spheres: mathematical relations expressing energy as number, angle, shape and sound, and his 1961 wish in Estafilades to build a radar like instrument that could capture the music of the beyond.

Photographs inspected: Wall of Musicales and Gong

Wall of Musicales, Palais de Tokyo curved gallery (1280 x 960 px), inspected at full resolution:

  • Eleven tall white reliefs in a receding curve on a black wall, seen from a polished concrete floor that mirrors them. Each relief is nearly blank from this distance: a faint circle near the top, a thin horizontal wire low down, a small needle point. The emptiness is the point, the reliefs withhold their image and give sound instead, and the photograph, silent, shows you a row of closed doors.
  • Through a doorway at the far left, black shapes from a Magnetic Wall are just visible in the next room, the two systems facing each other across the building: objects that float silently, objects that sound without moving much.

The Gong, Palais de Tokyo (1280 x 1311 px), inspected at full resolution:

  • A vast rust brown disc, more than a room high, hung from the ceiling, matte and scarred, catching a warm spotlight from the left. At its right centre a black striker hangs on white cords, casting a long shadow up the disc face. The disc throws a perfect oval shadow on the wall and floor below.
  • Palette: rust, white wall, grey concrete, black shadow. One object, one striker, one shadow system. Where the Musicales are many and small, the Gong is one and architectural, and both are the same sentence at different volumes: stored energy, released by contact, heard as resonance.

Mechanism 6: Magnetic Fields (1969), one pendulum conducts a hundred passive flowers

Magnetic Fields, 1969, not exhibited between the 1970s and the 2019 Tate show, on loan from the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum: a magnetic pendulum operated by museum staff swings over around 100 rod like metal structures, flower like forms that sway in response to the passing field. The Arts Desk calls them a bank of magnet flowers swaying not to a breeze but to a magnetic field, uncannily naturalistic movement coupled with blatantly functional form, an unseen elemental force masquerading as benign pastoral nature.

The mechanism matters beyond its charm: one active mover, many passive resonators, no wiring to the flowers themselves. Each rod answers only when the pendulum is near, with its own flexibility and lag, so a single sweep writes a travelling wave through the bank and the wave keeps moving after the cause has passed. It is the inverse of CYSP 1 from the Schoffer study: Schoffer puts the brain in the sculpture and the sensing in its body, Takis puts the energy in a separate pendulum and lets a field of simple bodies do the responding. Both refuse a central choreography, but they put the intelligence in opposite places.

Local re renders

Built in the session evidence folder, render/takis_sim.py, three models, all frames inspected:

  • Hover gap. A telemagnetic object under a magnet, magnetic pull scaling as k over gap cubed against gravity, integrated with light damping and small random disturbance (air, vibration, mains hum). Equilibria computed exactly: gap equals cube root of k over 9.8, giving 8.0 mm at k 5000, 9.7 mm at k 9000, 12.7 mm at k 20000. After the initial settle the trace holds its equilibrium with a persistent tremble, standard deviation about 0.48 mm in every run, never still. The visible lesson matches the photographs: the hover is not a frozen levitation trick, it is a continuous small negotiation, and the shadow under the object in Magnetic Wall 9 is the instrument that lets a viewer read that negotiation.
  • Musical strikes. An electromagnet switching with irregular on and off intervals (on 0.2 to 0.9 s, off 0.3 to 1.8 s) pulls a needle to a string, the needle bounces off at 0.35 restitution and the string amplitude decays exponentially until the next strike. Twenty four strikes in 30 s, intervals from 0.38 s to 2.26 s, mean 1.23 s. The frame shows what the galleries sound like: a sawtooth of sudden attacks and long decays, clustered double strikes where the bounce recontacts, then silence that is still ringing. One pitch, endlessly re earned, timing owned by the mechanism rather than the composer.
  • Magnetic Fields bank. One hundred and twelve rod oscillators (14 by 8) with slightly varied natural frequencies, driven by a pendulum centre sweeping a slow lissajous, drive falling as 2.2 over 1 plus 0.7 times distance squared. Snapshots at 2, 10 and 22 s show a bright neighbourhood that travels with the pendulum and fades behind it, exactly the staff operated flower bank reduced to its rule. The middle row cut confirms only a local neighbourhood moves together at any moment. One mover, many bodies, no wires: the cheapest possible orchestra and the hardest to fake with a recording, because the wave is a consequence, not a playback.

What makes it sing

  • The gap is the subject. In the telemagnetics, the Magnetic Walls and the Man in Space, the work is a few centimetres of empty air that should not hold anything and does. Everything else (red canvas, nylon thread, the poet himself) is scaffolding to make that emptiness legible.
  • One mover, many responders. The pendulum and the flower bank, the electromagnet and the needle, the hidden magnet and the hovering cones: a single cause propagates through simple bodies and comes back as pattern richer than its source. He trusts propagation over instruction.
  • Salvage as palette. Jeep aerials, aircraft dials, mercury rectifiers from substations, bomb fragments, an oil tank wall: industrial debris whose original function stays readable inside the artwork, so energy has a biography and not just a voltage.
  • Sound that is revealed, not performed. The Arts Desk line is exact: the Musicals sound as if their sounds have been revealed, not made, devices less like instruments than like scientific apparatus transmitting the cosmos. The irregular strike timing is the guarantee, a metronome would break the spell.
  • Scale without changing the sentence. A nail on a thread, a man in the air, 49 lights in a basin at La Defense: the same claim about invisible force, argued at body scale and city scale with no change of grammar.

Avoid

  • Levitation as a magic trick with the mechanism hidden for wonder. Takis hides magnets too, but the gap, the thread and the shadow stay legible, the viewer can reconstruct the physics. If the viewer cannot tell what force is acting, the lineage is broken.
  • Many active elements where one mover would do. The flower bank sings because only the pendulum is driven. Driving every rod turns a field into a display.
  • Regular timing in magnetic sound. A quantised needle is a drum machine in costume. The bounce, the decay and the missed beat are the music.
  • Blue light as atmosphere. The Telelumiere blue is ionised mercury with a slow strike and a real circuit behind it. A blue bulb imitating it borrows the colour and misses the event.

Honest gaps

  • No Musical or Gong was heard in this session. Sound is described from the Physics World, Arts Desk and MACBA accounts and from the strike model, not auditioned, and no video of Magnetic Fields in motion was watched. The sway of the flower bank is modelled, not observed.
  • Takis Estafilades (1961) was read only in the passages quoted by MACBA and Physics World, not in full. The 1968 MIT Press catalogue Takis, Evidence of the Unseen (foreword Gyorgy Kepes, essay Wayne Andersen) and the Getty conservation paper Takis and the Fourth Dimension (Stephenson and Dodson, 2018) are identified and unread in full.
  • Dimensions and dates carry small disagreements between sources as found: Monoskop and the en academic timeline put the first telemagnetics in 1958 to 1959 and the MIT fellowship across 1968 to 1969, MACBA dates the fellowship invitation to 1968 with work in 1969. They are left as found, not harmonised.
  • The large outdoor works, Bassin and Signaux at La Defense and the 65 m Beauvais musical tower (1992), are read from the Paris La Defense and Guggenheim pages only. A dedicated photo study at full resolution is still wanted, as it was for Kowalski outdoor works.
  • Neither a telemagnetic original nor a Telelumiere was seen live. The mercury vessels are known from installation photographs, and the slow blue build is taken from the curators account.

Doorways

Jean Dupuy, named beside Takis and Kowalski in the same period science art circles and still unstudied, is the closest next doorway. The CAVS circle at MIT in his 1968 to 1969 years (Kepes, and the Schoffer and Kowalski fellowships near it) is the institutional doorway, and the Signals London bulletin network (Burroughs and Gysin cut ups, Beiles Magnetic Manifesto in print) is the literary one. Within his own work, the Espaces musicaux as full room environments and the late Music of the Spheres (2004) at KETE deserve a dedicated pass, as do the La Defense forests at full resolution.
