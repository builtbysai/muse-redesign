Subject
Cybernetic Serendipity, the exhibition curated by Jasia Reichardt at the Institute of Contemporary Arts, London, 2 August to 20 October 1968, then the Corcoran Gallery of Art, Washington D.C. (1969) and the Exploratorium, San Francisco (1969). The catalogue: Cybernetic Serendipity: The Computer and the Arts, a Studio International special issue, July 1968, second revised edition September 1968, 104 pages, cover and exhibition designed by Franciszka Themerson.
Hop path
named as the next doorway by four of today's studies (Noll, Nake and Nees, Poemfields, Julesz and Harmon). The queue is exhausted, so this session follows the hops outward. After a run of Bell Labs single-artist studies, this one studies the room they all ended up in: the exhibition as a network node, where the plotter drawings, the mobiles, the poems and the films were hung together with no labels saying who was an artist and who was an engineer.

An exhibition run like a system

Reichardt's introduction is the honest core of the show. She writes that Cybernetic Serendipity "deals with possibilities rather than achievements, and in this sense is prematurely optimistic," and that computers had "so far neither revolutionized music, nor art, nor poetry, in the same way that they have revolutionized science." No heroic claims. Then the curatorial move that still feels radical: the work was hung in three sections (computer-generated graphics, films, music and poems; cybernetic devices, environments, robots and painting machines; and machines demonstrating the uses of computers, with a history of cybernetics), and, in her words, "no visitor to the exhibition, unless he reads all the notes relating to all the works, will know whether he is looking at something made by an artist, engineer, mathematician, or architect." The labels carried the work, not the profession. Over 130 participants, nearly 60,000 visitors by the ICA's own later count, and the person Reichardt credits for the idea itself is Max Bense of Stuttgart, whose 1965 shows of Nees and Nake plotter drawings proved a gallery could hold this material. Work started in the autumn of 1965.

What survived is instructive. The machines mostly did not: SAM is a static exhibit now, the Colloquy mobiles survive in photographs and one full replica. What survived intact is the catalogue, a magazine special issue that anyone could buy for 25 shillings, and the LP. Reichardt edited the show twice, once as rooms and once as print, and the print version is the one that kept travelling. The catalogue is not a souvenir of the exhibition; it is the exhibition, in the form that could be mailed.

The centerpiece: a conversation with drives

Gordon Pask's Colloquy of Mobiles is the work the show is still remembered by, and the catalogue gives Pask two pages to explain himself. His term is the "aesthetically potent" environment: one that encourages the viewer to explore it, form concepts about it, and, in the step past passive work, lets the viewer join in. "It is a group of objects, the individual mobiles, that engage in discourse, that compete, cooperate and learn about one another." Each mobile has programmes for motion and visible state, and each "learns how to deploy its programmes in order to achieve a goal; namely, to reduce an inbuilt drive."

The protocol, assembled from Pask's text and the 2018 team's mining of his case history: two males (geometric, aluminium, emitting light, photocells above and below) and three females (organic fiberglass forms by Yolanda Sonnabend, glowing from inside, each with a mirror and a light sensor), all hanging, the computer outside the installation running the females. A male with a drive signals it in the flash frequency of his light and rotates. When his beam lands on a female's sensor, she stops and compares flash frequencies: if her drive matches his, she answers with a sound and begins oscillating her mirror. His light steadies. When the mirror angle throws his beam back onto his receptor and it holds there, reinforcement lands: his drive is satisfied and drops, hers drops by one step, and she keeps the successful mirror angle in short-term memory. Drives rebuild, so the conversation restarts on its own. Visitors could join with flashlights and mirrors, and their goals were allowed to be alien to the mobiles' goals. Pask, deadpan: "You may engage in this discourse if you wish to, though your goals may be alien to the goals of the mobiles; for example, you might be trying to achieve a configuration that you regard as pleasing."

The 2018 replica team (Paul Pangaro and TJ McLeish, College for Creative Studies, Detroit) rebuilt behavior, size and sensibility with modern electronics rather than restoring the original circuitry, and their paper is a study method in itself: they treated Pask's "A comment, a case history, and a plan" as a spec, then found the spec wrong in places (a reversed yes/no in a flowchart, positional errors in the plan and section diagrams, a misdescribed sensor location) and had to decide, error by error, what the work actually was. The Hyperallergic reviewer watched the replica for an hour without seeing one full flashing sequence complete. That slowness is not a defect of the replica. It is the tempo of the original: a conversation that takes its time is the piece.

The other machines in the room

The machines and environments section is a catalogue of feedback strategies, each one a different answer to the question of what a machine should do with a visitor.

Edward Ihnatowicz's SAM, the Sound Activated Mobile, is the direct one: an electrohydraulic spine of aluminium castings, vertebra by vertebra, topped with a fiberglass reflector carrying four microphones. The analogue circuit works out where the predominant sound is coming from and turns the column to face it. The behavior that made it a hit, per Mason and the Kinetica notes, was its preference for quiet, sustained sound over shouts: people stood in front of it for long stretches, modulating their voices, trying to hold its attention. Sensitivity as a personality trait, tuned in circuitry.

Wen-Ying Tsai's cybernetic sculptures are stainless-steel rods topped with polished plates, kept in constant harmonic motion inside a strobe-lit field. The strobe frequency is tied to an external stimulus, viewer proximity or ambient sound, so the rods appear to shift from gentle undulation to rapid vibration. Nothing moves faster; the light lies about the motion. It is the cheapest trick in the room and one of the most durable ideas in it.

Nicolas Schoffer's CYSP-1 (1956, the oldest machine in the show) runs on opposites: excited by blue, calmed by red, excited by silence, calmed by noise, excited in the dark, calm in bright light, its sixteen polychromed plates spinning on a rolling base with a Philips electronic brain. The catalogue entry reads like a temperament chart. Next to it Schoffer's unbuilt cybernetic light tower for La Defense, 307 metres of mirrors whose computer would read weather, traffic and the stock market and answer in light: the city as display surface, proposed in 1968.

Jean Tinguely's two Meta-Matic painting machines and the Meccano mechanical patternmaker make the anti-computer argument inside a computer show: four independent motions, pen and paper, gear ratios varied until, in the patternmaker's words, "all the patterns are regular and can be repeated. However, in practice, if all four motions are used at once for a pattern, it becomes so confused that the pattern appears to be random." Complexity past the point of legibility reads as chance. Gustav Metzger's Five Screens With Computer proposes the opposite extreme: five stainless screens of ten thousand elements each, ejected one by one under computer control over ten years, weather feeding the random ejection programme, until only the empty site remains. The computer as a very patient demolition scheduler.

Nam June Paik gets a catalogue essay (Norman Bauman's) that is mostly about touch: visitors pressing switches, holding magnets to the reworked television sets, turning the knob on Tango Electronique until the shimmering lines explode into patterns and, after a while, "you get a feel for it." Bauman's line is the section's thesis: the feeling of holding a magnet and seeing a striking visible result "is not chickenshit iron filings, but a real, living, breathing MAGNETIC FIELD." Paik's sets taught the field by hand. Robot K-456 and the distorted TVs were in the room too, but the essay understands that the work is the visitor's education, not the object.

Words and pictures

The poems section is smaller and stranger. Knowles and Tenney's The House builds each description from random meetings of four categories (materials, situations, lighting, inhabitants), a fragment of a programme that "would run for hundreds of houses if all the possibilities were exhausted," with the promise that one of the houses would actually be built in New York. Balestrini's Tape Mark I (1961, IBM 7070, 322 punched cards) is fully specified in the catalogue: three source texts (a Hiroshima diary, a mystery novel, the Tao Te Ching), fifteen elements, combinations of ten, head and end codes governing the chains, no two elements from the same extract adjacent. The instructions are printed next to the output, which is the right way to show this work: the poem is the procedure, the printout is one run of it.

In the graphics essay, Reichardt reports Noll's Mondrian experiment with the numbers left in: shown both the 1917 Mondrian and a computer version with the same count of bars in the same area, 59 percent preferred the computer picture, 28 percent identified the computer version correctly, and 72 percent thought the Mondrian was the machine's work. She refuses to make it prove anything: "The experiment is not involved either with proof or theory, it simply provides food for thought." That restraint, curating results without inflating them, is the voice of the whole catalogue.

Re-render: the conversation, simulated

colloquy_sim.py (kept with this session's evidence) rebuilds the protocol in plan view: two males scanning with rotating beams, flash frequency encoding drive, three females who stop and answer with sound on a frequency match, mirror sweep, reflection held on the male's receptor for a sustained count, reinforcement, drive drops, short-term memory of the mirror angle that worked, drives rebuilding. It is a study of the protocol, not of Sonnabend's fiberglass forms, and it makes one testable claim, the same one Pask made: the system should get to satisfaction faster once memory is in play.

Over 60,000 ticks the simulation produced 95 reinforcements, one sound signal per engagement. Mean time from lock to reinforcement was 28.7 ticks for episodes with no remembered mirror angle and 9.9 ticks once a female had memory to start from. The first ten episodes averaged 26.2 ticks; the last ten averaged 8.0. The learning curve Pask described, satisfaction reached with less energy as the conversation accumulates, falls out of a very small rule set. Three snapshots (ticks 2000, 20000, 45000) show the plan in the study's colors: pale female discs above, teal males below, beams white when locked and yellow when scanning, drives annotated as they fall and rebuild. What the sim leaves out is everything physical: inertia, the glow, the sound carrying across a gallery, the visitor with a flashlight. The protocol is the portable part.

What makes it sing, what is overdone

What sings: the refusal to sort people. One room, one catalogue, engineers and artists in the same typeface, and the work had to hold up without a credential next to it. Most of the pieces in the room are protocols rather than objects (a drive to reduce, a preference for quiet sound, a ten-year ejection schedule), which is why they can still be studied, simulated and argued with in 2026 while the hardware is in storage. And the catalogue understood itself as the durable edition of the show. Reichardt wrote the exhibition that could be posted.

What is overdone, then and now: the word cybernetic stretched until it covers everything from Wiener to a Meccano patternmaker to a bamboo joke computer with a Brain Drain. Reichardt knew it was a flag of convenience and flew it anyway, which was right for 1968 and is not a move available twice. The gender language of the Colloquy (males compete, females cooperate, Pask himself calls it "a whimsy") dates the piece's framing, though the underlying design, asymmetric capabilities that only work in pairs, is better than its labels. The avoid list for our own work: do not borrow the 1968 optimism as a style, do not call a piece a conversation unless something in it can actually be satisfied, and do not let the catalogue of techniques substitute for one piece done properly.

Next doorways

Gordon Pask's wider cybernetics (the conversation theory papers, the MusiColour machines that predate Colloquy), Edward Ihnatowicz's Senster (the computer-controlled successor to SAM, Evoluon 1970, revived as RE:Senster), Wen-Ying Tsai's later cybernetic sculptures, the Computer Technique Group in Tokyo (the lone Japanese exhibitors, and a bridge to the Japanese computer art thread), Robert Mallary's Quad 1 and the first computer-aided-design sculpture, and the Motif Editions print portfolio (Boeing Man, the CTG lithographs) as a study in how plotter work was editioned for walls.
