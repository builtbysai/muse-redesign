Who he was

Piotr Kowalski, born 2 March 1927 in Lwow (then Second Polish Republic, now Lviv, Ukraine), died 7 January 2004 in Paris. War refugee in Brazil by 1946, then at MIT from 1947 to 1952, Bachelors in Architecture, having first aimed at mathematics. Architect for I. M. Pei 1952 to 1953, then with Marcel Breuer at UNESCO in Paris, and with Jean Prouve on prefabricated structures, including a Sahara cabin shown in 1958. Fellow at the Center for Advanced Visual Studies at MIT 1978 to 1985. President of the Ars Technica Association in 1985. Professor at the Ecole nationale superieure des Beaux-Arts de Paris from 1987. He worked in non traditional materials throughout: electronic and mechanical devices, neon, large earth works, explosions and other natural phenomena, plant growth, gravity, and temperature.

His own line, quoted in the 2024 Espace Meyer Zafra material and the 2013 Galerie Downtown material alike: there is no dichotomy between science and life for him, they are part of the same world. And: you have to use objective things, external to art, to be free. Serge Lemoine, quoted in the same press material, calls him a major but relatively marginal figure in art history who uses technology the way a painter uses color, and who, unlike artists of the 1960s who believed science announced a new era for art, used modern technology only as a means, not an end. That distinction matters and it runs through every mechanism below.

Mechanism 1: explosion forming, forces shown at work

For Now, 1965, California State University, Long Beach, commissioned for the 1965 California International Sculpture Symposium, Kowalski shaped sheets of stainless steel with underwater explosions of dynamite. The process has an industrial name, explosion forming, and the 1965 documentary short about the work uses exactly that term. A CSULB history of the symposium collection confirms the essentials: created using explosives and stainless steel, intended to connect viewers with the universe, built from triangular sheets whose reflective surfaces bounce sunlight between sheets, and made with North American Aviation, an aerospace metal supplier of the period. That is the whole doctrine in one object: a real physical force does the forming, the artist chooses the setup and the moment, and the finished surface keeps the evidence of the event, reflecting the sky and the viewer rather than a polished idea of sculpture. The same thread runs through his 1958 company for experimental architecture and sculpture based on surface tension, his 1960 prefabricated architectural sculptures in translucent polyester with an international patent for forms issued out of elastic surfaces in tension, and the 1977 Thermocouple in Linz, a monumental heat rolled Corten and stainless steel work that changes form with ambient temperature. Force, tension, temperature: the material is never just a carrier, it is the subject.

Mechanism 2: neon as a tool for thinking, perspective and symmetry under test

The light works, mostly 1970s while he was also doing large scale projects, use neon for its strong ability to communicate, to highlight scientific, cultural, or philosophical questions. Two principles are put under objective test:

  • Symmetry, in Room, Room (1973) and Reflet, Reflet (1991). Room, Room is listed by Espace Meyer Zafra as neon, 50 x 10 cm / 120 x 40 cm, the paired dimensions already suggesting a mirrored or doubled construction. Reflet, Reflet, 1991, is neon and inox, 70 x 290 cm, edition of 3.
  • Perspective, in Perspective New York (1973 to 1997) and Perspective Dhuizon (1973 to 1997), both neon and photography on canvas, 145 x 195 cm. documenta 14 lists the Dhuizon work dated 1970, photograph on canvas with blue neon, 130 x 195 cm, held by the National Museum of Contemporary Art, Athens (EMST). The date and size discrepancy between the gallery and documenta listings is left as found here, unresolved.

Wozu (1978), translatable as whats the point, is the subjective counterpart: a word as a question, held up in light.

Photograph inspected

Perspective Dhuizon, image file supplied by the gallery at 1600 px wide (delivered as WebP bytes under a .jpg name, 1600 x 1124 px). Inspected at full resolution in this session:

  • A misty blue landscape photograph printed on canvas, a field at the bottom, trees framing left and right, pale sky at top. Hung on a white wall, photographed straight on.
  • Across the middle, a single blue neon word: PERSPECTIVE. The letters are small at the left and grow steadily to the right, so the word reads as if it recedes into the photographed depth while physically sitting flat on the canvas surface. Two white power cables drop from the neon to below the canvas edge, unhidden, part of the object.
  • Palette: deep blue field and trees, pale blue white mist, electric blue neon with a white hot core and a blue halo. The neon does not illustrate the photograph, it argues with it: the photograph has its own natural perspective of trees and mist, and the neon imposes a second, artificial perspective that is deliberately wrong in scale against the first, so the viewer holds both systems at once and feels which one their eye trusts.

That double system is the lesson. Perspective here is not a rendering technique, it is a testable claim, and the work is the test rig.

Mechanism 3: the Time Machine and disrupted time

For the Centre Pompidou exhibition Piotr Kowalski, Time Machine + Projets, 17 December 1981 to 8 February 1982, the 1981 press document describes the arc plainly, in the translations made for this study: his work runs from a small portable machine, a mold for forming arrows with fine sand, to monumental sculptures intervening in architectural programs such as La Defense and Les Halles. The exhibition opens with monitors dispersed through the building and closes with the Time Machine, then his last work. It confronts the spectator with their own perception of time and with societys television images, perturbs the linear time of those image flows, relativizes their perception, and comically dismantles the ideology driving the flows. A portable pataphysical machine, acting at the source of information. A second quoted doctrine line, to verify verbatim against the original before quoting it in public copy: acting with forces, showing them at work, choosing cases of matter developing or energy being produced, working with sand, wax, or plants as readily as with cold rolled special steel or complex electronic apparatus. That sentence, sand to special steel in one breath, is the shortest statement of his range and of why the scale jumps in his work never feel like showing off.

Large scale works, read but not yet inspected in depth

The outdoor and architectural list is long, with coordinates recorded in the reference sources: Now (1965, Long Beach), Signal, Tour de Vitry (1974), the Bimetallic Thermocouple at the Metal Forum in Danube Park, Linz, Tour de Creteil (1990), Place des Degres at La Defense and Le Grand Escalier (1991 to 1993), LAxe de la Terre (1992, Universite de Marne la Vallee), LArche de Saint-Quentin en Yvelines, Totem Holographique (1993), and in Japan the Windmill at Yamaguchi Beach, Sunflower at Daikanyama in Tokyo, the Oike Dori Fountain in Kyoto (1997), and the Electronic Tree in Morioka. These are named here from the sources read; none of them was visually inspected at full resolution in this session, and the note does not pretend otherwise. They are the clear next photo study.

Local re render

Built in the session evidence folder, render/kowalski_sim.py, three pressure steps plus a neon study, all frames inspected:

  • Explosion forming model: a clamped plate profile under a Gaussian underwater pressure field. Center deflection measured 42.0 px at step 1, 112.0 px at step 2, 196.0 px at step 3, in model units, with edge deflection staying near zero (3.3 px at the strongest step). The growth is superlinear once the field strengthens, which matches the intuition of permanent plastic set: small charges make a dimple, stronger charges make a dome that stays. The visible lesson is how much of the final form is decided by the pressure distribution rather than by any drawn outline.
  • Neon perspective study: the word PERSPECTIVE set with letter heights growing from 22 px at the left to 80 px at the right, in 11 steps (22, 27, 33, 39, 45, 51, 56, 62, 68, 74, 80). On a flat blue mist ground the word immediately reads as depth, even though nothing else in the frame agrees with it. Rebuilding it took minutes and made the originals trick literal: the eye accepts the letter gradient as perspective first, and only afterwards notices the landscape obeying different rules. That ordering, acceptance then doubt, is the experience the work is designed to produce.

What makes it sing

  • Physics as author, artist as editor. The explosion, the tension, the temperature change do the forming. Taste enters in choosing the setup, the material, and the moment to stop.
  • A principle put under test in public. Perspective and symmetry are not illustrated, they are staged so the viewer catches their own perception in the act.
  • Neon used to communicate, not to decorate. The words are questions or claims, and the light is the medium of the argument.
  • Scale without vanity. A pocket sized sand mold and a La Defense plaza belong to the same inquiry, and the work says so openly.

Avoid

  • Neon as signage gloss, or words chosen for mood rather than as a testable claim.
  • Simulating an explosion as a visual effect. If force is the subject, use a real gradient, a real material response, or do not claim the lineage.
  • Forced perspective that agrees too neatly with the ground. The interest is in the disagreement between two systems, not in a clean illusion.
  • Citing the outdoor works as studied. Until the photographs are inspected at full resolution, they stay named, not analyzed.

Doorways

Takis is the natural next doorway, named beside Kowalski in the 2024 press material as a closer relative than the luminokineticists, and still unstudied. Jean Dupuy is named in the same passage. The CAVS and Center for Advanced Visual Studies circle at MIT in his 1978 to 1985 years, and the Ars Technica association he presided over in 1985, are the institutional doorways. Finally, the outdoor works themselves: LAxe de la Terre and Place des Degres deserve a dedicated photo study at full resolution.
