Machines in Motion

David Bowen

Artist
David Bowen, Minneapolis. Kinetic sculptor building sensor-driven drawing machines and tele-present devices out of robotics, custom software, and living systems.
Doorway
hop from the Patrick Tresset study, continuing the drawing-machine lineage backward into the autonomous-device branch. Tresset's robots draw what they see; Bowen's devices draw what they sense, or re-enact what a distant sensor felt. The other pole of machine drawing: data and living processes as the pilot.

The thesis in his own words

From his about statement: his devices play both the roles of observer and creator, giving limited mechanical perspectives on dynamic living situations, and the outputs are collaborations between the natural form, the mechanism, and the artist. The mechanisms are often trying, futilely, to simulate a natural form or system, and when they fail to replicate it the result is a completely unique outcome. These unpredictable occurrences are what he finds most fascinating.

Observer-creator devices

Every Bowen piece fuses sensing and making into one body. The infrared drawing device carries four IR sensors on spindly legs and moves a drawing arm in real time as people move in front of it. The fly drawing device lets houseflies into a chamber; sensors track their movements and a microcontroller articulates a drawing arm from them. When no fly is detected, the paper scrolls over and the device waits. The sensor and the pen are the same creature.

Highly qualified data

His phrase from the interview, and it is the key to the whole practice: the devices collect highly qualified data, shaped by their construction and by how the device and the participant respond to the situation. The accelerometer on an outdoor dried stalk only knows wind as sway. The fly chamber only knows the fly as light-pattern changes. The artwork is the data exactly as the body permits it, and he is explicit that the embodiment is the point. A cleaner sensor would make a worse piece.

Reciprocal loops

The growth rendering device is the purest form. The system provides the plant light and hydroponic food; the plant reacts by growing; the device reacts by producing a rasterized inkjet drawing of the plant every 24 hours, then scrolls the paper about four inches. Neither side is the master. He wanted, his words, a reciprocal relationship between a natural object and a mechanical system, and the final outcome is not predetermined. In the full-res photo you can read the whole logic: the amber hydroponic flask with the pea plant, the wall-mounted gantry printer, and the long scroll of frames where the plant's silhouette visibly thickens day by day in halftone dots.

Discrete time samples made physical

Two pieces make time into an object. Growth rendering: one inkjet raster every 24 hours, paper advances 4 inches, run indefinitely. Growth modeling: lasers scan an onion plant from one of three angles, a fused deposition modeler prints a plastic surrogate in real time, the conveyor advances about 17 inches, repeat every 24 hours. The full-res photo shows the row of small white onion models spaced along the belt like specimens on a timeline. The scroll and the conveyor are the time axis made physical; the piece is a clock that draws.

Mechanical re-enactment of remote data

Tele-present wind: an accelerometer on a dried stalk outdoors sends its sway in real time to 42 (or 84, or 126) x/y tilting mechanisms with dried stalks in the gallery. The Mars version swaps the outdoor stalk for wind data from the Perseverance rover's MEDA sensor, via JPL. Tele-present water: wave intensity and frequency from NOAA buoy Station 46246 drive a grid of mechanical arms manipulating a flexible grid. The mapping is re-enactment, not charting: wind tilt becomes stalk tilt, wave height becomes rod height. In the Wroclaw photo the yellow tape grid on the floor marks the surface boundary, a frame around an amputated slice of ocean. The data arrives through bodies, never through a graph.

Nonhuman pilots

Bowen keeps handing the controls to things that are not him. The phototropic drawing device is a solar-powered robot attracted to the most intense light source, dragging charcoal across paper; lamps on timers compose the piece. Left alone, the devices work their way across his desk toward the light and, his words, commit suicide off the edge. One phototropic drawing ran for four months of continuous drawing. Fly blimps give 3 to 5 helium blimps to collectives of houseflies; the flies are the brain of each device. Plant machete reads the electrical noise across a live philodendron's leaves and maps it to an industrial robot arm holding a machete. Cloud piano points a camera at the sky and plays keys from recognized cloud forms. The two (2025) gives two robot arms cameras and a custom deep network trained to recognize each other; they track, approach, pull away, and retrain on each new sighting, getting better at finding one another. Autonomy is not a feature; it is the medium.

Failure and neglect as material

Two standing habits. First, the failure quote above: the attempt to mimic nature produces the interesting part when it fails. Second, neglect as process: he sets up the phototropic device and goes to have a sandwich, or teach a class, and it keeps drawing. The sonar drawing device was drawing in the Tweed Museum while he typed the interview. The work happens while the artist is absent, which is why the drawings need the didactic, the video, or ideally the device in action to be understood.

Palette and composition

Industrial grays (aluminum framing), circuit-board green, black charcoal on white paper, dried-plant tan, the amber of the hydroponic flask, white PLA plastic. Composition is always the device, the subject, and the residue shown together; he sells the drawings alone but insists they need the story of their making. The didactic is part of the piece.

What makes it sing

The loop closes. Data arrives through a body with limits, and the limits are legible in the mark. The daily raster is a time object you can hold. The mechanism is never hidden: wires, zip ties, and raw aluminum are the aesthetic, and the contrast between the crude machine and the delicate living input (a fly, a seedling, a wave) does the emotional work.

What is overdone (avoid-list)

Physical data viz turns into a gimmick when the mapping is arbitrary. Bowen's mappings survive because they are 1:1 re-enactments: wind tilt to stalk tilt, wave height to rod height. Any piece that maps data to an unrelated visual language (a wave becoming a pie chart of motors) is doing infographics with extra steps. Also: the drawing without the device is a dead object. A Bowen-derived piece must keep the sensing-to-marking chain visible, or it is just a texture.

Open doorways

From the sources: Christa Sommerer and Laurent Mignonneau's Interactive Plant Growing (1993, virtual plant evolution), Ken Goldberg's Telegarden (1996, internet-tended garden), Mary Lucier's Dawn Burn (1973, burning sunrise progression into TV tubes). From his influence list: Jean Tinguely, Arthur Ganson, Cy Twombly for kinetics through mark making; Eduardo Kac, Stelarc, Simon Penny, Natalie Jeremijenko for technology as material integrated with the natural world.

Re-renders (local, visually inspected)

  1. Growth scroll: one stochastic branch-and-leaf plant grown over 9

days (fixed topology, geometry scales with age), each day's silhouette rasterized as inkjet halftone dots onto a scroll frame with gaps like the paper advance. Reads as the daily-raster mechanic: discrete samples, same plant, time as a physical axis.

  1. Tele-present water grid: 9x9 rod field where each rod tilts x/y

from summed-swell wave data, re-enacting a distant water surface through tilt rather than height. Confirms the mechanic is mapping, not modeling: the wave exists only as rod angles.
