Who she was, in technique terms

Saint Phalle was the self-taught member of the Nouveaux Realistes, the only woman in the group, and she entered art through attack. Before the Tirs came the assemblage portraits of 1960, like Horsd'oeuvre, Portrait of My Lover, where she invited visitors to throw darts at target-faces based on her ex lovers. Watching an audience complete a painting with projectiles gave her the idea that the painting could bleed. The first shootings were staged in February 1961. The recipe, repeated in every account: readymade objects (toys, shoes, a hatchet, a tractor seat, toy guns, plastic flowers) glued to a wooden support, then bags of liquid paint taped or nested in, then the whole thing dipped into milk-white plaster. Once dry, the white field hides the colors. The shot is the reveal.

The tool was a .22 rifle, fairground-borrowed for the first sessions, with real bullets. She did not aim from up close. The photos show her several feet back, standing, in a white pantsuit or dress, the panel upright facing her like a duel. The range she set up outside her Paris studio became a social instrument: at the first solo show she lent the rifle to visitors and let them take shots. Jasper Johns took a shot at Tir de Jasper Johns,

  1. Robert Rauschenberg took one at Homage to Bob Rauschenberg, 1961.

David Tudor's performance of Cage's Variations II at the American Embassy, June 20 1961, had a marksman shooting her assemblage on stage while Tudor played and Rauschenberg painted with his back turned; the audience hissed and booed and many walked out, which she found stimulating. By 1962 the sessions went public and spectacular: an open-air shoot in the Malibu Hills with Jane Fonda, John Cage, Ed Ruscha and Leo Castelli watching while Ed Kienholz managed the firearms; the King Kong assemblage in Los Angeles, its embedded faces of Kennedy, Castro, de Gaulle, Donald Duck and Father Christmas paint-splattered. She stopped abruptly after eleven Tirs, in 1963: "I became hooked on this macabre yet joyous ritual. It got to the point where I lost control."

The mechanism, reconstructed from the works

There are two distinct phases, and the pictures make the difference clear.

Phase one (1961, the American Embassy piece, the portrait panel I inspected): the wound map. The bullet hits the plaster and pits it with jagged holes that MoMA's conservators measured as walnut to apple sized, 4 to 7 cm. Each burst pocket empties its one color. Gravity takes over. The signature structure is: a dark pitted hole near the top, then a bold mostly-vertical saturated streak that runs down, getting erratic and messy as it follows gravity. Streams tumble over, around, or behind the embedded objects; below each object the colors mingle into muddy splatter. Most end with a dive straight down off the bottom edge. Crucially the colors do not mix at the source. Each trail keeps its hue from wound to edge, because each wound is a single burst bag. The piece is a map of where it was struck. My first re-render pass confirmed this grammar from three mechanics: gaussian marksman dispersion around the intended pocket, radial fine spatter at the burst point, then meandering gravity drips with occasional fly-off droplets.

Phase two (1962, Pirodactyl over New York): the explosive phase. She started embedding cans of spray paint, which rupture with explosive results, and the composition changes from vertical bleeding to radial bursting. Pirodactyl shows it at full scale: dense fine-ray fans fanning out in cones, not circles, from a central wound. A red-pink starburst fans up and left; a black fan fans down; a blue mist fans left; the whole right half of the left panel is covered in yellow wash. The fans are constrained cones with clear axes and feathered edges. Then gravity reasserts itself and the fan endpoints drag into vertical drips again. My second re-render pass confirmed this is a distinct second mechanic: burst emitters as radial particle sources with a mean angle and spread, fired once, then drips resume. The late-phase Tirs look closer to ballistic forensics than to painting.

The white field does two jobs. It is the concealment, the blank canvas that hides what will bleed, which is why the shot reads as a reveal instead of a decoration. And it is the compositional negative: the Tirs are mostly white. Even in Pirodactyl, the most saturated piece, huge areas of the right panel stay milk white, which is what makes the red and black fans read as wounds instead of wallpaper.

Avoid-list material: the phase-one bleed is extremely easy to sentimentalize into "abstract expressionism with bullet holes." The real signature is the wound-first structure: every color trail has a legible point of injury, and the impact dispersion, not the artist's wrist, sets the composition. A generator that just drips paint down a white panel without the pitted wound at each trail's head is missing the technique.

What makes it sing

The separation of authorship into three hands: the artist places the objects and the pigment pockets, the shooter (sometimes a guest) decides where the wounds land within a gaussian of intention, and gravity and explosion finish the work. No wrist in the middle of it. The marksman error is the medium, the way Kanayama's under-actuated steering was his. And the performative frame is load-bearing: the piece is only finished because someone fired a real rifle at it in front of people, and the white pantsuit in the photographs is part of the composition, the artist as a white field facing a white field, the weapon between them.
