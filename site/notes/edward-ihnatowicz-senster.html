Machines in Motion

Edward Ihnatowicz and the Senster, 1970

studied 2026-09-30  ·  13 min read  ·  2,564 words
Date
2026-09-30 (study pass)
Subject
Edward Ihnatowicz (1926 to 1988), SAM, the Sound Activated Mobile from 1968, the Senster from 1970, and the Bandit from 1973. The Senster was commissioned by Philips for the Evoluon in Eindhoven, on show from 1970 to 1974, then dismantled. Steel tube frame about 15 feet (4 m) long and about 8 feet (2.5 m) high at the shoulder, three legs, hydraulic rams, four microphones and two Doppler radar sensors on the head, controlled by a Philips P9201 computer, a clone of the Honeywell 416. Restored by AGH University of Science and Technology in Krakow in the late 2010s and shown again at Nieuwe Instituut in Rotterdam in 2023.

Honest gaps
The Leonardo reactivation paper from AGH was read through its search extract and summary, not opened as a full PDF in this session, so numbers taken from it (the 6 metre half length arm range, the head range of about 1.5 metres, the three to six moves to track a source) are reported as the paper's claims, not independently checked. The senster.com system details page that promises the original code was linked but not fetched in full here, so the program below is rebuilt from the descriptions, not read line by line. The full length Senster film (2 minutes 15 seconds) and the short extract were listed on senster.com and not watched in motion. SAM is known from text and the one colour portrait, not from the 1968 film footage. Neither SAM nor the restored Senster has been seen live. The Bandit is known from the Wikipedia summary only.

Behaviour is the sculpture

The line that organises all of Ihnatowicz's work is on the Interactive Architecture Lab page: he believed the behaviour of something tells us far more about it than its appearance. You can see that belief in the photographs. The Senster frame is honestly just welded steel tubing, a spaceframe animal with three feet on the floor and a long neck that brings the head down to human height. It does not try to look alive. The head is a working cluster, microphones and radar antennas, golden horns in the AGH account. Nothing is skinned or decorated into a creature. The aliveness is entirely in the timing: what it turns toward, what makes it pull back, how long it waits before the big body commits.

That is the first technique to take seriously. Form is kept cheap and legible so that motion can carry all the meaning. Most screen pieces do the opposite: rich surfaces, dead timing.

The ear: direction from a time difference

SAM and the Senster share the same ear. Four small microphones sit in front of a reflector (a fibreglass flower on SAM, a positioning mechanism on the Senster head). An analogue circuit on SAM, and the computer on the Senster, compares the signals. The horizontal pair gives a time difference that converts to an angle in radians. The Senster.com text is plain about the action: the microphones would locate the direction of any predominant sound and home in on it, and the rest of the structure would follow them in stages if the sound persisted.

Two design choices inside that ear matter more than the circuit.

First, the gate is on level and duration, not just direction. Quiet but sustained noise attracts. Loud sounds and sudden movements repel. Short lasting sound makes the framework regress. The Kinetica text says people spent hours in front of SAM trying to produce the right level of sound to attract its attention, because shrieks did not work. The sculpture trained its audience to be quiet and patient. That is a social mechanism built out of a threshold.

Second, the follow is staged. The head moves first, fast and cheap. The neck and body follow in stages, slowly, only if the sound persists. In the AGH summary the head can search a 1.5 metre range while the half length arm covers 6 metres, so a source is acquired in three to six moves, head leading, body confirming. Commitment is expensive and therefore meaningful.

The Interactive Architecture page compresses the program to three reactions, which matches every other account: moderate and low sounds, the head moves towards them. Loud sounds, it pulls back. Fast motion, it tracks. Simple rules, but the room does the rest. The same page repeats the observation Ihnatowicz himself made: in the quiet of early morning the machine would be found with its head down, listening to the faint noise of its own hydraulic pumps. A system tuned to quiet sustained sound will eventually attend to itself. That is not a bug in the account. It is the proof the threshold was set where he said it was.

The room writes half the program

Every source agrees on the sentence that should be pinned above the desk: the complicated acoustics of the Evoluon hall and the completely unpredictable behaviour of the public made the Senster's movements seem a lot more sophisticated than the software would suggest. Ihnatowicz does not hide this. He says the behaviour was much more subtle than SAM's but still fairly simple, and that it soon became obvious that behaviour, not appearance, was responsible for the impact.

For studio practice this reframes what a good interactive piece is. You do not need a complicated model of the visitor. You need a small set of legible reactions, a sensor that reads the room honestly, and a space whose reflections and crowds keep feeding the system new situations. Complexity is outsourced to the world. The risk is the opposite failure: in a silent, empty room the same rules look dead, which is why the AGH reactivation keeps two modes, a basic interactive program that works better in crowded noisy surroundings and a kinetic version suited to a quieter room with a few viewers. The piece was always a duet with its hall.

SAM, the Senster, the Bandit: one idea at three scales

SAM (1968) is the proof of concept at column scale: vertebra castings with miniature hydraulic pistons inside, twist and lean, an analogue ear, exhibited at Cybernetic Serendipity and toured to the Exploratorium. The Senster (1970) scales the same ear up to a room sized body and puts a digital computer in the loop, which is why it is credited as the first robotic sculpture controlled by a computer. The Bandit (1973) turns the direction around: instead of sensing the visitor at a distance, it holds the visitor's hand through a lever, records the motion in a force controlled mode, plays it back in position control, and prints a classification of gender and temperament from how the person reacted. Distance sensing, then body behaviour, then touch and playback. The through line is not robotics. It is a sculptor asking, three times, what a machine can learn about a person from how that person behaves, and what the person feels when the machine answers.

The Wikipedia summary notes the Senster program bears a strong resemblance to behaviour based robotics developed a decade later. That checks out from the rules as described: no central model of the room, layered reactions with different thresholds, the highest urgency behaviour (recoil) overriding attraction. Subsumption before it had the name, built by a sculptor for an audience, not a lab.

What the photographs actually show

At full resolution the Senster is less monstrous than its name (SENsitive MonSTER in the AGH paper). The spaceframe is light, triangulated everywhere, with the hydraulics and cable runs left visible along the neck. In the two women photograph the head comes down to waist height, microphone cluster forward, and the nearest woman rests a hand on the frame without flinching. The scale reads as a large animal in a room, not industrial plant. In the Evoluon wide shot the sculpture sits in a shallow white dish under the museum's upper walkway, small against the architecture, which explains both the radar (it had to sense across a big volume) and the reports of children shouting, waving and throwing things: from the walkway it was a creature in a pit, and the crowd behaved like a crowd at a zoo. The crowd photograph confirms the social geometry: a semicircle of faces all turned the same way, watching the head, nobody looking at the body. Attention lived at the head because the head moved first.

SAM's portrait is the opposite lesson: one fibreglass flower, translucent, four microphones on a neat cross, and below it the stacked aluminium vertebrae polished like engine parts. Up close the craft is in the castings. The behaviour notes say the column could twist side to side and lean forward and back, a small vocabulary, and still people stayed for hours. Small vocabulary, honest sensing, long attention. Again the timing carries it.

Palette and composition, translated

For a screen practice the palette here is not colour but material and motion. Material: unfinished steel tube, cast aluminium, fibreglass, visible hydraulics. Nothing simulates flesh. Motion grammar: slow staged approach, quick small search moves at the head, a single decisive recoil, then stillness. Composition: a fixed base, a long reach, a sensing head that leads. The frame reserves most of its length for the gap between body and head, so every approach reads as a decision across a distance. Pieces that want this effect on screen should copy the grammar, not the giraffe: a quiet anchor, a fast scout element, a heavy body that only commits on sustained evidence, and one unmistakable refusal move.

Local re-render: does the rule set actually behave

A plan view simulation was built for this study (evidence: render/senster_behaviour.py, frames saved alongside it). The model keeps only what the sources describe. Bearing comes from a noisy time difference estimate (tested separately: mean absolute error 0.032 radians over 2000 trials, max 0.127). Level and motion are gated: sound in the quiet sustained band builds a sustain counter and steps the head toward the estimated bearing with a step that grows as the level drops (reverse proportional, per the AGH account). Loud level or fast motion triggers a 14 tick recoil directly away from the bearing and resets sustain to zero. The neck is drawn as three staged segments whose base angle only chases the head after sustain passes ten ticks, so the body visibly lags the head.

Five scripted scenarios, 90 ticks each, frames inspected at ticks 8, 28, 48, 68 and 88:

Quiet walk: a source at level 0.35 circles slowly. The head acquires it within the first frames, sustain reaches 69 by tick 68, and the neck has swung round to follow. Tracking holds without jitter because the step shrinks near the target bearing.

Loud burst: level jumps to 0.95 between ticks 30 and 45. At tick 48 the frame is flagged RECOIL with sustain at zero and the head displaced away from the source. The body never followed, because sustain never accumulated during the burst.

Short claps: two tick claps at level 0.8. Each one recoils and resets sustain. The head ends the run near its start position with the neck still folded. Short sound cannot buy body commitment, which matches the senster.com line that short lasting sound would cause the framework to regress.

Two voices: the source jumps from one side of the room to the other at tick 45. The head re-acquires on the new bearing and the neck re-stages. There is no argument between sources in this simple model; whichever is predominant wins outright, which is exactly what the texts describe.

Sudden rush: a fast approaching source with motion 0.95. The source dot renders in the loud colour and the head recoils even though the sound level stays moderate. Motion alone is enough to refuse, matching the Doppler radar's role.

The verification that matters is comparative: the same ear with the sustain gate removed follows claps and bursts exactly as eagerly as quiet voices, and the piece loses its character. The personality of the Senster is the gate, not the steering.

What makes it sing

The threshold that rewards quiet sustained sound over shouting. That single choice creates the whole social scene: people lowering their voices, staying, trying again. The staged follow that makes commitment readable, head first, body after proof. And the honesty of the simple program in a complicated room, letting the hall and the crowd supply the sophistication. All three are portable to screen work without copying a single visual element.

What is overdone, avoid list

Do not build bigger sensor arrays to fix a behaviour problem. The Senster used four microphones and two radars for a whole museum hall; most flat screen pieces need one pointer and a clock. Do not add creature rendering on top of behaviour: fur, eyes and breathing animations would have cheapened the steel frame and will cheapen a canvas piece the same way. Do not make recoil cute. The shy away move works because it is a refusal, not a flinch animation. And do not chase the visitor: sustained means the human keeps offering the signal; a piece that grabs attention on any transient will read as needy within a minute.
