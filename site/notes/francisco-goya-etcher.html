Deep study, 2026-10-04. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named first among next doorways by both the 2026-10-04 Hercules Segers study and the 2026-10-04 Rembrandt the etcher study. The quality-first choice after the long restraint run: the master of the composed dark, the aquatint ground as a compositional instrument, and the reserve as the brightest value. Where Rembrandt revised the plate and Segers revised the printing, Goya revised the GROUND: each series rethinks what the dark is for.

Sources

Biography, technique, and criticism via a delegated research dossier (text-only pass, no artwork inspected by the dossier author; this study's visual inspections are mine). The dossier arrived after the visual pass and first push; its key facts are integrated below, with this session's own firsthand reads (Wikipedia's Los Caprichos, Los Disparates, and Los Desastres articles, the Prado's Cruel Folly page, museum catalogue data on the inspected impressions) where noted. The dossier's own honest gaps are listed at the end.

Key facts, carried:

  • Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, 1746-1828. The print career starts when

he is already an established painter; he learned etching (a draughtsman's technique, unlike the burin which took ten years to master) and then aquatint, which gave him tonal gradations "from white to black, similar to making washes in paint." Using etching and aquatint together was new, and the results were "very like paintings" (Wikipedia, Los Caprichos). Burnishing the aquatint let him pull light back out of the dark: the scratch is the highlight. Who taught him aquatint is undocumented; the sources treat him as essentially self-taught as a printmaker (dossier).

  • The Rembrandt comparison, via Lemoisne in the dossier: Rembrandt

builds tone through accumulated fine curving lines; Goya gets his darks as broad fields of tone and carves light back out of them. Goya drew meticulously on paper first, then simplified and lightened the drawing when transferring it to copper. He did not cross-hatch the way Rembrandt did, and used a single type of aquatint to fill whole backgrounds uniformly. His darkness is a field, not a weave.

  • Los Caprichos: 80 plates, finished January 17, 1799 (the Osuna

Archive receipt), sale announced in the Diario de Madrid on February 6, 1799, 300 copies at the perfumery on Calle del Desengano No. 1. Only 27 sold before he withdrew the album, apparently fearing the Inquisition; in 1807 he gave the plates and the 240 unsold sets to Charles IV in exchange for a lifetime pension for his son Javier (dossier; one source says 1803, flagged). Grew out of the "Dreams" drawings after Quevedo; the title follows Callot's and Tiepolo's Capricci. The personal-revenge reading (the Duchess of Alba) is real but partial: he denied any plate depicted a specific person.

  • Los Desastres de la Guerra: 80 plates (82 in the Bermudez proof

album, plus three small "Prisioneros"), 1810-1820, the Peninsular War years. Goya's own title: "Fatal consequences of Spain's bloody war with Bonaparte, and other emphatic caprices"; "The Disasters of War" is the Academy's 1863 invention. The individual captions ARE his, etched on the plates with his spelling mistakes; the Academy changed only one title and retouched one plate (dossier correction to this session's earlier read). The series breaks into three movements: 47 plates of atrocities, 16 of the 1811-12 famine, and the final "emphatic caprices" on the postwar repression.

  • La Tauromaquia: 33 plates published October 1816 plus 7 unissued,

c.1815-1816, the only series after the Caprichos he published himself. The most conventional and commercially minded of the four.

  • Los Disparates: etching and aquatint with drypoint and engraving

retouching, 1815-1823, made at the Quinta del Sordo alongside the Black Paintings (the estate was named for a prior owner's deafness, not his). Never published in his lifetime. First published 1864 by the Academy as "Proverbios": the proverb titles are the publishers', from Goya's proofs the series is the Disparates. 22 prints survive (18 plus 4 added later, published 1877 in L'Art); the highest proof number is 25. The 1864 ordering was random. The preparatory drawing for Cruel Folly shows how far he reworked: a soldier expelling people with a rifle at a sentry box became a civilian with a pike and no sentry box at all.

  • Robert Hughes's formulation, carried from the dossier: in the late

work, darkness is not absence but a positive presence, "pervasive, as demons were to the medieval mind"; the late career is "cumulative despair," with plate 15's "y no hai remedio" (and there is no remedy) as its motto.

  • Critical through-lines: the Disparates as the continuation of the

Caprichos and the late "emphatic caprices" of the Desastres; the nightmare and grotesque quality; Glendinning's Carnaval reading (the subversion of authority: soldiers, marriage, clergy); the 20th-century avant-garde's claim on the series' modernity.

The four series as compositional species

Four different answers to the same question: what is the dark for.

  • Los Caprichos (1799): the satirical emblem. Small figures in a

composed mid-tone void, the caption doing half the work. The ground is a stage, not a night.

  • Los Desastres de la Guerra (1810-1820): the report that refuses

reportage. Light grounds, the horror in one dark instrument (the tree, the brawl knot), the caption's irony against the image's withholding. No heroics, no distance: the event is small and close.

  • La Tauromaquia (c.1815-1816): motion in a ring. The action band

against the empty sand; the dark mass (the bull) against the pale victim. The arena is a compositional machine: bands of spectators, action, and void.

  • Los Disparates (1815-1823): the dream sheet. Full-bleed aquatint

night with burnished light, or nearly blank paper with one dark mass. No horizon, no narrative, figures floating in a void or walking off the edge. The ground values swing the widest in all his work, from volante's near-black to cruel's near-white.

Six plates at source resolution

All six from open-access museum scans (MET DP series, Google Art Project), inspected at 2600-4000px. Tonal stats below are measured from the reproductions with a center crop to reduce mount pollution; the mounts inflate the means, so treat them as relative only.

Caprichos No. 43, El sueno de la razon produce monstruos (MET DP816991, 2640x3697)

The desk anchors the bottom third; the sleeper's white shirt is the brightest mass in the plate, slumped across it. Behind and above, the owls and bats are small dark hatched knots against a flat mid-gray aquatint ground that fills the whole background. The ground is one even value, no gradient, no vignette. The technique contrast is the composition: etched hatching for the creatures, smooth grain for the void. The title is written on the desk front, INSIDE the picture: the caption is part of the composition, not a label under it.

Desastres No. 39, Grande hazana! Con muertos! (MET DP817382, 3765x2825)

The tree trunk is the one dark instrument, a vertical mass of dense hatching dividing the plate. The bodies are pale reserves hung and strewn against it: one head-down at the base, one bound to the trunk, one upside-down corpse tied to a branch at the right. The ground here is LIGHT aquatint, not dark; the horror sits in the dark tree mass. The staging borrows the pietà without the consolation: bodies as evidence, faceless, arranged, the caption's irony ("heroic feat") doing the moral work the image refuses.

Desastres No. 3, Lo mismo (MET DP817147, 3748x2754)

The brawl fills only the bottom half: a tangled low mass of limbs, faces lost in dark knots, an axe raised at the right corner whose head is the highest dark mark in the plate. Above it, the top half is a vast nearly empty aquatint sky. The empty half is the composition's second instrument. The violence is not in the crowding but in the ratio: a small knot of horror under a huge indifferent field.

Tauromaquia No. 33, The unlucky death of Pepe Illo (MET DP817525, 3781x2683)

Horizontal layering: a thin band of spectator heads along the top of the ring wall, the action in the middle band, and a huge empty foreground below split by a diagonal shadow. The bull is the darkest thing in the picture, a solid crosshatched mass; Pepe Illo below it is the lightest horizontal. The drama is in the ratio of event to emptiness: the action is small and low, the sand does the rest.

Disparates No. 5, Disparate volante (MET DP817503, 3735x2657)

Full-bleed near-black aquatint, no horizon, no sky gradient, just the grain. The giant owl-creature's wingspan fills the whole frame; the wingtips touch the plate edges. The feathers are burnished OUT of the dark: light scratch-work in the black, the most brilliant passage in all six plates. The bird's body is reserved paper, the rider dark. The action floats in a void with no ground plane at all.

Disparates No. 6, Disparate cruel (Google Art Project, 4001x3090)

The opposite instrument: the sheet is nearly all untouched paper. A dark hatched cliff mass at the left, a procession of cloaked figures walking away to the right, one man lunging with a lance as the single diagonal. Left dark mass versus right light procession, the paper doing all the atmospheric work.

Tonal arc (crop means/std, relative): volante 133.3/42.6 the darkest (full black ground, compressed); cruel 151.4/78.8 the widest range (paper plus one dark cliff); sleep of reason 155.8/50.2; lo mismo 163.2/51.3; grande hazana 162.6/57.2; pepe illo 165.9/36.2 the most compressed (light paper, one black bull). Goya's dark is never one dark: each plate chooses its ground value and its range, and the choice is the composition.

Re-render families

Two PIL schematic families, single pass, all frames visually inspected. Abstract masses only: no owls, bulls, or corpses.

Study A, The Ground Is the Composition. A1 species: flat mid-gray aquatint ground (one even value, full bleed), a pale ellipse fully reserved with zero marks inside, one hatched dark bar as the single dark instrument. A2: the same drawing with darkness applied as a global vignette multiply (the named failure, the "dark academia filter"): the darkness has no placement, and it grays the reserved bright. A3: the ground as a smooth vertical gradient (atmosphere, not instrument). A4: the ground as all-over crosshatch texture (wallpaper): the dark instrument disappears into the field. Finding: the ground must be flat composed zones. Darkness as filter, gradient, or texture is decoration.

Study B, The Reserved Bright. B1 species: full-bleed dark grain ground, the pale mass reserved as untouched paper (the brightest value in the chord), burnished scratch highlights drawn INTO the dark around the mass, stopping at the reserve edge. B2: the pale mass drawn with white outlines and shading lines inside (the illustration failure): reads as a logo. B3: everything filled with mid tone, no reserve (the rendering failure): the picture has no bright event. B4: B1 negated (the photoshop-invert failure): the reserve becomes a black hole and the logic collapses. Finding: the brightest value must be untouched ground. Light is the absence of work, and a negation is not a re-cutting.

Quality notes

What makes the work good, as decisions:

  • The ground value is chosen per plate, and the choice is load-bearing.

Volante's near-black and Cruel's near-white are opposite instruments in the same series. A derivative that "darkens for mood" has understood nothing.

  • One dark instrument per plate. The tree, the bull, the night, the

cliff, the brawl knot. Everything else is quiet around it.

  • The brightest passage is reserved, never drawn. The sleeper's shirt,

the hung bodies, Pepe Illo's corpse, the owl's breast. White is not a color here, it is a decision to stop working.

  • Technique contrast is compositional: hatched line masses against

smooth aquatint grain. The two never mix into one mush.

  • The event is small and the field is large. Lo mismo's violence under

half a sky of nothing; Pepe Illo's death in a third of the sheet. The ratio is the rhetoric.

  • The caption is inside the work. Sleep of Reason writes its title on

the desk; the Desastres titles bite because the image withholds what the caption says. And his own caption for Capricho 43 complicates the easy Enlightenment reading: "Fantasy abandoned by reason produces impossible monsters; united with her, she is the mother of the arts and the origin of their marvels." The print stages both the fear of unreason and the defense of imagination in one frame (dossier, via Hughes).

  • The plate is a two-layer system (dossier): a sparse gestural line

drawing carries the figures, and a separate tonal layer carries the background mass. Light is not built up; it is recovered from darkness by burnishing. That inversion of the usual drawing process, dark out of light, is one of his most usable devices.

  • Grande hazana delivers horror through classical composition, not

mess (dossier, via the MAPFRE catalog): the bodies are distributed around the tree "with an almost aesthetic criterion," the inverted torso recalling the Torso Belvedere, the victims calm-faced and beautiful. He does not try to horrify; he forces reflection, and the bitter caption does the accusing.

  • Lo mismo works by pairing (dossier): plate 2 asks "With or without

reason," plate 3 answers "The same." The composition repeats the firing-squad geometry, so the doubling does the philosophy in two words.

  • The refusal of heroics is formal, not just moral (dossier): no

battles, no generals, no establishing shot, no anecdotal background. Both sides commit atrocities; the captions offer no consolation. The images behave like photojournalism before photography: immediacy plus the refusal to compose the event into a story.

What would make a derivative fail:

  • A vignette or crushed-black filter passed off as his darkness.
  • Uniformly dark pictures with no composed ground value.
  • The owl, the bull, the hanging man as signifiers.
  • Horror as the subject instead of the compositional discipline.
  • Text pasted under the image as his captioning.

Avoid-list additions

  • Dark filters, vignettes, or crushed blacks as Goya-like.
  • Gradient skies as his atmosphere.
  • Texture-everywhere grounds.
  • White line drawing on dark as his reserve.
  • Negation as his inversion.
  • Owls, bulls, hanging bodies, witches as motifs.
  • The tortured-deaf-genius story as branding.
  • Horror content without the ground discipline.

inspection and fixed (Study A: an opaque composite wiped the grounds; Study B: a masked layer produced a ring instead of a ground); the inspected frames are the corrected ones.
