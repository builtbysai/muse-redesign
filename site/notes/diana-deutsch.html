Artist
Diana Deutsch (born 1938, London). Professor of Psychology at UC San Diego. Trained as a pianist before her parents steered her to Oxford (psychology and philosophy), then a doctorate at UCSD, arriving just as computers became usable for sound. Her laboratory output since 1972 is a catalog of illusions that read like a generative art toolkit: each one is a tiny stimulus program whose output the brain renders incorrectly, on purpose, every time.

The thesis, stated plainly

Deutsch's doctrine is that hearing is rendering, not recording. The ear delivers attributes; the brain bundles them, and the bundle is where the art happens. Her 1976 what/where model (with Roll) says it directly: one decision mechanism determines what pitch is heard, a separate one determines where it appears to come from, and the two can be pried apart by stimuli so simple a child could draw them. Every illusion in the catalog is a demonstration that perception runs a plausibility engine over the signal, preferring the interpretation that would make sense in a normal environment, and that this engine carries personal state: handedness, native language, childhood dialect, even current mood.

The career structure matters. She started at the hardware moment, like everyone in the Bell Labs circle, and turned the computer into a perceptual probe instead of an instrument. Mathews built the orchestra, Alles built the synthesizer chip, Risset built the paradoxes into the sound itself; Deutsch built the paradoxes into the listening. Her illusions are the only artworks in this study thread where the score is trivial (two tones, four per second) and the entire complexity lives in the audience.

The catalog, technique by technique

Octave illusion (1973, reported at ASA; Nature 1974). Two sine tones an octave apart (800 and 400 Hz), alternated at four per second. Both ears get the identical sequence; when the right ear gets the high tone the left gets the low, and vice versa. The tones are constant amplitude with no drops at the transitions, so the listener is really hearing one continuous two-tone chord whose components keep switching ears. Almost nobody hears it correctly. The typical percept: the right ear hears high-silence-high-silence while the left hears silence-low-silence-low. Reversing the earphones changes nothing: the high tone stays on the right, the low on the left. Right-handers reliably put the high tone on the right; left-handers vary, and the right-bias is stronger in people whose whole family is right-handed. The mechanism is the what/where split: the pitch heard at an ear is the pitch delivered to the other ear (what is computed globally), while each tone is localized to the ear receiving the higher frequency (where is computed locally). The combination is an illusory conjunction of pitch and location.

Scale illusion (1975). A scale played ascending and descending at once, successive tones alternating ears, so when the right ear gets an ascending tone the left gets a descending one, no pauses. The percept: the higher tones regroup into one ear, the lower tones into the other. Right-handers hear the high stream on the right. The explanation is plausibility: it is wildly unlikely that two jerky interlocking pitch patterns come from two different places, so the brain regroups by height. Variants work with other melodies, with real instruments in concert halls, and she found the mechanism operating inside the repertoire itself: the last movement of Tchaikovsky's Sixth does it live, meaning audiences at the same concert are hearing the passage in different ways. That last fact is the one to steal.

Glissando illusion (late 20th century). A constant-pitch synthesized oboe tone plus a gliding sine, switching speakers so they trade sides. The oboe is heard correctly, jumping between speakers; the glissando segments fuse into one continuous tone that seems to travel through space with its pitch. Again plausibility: a smoothly changing frequency is unlikely to teleport between speakers, so the brain assigns it one moving source. Right-handers hear it travel left to right as pitch rises.

Tritone paradox (1986, ASA; Music Perception). Two Shepard tones a half-octave apart: each tone is six octave-spaced partials under a fixed bell envelope, so the pitch class (C, F#) is crisp and the octave placement is undefined. C followed by F#: some listeners hear ascending, others descending, with total conviction, and the same listener flips judgment on G# followed by D. The judgments trace a personal orientation of the pitch-class circle: each listener has a region of the circle they hear as higher and an opposite region they hear as lower, like a clock face rotated to a private 12 o'clock. Two subjects in her figures peak at G#/A and at C#/D, nearly opposite orientations. The orientation correlates with the pitch range of the listener's speaking voice and with childhood language: Californians vs southern English differ strikingly; Youngstown subjects differ by where their parents grew up; Vietnamese-born subjects in California differ from California-raised English speakers regardless of when they arrived, which pins the template to early childhood. The paradox quietly proves most people have an implicit absolute pitch: they judge height by note name alone.

Phantom words. Two stereo speakers, each looping two-syllable words, the loops offset in time so syllables collide in the air. After continued listening the brain starts hearing words that were never spoken: dieters hear food words, stressed students hear stress words, women hear romantic words men do not, non-native speakers hear their own languages. Deutsch calls it an audio Rorschach. It needs speakers, not headphones: the words have to mix in air before they reach the ears. The perfect-circuit writeup notes the kinship with Steve Reich's phase pieces (Come Out, It's Gonna Rain), where the same mechanism produces phantom phrases by accident.

Mysterious Melody (1972). A well-known tune (Yankee Doodle) played with all note names correct but the tones scattered across three octaves: unrecognizable. Once you know the tune, you hear it instantly. Top-down processing made audible. The visual analog she cites is the Dalmatian dog in the dappled photograph.

What/where model (1976, with Roll). The two-mechanism theory behind the octave illusion, tested with a 3-high-2-low pattern per ear: listeners hear the pitch sequence of one ear but localize every tone to the ear receiving the higher frequency, so a low tone is heard coming from the earphone that never delivered it. Reversing the earphones makes the right channel appear to drop a high tone and the left appear to gain a low one. On her octave page she draws the Necker cube and Escher's horsemen as the visual cousins: same ambiguity, different sense.

What sings

The economy. Every illusion is two to four tones and a switching rule; the entire perceptual machinery of the piece is the listener. This is the purest version of the generative contract: the artist ships the stimulus program, the audience's nervous system is the renderer, and the output varies per viewer in lawful ways. For piece design, the catalog's real gift is the stimulus vs percept diagram format, the filled and unfilled boxes of her encyclopedia figure, which is itself a beautiful score notation: two rows of what is delivered, two rows of what is heard, and the gap between them is the artwork.

The personalization finding is the other steal. The tritone paradox turns a listener's childhood dialect into a visible clock orientation. Phantom words turn current preoccupations into hallucinated text. These are instruments that measure the player while being played. A piece that calibrates itself to the viewer's hidden state, then shows them the calibration, is Deutsch's whole career in one interaction.

What is overdone (avoid-list additions)

  • The wall-of-references academic page as aesthetic. Her site is a

wonderful archive and a terrible design: the figures are the treasure, the surrounding prose is committee copy. Steal the figures, not the page.

  • Explaining the trick before the experience. Her demo pages lead with

mechanism; the illusion lands harder when the listener commits to a judgment first and learns the machinery after. For pieces: perception first, diagram second.

  • Assuming the percept is universal. The handedness and dialect findings

mean any illusion piece has at least two audiences hearing two different pieces. Design for the split, or at least disclose it, rather than tuning to the majority percept.

Technique seeds for the practice

Seeds 229-231 in FUTURE_PIECES.md. The throughline: ship the stimulus, let the audience render it, and make the gap between delivered and heard visible. The what/where split is a compositional tool, not just a curiosity: any piece with two channels (stereo, left/right screen halves, two colors) can bind one attribute globally and localize another locally, and the viewer's brain will do the rest.
