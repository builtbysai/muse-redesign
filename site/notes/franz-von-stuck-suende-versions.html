Deep study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as a next doorway by BOTH the 2026-10-06 Franz von Stuck study and the 2026-10-06 Weber Portfolio study ("the eleven Suende versions as a serial study"), the quality-first choice: the one case in the whole lineage where one artist remade one composition eleven times over twenty years and scholars did the version-by-version comparison for us.

The study has two halves. A delegated research dossier (text-only, zero em dashes) did the version inventory and the scholarly comparison, built on Albert Ritthaler's 2009 lecture-catalogue "Franz von Stuck und seine Suenden" (the only source that names, dates, measures, and compares all eleven) and Heinrich Voss's 1973 catalogue raisonne. The dossier corrected several facts in the earlier Stuck study, all folded in below. I visually inspected three versions at source resolution: the 1893 Pinakothek (oil, 94.5 x 59.5 cm, inv. 7925), the c.1912 Berlin Alte Nationalgalerie (oil, 88 x 52.5 cm, FV 72), and the ca. 1908 Frye Art Museum Seattle (Syntonos on canvas, 88.58 x 54.93 cm, 1952.169). The other four located versions (Passau private, Palermo, Florio private, Villa Stuck) and the four lost versions are text-only, honestly marked.

The count

Voss and Ritthaler: eleven large oil versions of the motif, four missing. Ritthaler counts only the large versions (about 90 x 53 cm). Add two small oils (Voss 59 and 91, about 50 x 30 cm) and one pastel and gouache on paper (Voss 86) and it becomes fourteen, but Ritthaler excludes those from the eleven. German Wikipedia now lists two additional c.1897 versions (Gutmann, Flersheim) from post-2009 restitution research, unreconciled with Ritthaler's list. English Wikipedia's lead says twelve while its Versions section says eleven. The count is not settled; eleven is the scholarly canon, carried with the dispute.

The eleven, in order (after Ritthaler 2009)

  1. Hanfstaengl, 1891/92. LOST. The first large version. The ONLY one with the

serpent wound twice around the woman's belly (which stretched the format taller). Maidenly face, nearly round snake head, light diamond pattern on the dark snake body, the hell patch unstructured. Sold to the publisher Franz Hanfstaengl on 24 October 1893 for photographic reproduction rights only, against royalties, with Stuck asking it be photographed as large as possible. Shown March 1892 at the Kunstverein Munich, the earliest documented exhibition of any Suende.

  1. Pinakothek, before 1893. Munich, Neue Pinakothek, inv. 7925. Shown 1893 at

the first Munich Secession exhibition, bought days after the opening by the landowner E. J. Haniel for 4,000 marks and offered to the Pinakothek. The museum says acquired 1893 as a gift from a private collection; Ritthaler says it entered at the end of 1895. Conflict carried. Oil, 94.5 x 59.5 cm (Ritthaler measured 95.0 x 59.7). Original temple frame with DIE SUENDE (round U, the early spelling) incised on the plinth. Signed upper right: FRANZ / STUCK. The serpent now winds only once, the form kept for every later version. Hanfstaengl bought the reproduction rights in 1903 and catalogued it "Die Suende III".

  1. Bierbaum, about 1895. LOST. First illustrated in Otto Julius Bierbaum's

1899 Stuck monograph; Bierbaum wrote in all four editions that Stuck had painted the picture twice and the reproduction showed the second version. The snake's highlight becomes a broad field of cross-hatching, the diamond pattern reduced, an adult woman's features in the face. Signature analysis (one signature type not used before 1894) kills the old June 1893 dating. Hanfstaengl's "Die Suende II" (rights bought 1899).

  1. Littmann, about 1895. LOST. Known only from a 1931 photograph of the

gentleman's room in architect Max Littmann's villa (Littmann advised Stuck on the Villa Stuck build; his firm executed it). The most elaborate temple frame known for any Suende: deeper, columns fully round rather than half-round, gilded piers at the ends. The frame carries DIE SUENDE with the round U, a spelling otherwise known only from the Pinakothek frame.

  1. Kuenstleraltar, about 1895. LOST. The version that stood at the center of

Stuck's "Kuenstleraltar" (artist's altar) in his studio. Two period photographs exist (Die Kunst 1903/04 and Ostini 1909) and they show different paintings; Ritthaler holds the 1909 one is the Palermo version and the 1903/04 one is the altar version proper. Unique detail: the frame inscription uses a V with two i-dots, a spelling found on no other Suende frame.

  1. Passau, about 1895. Private collection, current location unverified, last

seen publicly 1993 in Passau. Close to the Bierbaum version; the snake body has red and green patterning with light-blue cross-hatching highlight. Oil, 88 x 53.3 cm. A pigment analysis exists (unpublished, Hermann Kuehn 1991). The frame is not original; it was newly made after the Villa Stuck frame.

  1. Palermo, before 1908. Civica Galleria d'Arte Moderna "Empedocle Restivo",

Palermo, inv. 334. Bought 21 October 1909 at the Venice Biennale for 10,000 lire. With this version Stuck presented a wholly new treatment in 1909: the earlier chiaroscuro gives way to stronger color and brighter tones, the background becomes dark green instead of black, the snake gets luminous blue and turquoise accents, the orange-yellow hell patch becomes a green-yellow area, the face becomes "lasterhaft" (depraved). Oil, 88 x 53 cm, in the original temple frame with DIE SVENDE (VE ligature) on the plinth. Hanfstaengl's "Die Suende IV" (rights bought 1909).

  1. Florio, about 1908. Private collection (the Sicilian Florio family until

1997). Very close to the Palermo version: luminous colors, the snake's body with blue and turquoise dots as highlight. Stuck deliberately used a coarse canvas, which with the glazed paint gives a restless shimmering surface. Oil, 89 x 52.8 cm. Original architectural frame after Stuck's design, DIE SVENDE on the plinth.

  1. Berlin, after 1908. Alte Nationalgalerie, FV 72. The signature with the

elongated S is documented only from 1908 onward, which dates it. The face is the "depraved woman" type; the snake head luminous green and blue with a red mouth; the highlight dotted in blue, light blue, yellow, and white; the hell patch with yellow-green horizontal hatching. Oil, 88 x 52.5 cm (Ritthaler's text says 88 x 52). Provenance runs through the Sturzenegger collection St. Gallen, the dealer August Combe, Galerie Almas-Dietrich, and the "Sonderauftrag Linz" before July 1938, intended for Kremsmuenster; it may be identical with a version that hung in Hitler's private apartment at Prinzregentenplatz 16. On loan from the Federal Republic of Germany.

  1. Seattle, after 1908. Frye Art Museum, Seattle, 1952.169. The museum's

object record: "Die Suende (Sin)", ca. 1908, Syntonos on canvas, 34 7/8 x 21 5/8 in. Stylistically closest to the Berlin version. The crucial detail: Stuck first signed it FRANZ VON STUCK, then rubbed the VON out and overpainted it, so it reads FRANZ STUCK. The museum's stereomicroscope notes confirm the abraded and retouched "von", and the museum holds Stuck's own 1925 certificate of authenticity. This proves Stuck kept signing FRANZ STVCK without the noble "von" even after his 1906 ennoblement, at least for this subject. Original leaf-gilded temple frame with DIE SVENDE incised on the plinth.

  1. Villa Stuck, after 1908. Museum Villa Stuck, Munich, inv. G 91 1-6,

Ziersch gift (bought 1965 from Stuck's estate). The museum's metadata says "before 1906" because the noble title is missing; Ritthaler rejects this on the elongated-S signature evidence (no such signature documented before 1908). Oil, 88.5 x 53.3 cm (museum: 88.5 x 53.5). Original frame after Stuck's design.

Excluded: the 1889/90 "Sinnlichkeit" etching and painting (the direct precursor; contemporary critics already called it the "first Suende"), the two small oils, the pastel/gouache on paper, a Graz version Voss lists under doubtful attributions (Ritthaler: the signature is impossible for Stuck in any period), and a "New York Suende" withdrawn from Sotheby's 1996 after Ritthaler, the Stuck estate, and Voss unanimously rejected the attribution.

Corrections to the earlier Stuck study

  • The Villa Stuck enshrinement: the earlier study said "enshrined in a marble

altar of his own design." The dossier could not confirm marble; what the sources document is an "Altar der Suende" (artist's altar), an architectural construction of Stuck's own design that stood in his studio, with a Suende in a temple frame at its center. The altar version itself (Voss 88) is one of the four lost versions. The version at the altar in the museum's present display is the Villa Stuck SVENDE (no. 11). Marble carried as unverified.

  • The Frye version is not tempera loosely described; the museum says

"Syntonos on canvas" (Ritthaler hedges "water-soluble paint, Syntonos?").

  • The "four missing" are named: Hanfstaengl, Bierbaum, Littmann,

Kuenstleraltar.

  • The Chicago 1893 gold medal was Stuck's at the World's Columbian

Exposition, not necessarily awarded for this picture; the Suende itself got the state gold medal for foreign artists in Vienna 1899. Two different medals, often confused.

  • The Berlin version's provenance runs through the Sonderauftrag Linz; it

may have hung in Hitler's private apartment. Documented via the federal art administration (KVDB), not alleged.

Visual inspections (3)

All three inspected at full resolution, no original handled, color balance unverified. The Frye images came via the museum's own files through a CDN cache (the site hotlink-blocks direct fetch); the Berlin file is a visitor photograph (creazilla illustration of the DSC7990 photo), the Pinakothek file the Commons shrine photograph from the earlier study.

  1. Pinakothek 1893 (1954x2407 with frame). The shrine first: leaf-gilded

temple frame, half-columns with Doric capitals, the plinth lettered DIE SUENDE between two roundels. Inside, the pale torso is the one great light, dissolving at every edge into the black hair mass and ground. The face is shadowed, the pupils turned sideways to the left (the Smarthistory reading confirmed on the pixels). The orange-yellow hell patch is small, tucked at the upper right against the inner molding, with visible brushwork. The serpent's head rests at the left on her shoulder, the mouth open. The ground is black without depth. Signature FRANZ / STUCK two-line, upper right, inside the orange field.

  1. Frye ca. 1908 (809x1024 and 681x842, two museum photos of the same

object). The same temple frame with DIE SVENDE (the VE ligature) on the plinth, plus a small FRANZ STUCK cartouche on the frame front in the tighter photo. The painting: the face is more frontal and more lit than the 1893 version, the torso more modeled with olive shadowing, the orange hell field larger and brighter at the upper right, the serpent coiling visibly at the bottom with patterned body. The signature FRANZ STVCK sits in the orange field. Two photos of one object read as slightly different pictures; the tighter crop's color is hotter. Caveat noted.

  1. Berlin c.1912 (3844x4960). The same temple shrine, DIE SVENDE on the

plinth, FRANZ STVCK upper right. The painting: the face frontal, the pale torso the reserve against a near-black blue ground. The serpent's blue-patterned body sweeps down the right side in one broad visible curve, dotted with light blue, yellow, and white. The serpent head at the left over her shoulder is green-blue with a red mouth and fangs. The hell patch at the upper right is green-yellow with horizontal hatching, cooler and more worked than the 1893 orange.

Tonal stats (painting areas only, frames excluded)

  • Pinakothek 1893: mean 71.6, std 55.1, darkfrac 0.513, palefrac 0.017
  • Berlin c.1912: mean 55.6, std 54.5, darkfrac 0.630, palefrac 0.044
  • Frye ca. 1908: mean 61.4, std 50.3, darkfrac 0.598, palefrac 0.007

The late versions are darker by the numbers, which is the wrong conclusion if read naively: the 1893 black ground is flat black, the late dark-green ground reads darker in grayscale because the hue shift fools the mean, while the late versions are chromatically louder (blue and turquoise dots, the green-yellow hell). The value key is free per version, as every lineage study has found; the version discriminator is chromatic, not tonal. The stats are reported for honesty, not for the thesis.

The hell patch, close up

Detail crops of the upper-right corner of all three paintings (the dossier calls the hell patch the single most useful feature for telling versions apart) confirm it on the pixels:

  • 1893: warm orange-yellow field with visible brushwork, small, tucked

against the molding.

  • Berlin: green-yellow with yellow-green horizontal hatching, cooler,

covering more area.

  • Frye: bright orange-yellow, larger than the 1893 field.

The signature lives inside the patch in all three: FRANZ / STUCK (1893) and FRANZ STVCK (late). The signature is a light event placed in the brightest field, the one place it can read without touching the figure.

What stays constant, what changes (Ritthaler's comparison, confirmed where

the three inspections overlap)

Constant across all eleven: the composition. A pale frontal face oval dissolving into a black hair mass; the nude torso emerging from darkness; the large dark-blue serpent coiled around body and neck, head resting on the woman's right shoulder and breast, woman and snake both meeting the viewer's gaze; the bright hell patch upper right; the signature in the patch; the architectural temple frame with the title incised on the plinth. The serpent's position is fixed from version 2 onward.

Changes, documented version to version:

  • The serpent's winding: double around the belly in version 1 only, single

in all ten later versions. The one compositional change Stuck ever made, and he made it between the first and second painting.

  • The face: maidenly (1891-93), an adult woman's features (about 1895),

"lasterhaft" (1908 onward). Confirmed on my three: 1893 shadowed and turned away, Frye frontal and lit, Berlin frontal.

  • The snake's pattern: light diamond pattern with light lines (1891-93);

reduced diamonds, highlight as a broad field of cross-hatching (about 1895); luminous blue and turquoise dots (1908 onward). Confirmed on the Berlin pixels.

  • The background: flat black without depth (1891 to about 1895); dark green

from the 1908 group on.

  • The hell patch: orange-yellow unstructured (1891/92); orange-yellow with

visible brushwork (1893); oval forms (about 1895); green-yellow area (1908 onward). Confirmed on all three inspections.

  • The snake head: nearly round (1891/92); flatter, exact, metallic gray

(1893); more stylized (about 1895); green and blue with a red mouth (after 1908). Confirmed on the Berlin pixels.

  • The frame inscription: DIE SUENDE with round U (Pinakothek; also the

Littmann frame per the 1931 photo); a unique V with two i-dots (Kuenstleraltar, lost); DIE SVENDE with the VE ligature (the whole late group: Palermo, Florio, Berlin, Seattle, Villa Stuck). The ligature is documented from 1898 on other frames, but on Suende frames it belongs to the late group. The inscription is a dating instrument.

  • The signature, Ritthaler's dating instrument: two-line from 1891; pointed

V in STVCK after 1890; connected letters after 1894; the lower loop of the S round to 1908, elongated from 1908; and for this subject Stuck signed FRANZ STVCK without the noble "von" even after his 1906 ennoblement, proved by the rubbed-out VON on the Seattle version. The signature is version control, drawn in paint.

  • Medium: oil on canvas for ten versions; the Seattle version in

water-soluble Syntonos on a water-soluble ground.

Why eleven

Demand. The German survey line (wilfried-hartleb.de) puts it directly: Stuck painted the motif repeatedly between 1891 and 1912 to meet the demand of international museums and collectors, Palermo and Seattle included. He treated each version as an independent work: he sold the photographic reproduction rights to Hanfstaengl separately for four of them (1893, 1899, 1903, 1909), which Hanfstaengl catalogued as "Die Suende" I through IV. The 24 October 1893 contract asks that the picture be photographed as large as possible, against royalties. The serial production confused art writers almost from the start: Stuck's friend Ostini once identified the Hanfstaengl version as the Pinakothek one, and even the Villa Stuck's 1997 catalogue mixes up the Kuenstleraltar version with the Palermo one. Ritthaler's 2009 study exists largely to sort this out.

The frames were part of the strategy. Hans Meissner wrote in 1899 that Stuck saw his handcrafted frames as part of a "tactical masterpiece" on which he built not only his works but his whole artistic life. The temple frame, designed to make the painting stand out in the overloaded exhibitions of the day, is itself a repeatable signature element of the series: columns, foliate molding, the title incised on the plinth, the roundels. The frame is version control too.

Re-render families

Two PIL families, single pass, schematics (crude by design; the finding is the structure, not the drawing).

Family A: the serial grammar. A1 is the species: four versions of ONE composition where only the documented variables move (face from soft to defined to harsh, snake pattern from diamond to dots, hell patch from orange to green-yellow, ground from black to dark green). A2 varies the composition itself (the failure: four different pictures are not a series). A3 repeats one version identically (the failure: an edition is not a series). A4 shows the serpent head alone as an emblem (the failure: the signifier without the composition is a logo). Finding: the repetition IS the work, and what makes it a series rather than an edition is that the variables move. A series with no variables is an edition; variables with no constant composition are four different pictures.

Family B: the shrine grammar. B1 is the species: the temple frame with columns, plinth, and DIE SVENDE incised on the front, the picture enshrined. B2 is the painting bare, edge to edge (the failure: half the composition is missing). B3 is a generic ornate oval frame with no title (the failure: decoration without the inscription is not the shrine). B4 is a thin modern frame (the failure: neutrality is a different argument). Finding: the frame is load-bearing. Remove the title from the plinth and the object stops being an altar and becomes a painting in a fancy frame.

Quality notes

What makes this work GOOD, the compositional decisions rather than the technique.

The discipline of the constant. Eleven paintings over twenty years and the composition never moves: the face oval at the same height, the torso the same pale mass, the serpent head at the same shoulder, the hell patch at the same corner, the signature inside the patch, the title on the plinth. This is the opposite of the Klinger glove (which changes scale and register) and the opposite of the Caprichos arc (which paces by value). Stuck's serial grammar is: hold everything, move only the declared variables. The quality is in what he refused to touch. A derivative that changes the composition between versions has eleven paintings, not a series.

The variables are chosen, not drifted. The hell patch is the best version discriminator because it is the one thing Stuck allowed to be contemporary: orange-yellow in 1893, green-yellow with hatching in 1912, tracking his palette's move from chiaroscuro to color. The face tracks the same move: maidenly, adult, depraved. The series is a record of the artist's hand changing while the composition refuses to. That is why it works as a series and not as repetition: each version is the same picture painted by a later Stuck.

The shrine as the second composition. The temple frame is not a container, it is the second half of the object. The title is incised on the plinth, not written on a label; the signature sits in the hell patch, not in a corner of the canvas. Everything that names the picture is built into the object. Hans Meissner's 1899 line (the handcrafted frames as part of a "tactical masterpiece") is the period witness: the frame was the marketing, and the marketing was the art. A derivative that puts this composition in a neutral frame has removed the veneration and kept only the illustration.

The double winding, once. The one compositional change Stuck ever made to the motif (serpent wound twice around the belly in version 1, once thereafter) happened between the first and second painting and never again. He found the form in one move and then spent twenty years not moving it. The restraint is the point of view.

What would make a derivative piece fail:

  • Changing the composition between versions (then it is a set, not a series).
  • Changing nothing between versions (then it is an edition, not a series).
  • Varying the variables randomly instead of along one axis of the artist's

changing hand (then it is a slot machine).

  • The serpent head alone as an emblem (then it is a logo for the sin, not

the sin).

  • The painting without the shrine frame (then the veneration is gone and

only the illustration remains).

  • The title on a wall label instead of incised in the plinth (then the

naming is curation, not architecture).

Avoid-list additions

  • The "marble altar" as fact (unverified; the altar construction's material

is undocumented, and the altar version is lost).

  • Calling the eleven versions copies or reproductions of the 1893 original

(Stuck treated each as an independent work and sold the reproduction rights separately; the copies are the Hanfstaengl photographs).

  • The serpent wound twice as the motif's form (version 1 only; every later

version winds once).

  • DIE SUENDE vs DIE SVENDE as interchangeable (the spellings date the

frames: round U early, VE ligature late, V-with-two-i-dots unique to the lost altar version).

  • The Chicago 1893 gold medal as awarded for this picture (it was Stuck's

medal at the exposition; the picture's own medal was Vienna 1899).

  • The police-ordered destruction of reproductions (that is the Kuss der

Sphinx, not the Suende).

  • "Hitler's favorite painter" framing for the Berlin version (the

documented fact is the Sonderauftrag Linz provenance and the possible Prinzregentenplatz apartment hanging; no more).

  • Reading the late versions as darker (the grayscale mean drops, but the

discriminator is chromatic: the move is from black ground to dark green, from orange hell to green-yellow, from diamond pattern to blue dots).

Honest gaps

  • No original handled; all three inspections are reproductions (the Berlin

file a visitor photograph, the Frye files museum photos via a CDN cache, the Pinakothek the Commons shrine photo). Color balance unverified on all three; the two Frye photos of the same object read as different color temperatures.

  • Eight of the eleven uninspected visually (four lost, four located but not

seen: Passau, Palermo, Florio, Villa Stuck).

  • The dossier's conflicts carried: eleven vs twelve vs fourteen; the

Gutmann and Flersheim c.1897 versions unreconciled; Villa Stuck dating (museum "before 1906" vs Ritthaler "after 1908"); Pinakothek acquisition 1893 vs end of 1895; dimension discrepancies; the 2014 Sotheby's New York lot unmatched to any version; the Kuenstleraltar material unknown; the Passau version's current location unknown.

  • The re-render schematics are single-pass and crude; the A1 snake draws

dots and diamonds as glyphs, not as Stuck's handling.
