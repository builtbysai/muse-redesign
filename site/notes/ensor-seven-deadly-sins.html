Deep serial study, 2026-10-06. Hop-only session, queue exhausted. Named as the first next doorway by the 2026-10-06 Adam and Eve study ("the Seven Deadly Sins etchings 1902-1904"), the quality-first choice: the only serial study in the lineage where the series was assembled across sixteen years from two different hands of the same artist, and where the hand-coloring is documented as a second act of authorship that can redirect the satire.

Eight etchings: seven plates, one per sin, plus a frontispiece. La Luxure (Lust) is 1888 (the majority reading; one Sotheby's listing says 1889, unresolved). La Paresse (Sloth) is 1902. La Colere, L'Orgeuil (MoMA's spelling, an older French form), L'Avarice, La Gourmandise, and L'Envie are 1904, as is the frontispiece, "Les Peches capitaux domines par la mort" (The Deadly Sins Dominated by Death). Published as an album in 1904, self-published by Ensor in Ostend with a preface by his friend and first biographer Eugene Demolder. The printer is possibly Jean-Baptiste Van Campenhout of Brussels (MoMA's "possibly"; keep it). Edition unknown, approximately 50-100 impressions, of which about 20 hand-colored. The hand-colored sets sold at publication for 50 francs against 20 for the uncolored sets (Christie's, citing Tricot 2010). Catalogue numbers: Delteil/Croquez/Taevernier 59 (Lust), 119 (Sloth), 121-126 (the rest), Elesh 59, 124, 126-131. The middle of the mapping (T.122-125 to Pride/Avarice/Gluttony/Envy) rests on one auction listing's parallel ordering and is marked inferred in the dossier. Plates are small, about 9.5-10 x 13.5-15 cm, on much larger sheets. There are preliminary studies in colored chalk for all eight plates, in a private collection, mirror images of the prints except the Lust study, which was probably made after the 1888 etching.

A research dossier on the series' biography, publishing, criticism, holdings, and conflicts was delegated in-session (text-only, 2026-10-06, zero em dashes; in the evidence dir). Its key findings are folded in below. All visual inspections, the tonal measurements, the six detail crops, and both re-render families are mine.

What the series is

The sin is never a portrait. Every plate stages the sinner as a small theater with a chorus: witnesses, tormentors, skeletons, and devils pressing in on the guilty figure. The series is social. The sin happens in front of an audience, and the audience is the composition.

The two time signatures show in the line. Lust (1888) is airy and sparse, fine unhurried etching with pale hand-coloring, from the same burst as the prints that made Ensor notorious. The 1902-1904 campaign is denser, faster, more crowded: the line hurries, the crosshatch thickens, the plates fill to the edges. You can see the fourteen years between Lust and Sloth in the density of the mark, not in any change of subject.

The hand-coloring is a second act of authorship, not decoration. The washes are laid free, ignoring the etched contours: the blue sky wash in the frontispiece floats across figure boundaries, the yellow field in Avarice pools behind the miser regardless of the drawing. Opaque white gouache is used as added light, the clock face in Paresse picked out in white paint over the etching (confirmed in the detail crop). And the coloring can redirect the satire itself: the Burlington Magazine, reviewing "Ensor's States of Imagination," reports that in at least one impression Ensor hand-colored Pride so the central figure becomes a judge, "a seemingly small intervention, but one that made a generalised satire a focused jab." The color pass is allowed to change what the line drawing means. Ensor himself said he particularly preferred the hand-colored prints (MSK Gent records).

The smallness is the point. Each plate is about the size of a postcard, a moral theater you hold close. The whole grotesque world, crowd, skeletons, devils, and all, fits in a palm-sized stage.

The eight plates, visually inspected

All eight inspected at source resolution: four from the Museum Plantin-Moretus holdings on Wikimedia Commons (frontispiece, Luxure, Envie, Orgueil, ~3800-4600px wide) and four from the BnF Gallica digitizations (Paresse, Colere, Avarice, Gourmandise, ~3900-4100px wide). Note: the BnF scans are catalogued as second states ("2eme etat"). Three of the eight impressions inspected are uncolored (Gourmandise, Envie), the rest hand-colored to varying degrees.

Frontispiece: Les Peches capitaux domines par la mort, 1904. The winged skeleton rises over a row of grotesque sinner faces, each sin reduced to its emblem: Sloth the somnolent man with the snail, Envy with a bloody knife, Gluttony relishing a sausage, Lust fondling a woman who turns on him in anger, Avarice clutching a bag of gold, Pride a soldier in a bearskin hat (Christie's press release). The composition is a frieze: the death figure centered above, the sins as a head row below. Hand-colored in pale washes, blue sky, red, green, yellow on the figures. Signed "James Ensor 1904" in pencil; the Plantin-Moretus impression carries the KBR blindstamp.

La Luxure, 1888. The outlier. A bedroom scene, airy and spare: a lecherous bald man with scythe-like wings reclines with a nude woman in a curtained bed, a small skeleton floating upper left, red drapery above, a yellow column at right. The line is the finest in the series, the plate the palest measured (mean 173.1, palefrac 0.175). It belongs to 1888, not to the 1904 campaign, and it shows.

La Paresse, 1902. Two sleepers buried in a white bed, grotesque heads on the pillow, while through the window a whole tiny world toils: workers in a green field, factory chimneys, a yellow sun. A skeleton removes the hands of a clock at upper right (the High Museum description confirms the wit). Red curtains left, blue drapery center, the bedclothes a mass of white line. The sin is staged as a contrast: the busy world outside against the inert bed. The white gouache on the clock face is the clearest example of added light in the series.

La Colere, 1904. A brawl in full motion: three figures fighting, one in a top hat with arm raised, one seized by the hair, one in a green dress, a small dog between them, and above it all a flying green dragon. The sky is diagonal hatching, the only plate where the ground itself expresses the sin. Anger is the one sin Ensor gives no interior and no stillness; everything leans and lunges.

L'Orgueil, 1904. The darkest plate measured (mean 121.7, darkfrac 0.174). A stiff frontal bourgeois in a black suit, medals and decorations on his shirtfront tinted yellow by the hand-coloring, surrounded and pressed by bowing sycophantic grotesque heads. A green skeleton sprawls across the top of the plate; a donkey stands at upper right. The pride is in the rigidity: the figure is a column, the suit a mass of dense crosshatch, and the flatterers do all the moving. Note: the frontispiece reduces Pride to a soldier in a bearskin hat, a different emblem from this standalone plate's medal-wearing bourgeois.

L'Avarice, 1904. The most violent plate. An old miser in a nightcap clutches coins scattered across a table while his tormentors close in: a figure with a raised pickaxe behind him, a man with a knife at right, a green devil at left. A window shows a night sky with a crescent moon, deep blue against the strong yellow wash of the right background. The gold is the brightest thing in the plate, and it is what everyone is reaching for.

La Gourmandise, 1904. The BnF impression is uncolored, line only, and reads as a different species: a fat glutton at a table piled with food (fish, bird, sausage), a pig looming at upper right, a skeleton serving at right, a thin figure watching at left, and an inset scene of revelers at upper left. Without the wash it is the sparsest of the 1904 plates (mean 156.8), all appetite and no atmosphere.

L'Envie, 1904. The palest of the 1904 plates (palefrac 0.231), also uncolored in the Plantin impression. A crowd of grotesque faces, emaciated and ugly, hems in a central radiant woman with a baby, probably at a christening (High Museum; the mother in green in colored impressions). The envied figure is the reserve: the brightest, least-worked passage in the plate, ringed by dark hatched enviers. The sin is staged as a difference in light.

Technique

Pure etching, in every source found: no drypoint or aquatint is documented for these eight plates (the dossier carries the contrast with "Hop-Frog's Revenge," 1898, which MoMA catalogues as etching and drypoint). Ensor admitted he had "not mastered the profession of etcher at all" (his letter to Valere Gille) and routinely used Brussels printers, first Leon Evely, later the Van Campenhouts. The 1904 plates are bitten with a hurried, accumulating line: crosshatch for mass (the Orgueil suit), loose wavy contours for the grotesques, fine parallel hatching for skies (Colere's diagonal agitation).

The hand-coloring is the second instrument: watercolor and gouache washes plus colored pencil, applied after printing, free of the contours, sometimes extending beyond the image (the Mira Jacob frontispiece had paper strips attached at left and right and was colored so heavily it became "a small, jewel-like painting," Christie's). About 20 of the roughly 100 impressions were colored. Color is placed, never a global tint: a blue wash for one sky, a yellow field for one background, red dabs on faces, white gouache for added light.

The serial grammar

This is the fourth serial grammar in the lineage run, and the closest to the Disparates: a collection, not a sequence. There is no value arc across the eight plates. The tonal stats confirm the value key free per composition: Orgueil 121.7/0.174 darkest, Avarice 125.6, Colere 128.4, Paresse 137.6, frontispiece 148.2, Gourmandise 156.8, Envie 158.0, Luxure 173.1 palest. The densest crowd plates are the darkest, the airiest the palest, but no published order descends or ascends; the 1888 plate simply sits apart, visibly older in the hand.

What binds the series is the staging, not the tone: the sinner and the chorus, the small horizontal stage, the grotesque vocabulary recycled from 1886-1889. The frontispiece is the index, reducing each sin to its emblem, and the seven plates are the expansions. The Flemish Art Collection's assessment stands: the prints rework the grotesque and at times funny iconography developed since 1886-1889, and they are among the best of Ensor's late graphic work.

Quality notes

What makes these plates good, specifically:

  1. The sinner is never alone. Every sin is a social situation with

an audience, and the audience does half the moral work. Avarice is not the miser, it is the miser plus the knives. Envy is not the couple, it is the couple plus the ring of staring faces. A derivative that draws the sinner alone gets an emblem, not a sin.

  1. The chorus is at uniform scale and pressed close. The witness

faces crowd the sinner at roughly one size, overlapping, cropped by nothing, filling the small plate edge to edge. The rhythm is overlap and pressure, not arrangement. Space the witnesses evenly and the plate becomes an inventory.

  1. The color is a second author. The washes are placed free of the

line, and in the best impressions the color pass changes the picture's meaning (Pride's soldier becoming a judge). A derivative that colors inside the lines gets a coloring book. A derivative that washes the whole sheet gets a tint. The discipline is: color placed, color free, color allowed to mean.

  1. The small stage. Ten by fourteen centimeters forces the whole

moral world into one glance. Nothing is background; the plate is all foreground. Enlarge the format and the pressure leaks out.

  1. The 1888 plate is the control. Lust shows what the series would

be without the 1904 density: airy, legible, thinner. The crowding is not decoration, it is the late hand, and the series needs it.

What would make a derivative piece fail: the sinner alone (emblem), the witnesses in an even grid (inventory), the crowd with no sinner (wallpaper), color inside the lines (coloring book), color as shading (modeling), color everywhere (tint), the large format (pressure lost), the fourteen-year gap smoothed over (the series pretending to be one campaign).

What is overdone (avoid-list additions)

  • The sinner alone as the sin (emblem reading). The even witness

grid (inventory reading). The crowd with no sinner (wallpaper).

  • Color inside the etched lines (the coloring-book failure).

Color as shading or modeling (the wash must never model form). The global tint (color placed nowhere).

  • Dating the whole series 1902-1904 without the 1888 Lust (the

sixteen-year span is the fact; Christie's Mira Jacob release and the Flemish Art Collection both give 1888-1904).

  • The Sotheby's 1889 Lust date stated as fact (one listing against

the majority 1888; unresolved).

  • Van Campenhout as the certain printer (MoMA says "possibly" on

every record; keep the hedge).

  • The frontispiece's soldier-in-bearskin Pride presented as the

standalone plate's composition (the standalone Orgueil is the medal-wearing bourgeois; the vignette is a different emblem).

  • Tricot or Exsteens catalogue numbers for these plates stated as

found (not found in this pass; the middle Taevernier/Elesh mapping is inferred, marked as such).

  • "Precursor of Surrealism" as the frame for this series (the

Mira Jacob sale's marketing line; the plates are a summation of the 1886-1889 grotesque theater, not a Surrealist anticipation).

Honest gaps

No original handled; all eight from web reproductions (Plantin-Moretus and BnF scans), color balance unverified against the impressions. The BnF impressions are second states; state counts for Sloth, Anger, Pride, Gluttony, and Envy were not found (Lust II final and Avarice II/II documented). Whether any plate involves drypoint or aquatint beyond etching is unverified (literature says etching throughout, but Taevernier's full plate descriptions were not accessible). The T.122-125 and E.126-130 mappings are inferred from one auction listing's parallel ordering. KMSKA, Rijksmuseum, and Mu.ZEE holdings not verified. No 1904 press reviews found beyond Verhaeren's reported enthusiasm and Demolder's preface (whose full text was not retrieved). Taevernier 120 unidentified. The Demolder-suggestion story is the Flemish Art Collection's own hedge ("it is said"). Schematics single-pass, crude glyphs. Plate-area tonal stats use estimated inset crops (platemark auto-detection failed on the faint marks); the ordering of the stats is robust, the absolute numbers approximate.
